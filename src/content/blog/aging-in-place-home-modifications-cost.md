The most impactful aging-in-place modifications, grab bars, non-slip flooring, and improved lighting, cost **$150 to $2,500** and can be completed in a single weekend. If you or a family member needs more significant changes, a roll-in shower conversion runs **$2,000 to $15,000**, a stair lift costs **$3,000 to $10,000 installed**, and a wheelchair ramp ranges from **$1,200 to $8,000** depending on length and material. The full cost to make a typical two-story home fully accessible ranges from **$15,000 to $80,000**, though most families complete a phased approach and spend far less up front. Several federal programs, including a USDA grant of up to $10,000, a 30% IRS medical deduction, and VA housing grants reaching $117,014, can dramatically reduce what comes out of pocket.

If you are reading this because a parent just had a fall, or because you are starting to think about your own future in the home you love, this guide will walk you through the realistic costs, the smartest starting points, and every dollar of assistance available to you.

## Why Aging in Place Is Worth Planning Now

More than 77 percent of adults age 50 and older say they want to remain in their current home as they age, according to AARP. Yet most American homes were designed with no attention to accessibility. Doorways are too narrow for wheelchairs. Bathrooms have slick tile floors and tubs that require stepping over a high ledge. Light switches are at heights that become harder to reach with limited mobility.

The good news is that most of these problems are solvable, often for less than you might expect, and the modifications that matter most tend to be the least glamorous: a grab bar installed next to the toilet can prevent a fall that would cost $35,000 in an emergency room and rehab facility.

According to the [National Institute on Aging](https://www.nia.nih.gov/health/falls-and-falls-prevention/falls-and-older-adults), falls are the leading cause of injury-related death among adults 65 and older. One in four older adults falls each year. The modifications in this guide are not cosmetic upgrades. They are injury prevention.

## Start Here: The Free Safety Assessment

Before you spend a dollar, request a free home safety assessment. Two sources offer them at no cost:

**Area Agency on Aging (AAA):** Your local AAA office, part of the national Eldercare Locator network funded by the Administration for Community Living, can send a trained specialist to walk through your home and identify hazards. Call 1-800-677-1116 or visit [eldercare.acl.gov](https://eldercare.acl.gov) to find your local agency.

**Occupational Therapist (OT) Home Visit:** Many home health agencies and hospital discharge programs include a home OT visit covered by Medicare Part B when ordered by a physician. An OT is trained specifically to evaluate how a person moves through their home and will give you a prioritized list of modifications.

Both of these assessments cost you nothing and will help you spend any modification budget where it matters most.

## The High-Impact, Low-Cost Changes to Make First

These modifications are the foundation of a safe home for any age. Do them first, regardless of your overall budget.

### Grab Bars

**Cost to install professionally: $150 to $500 per bar**
**DIY cost (hardware only): $25 to $120 per bar**

A properly installed grab bar next to the toilet and inside the shower is one of the highest-value modifications you can make. The critical word is "properly." A grab bar must be anchored into wall studs or with specialized toggle anchors rated for at least 250 pounds of force. A bar that pulls out of drywall when someone grabs it is worse than no bar at all.

Professional installation by a handyman or contractor takes about 30 to 45 minutes per bar. If you are handy and willing to locate studs, this is a reasonable DIY project. [ADA guidelines](https://www.ada.gov/topics/title-ii-requirements/) specify 33 to 36 inches above the floor for horizontal bars beside toilets.

### Non-Slip Flooring and Rugs

**Cost: $0 to $800**

Remove all loose throw rugs in bathrooms and hallways. They are a trip hazard. For smooth tile or hardwood floors in wet areas, add non-slip adhesive strips (under $20) or apply a non-slip coating. In the shower, a textured mat rated for wet use costs $15 to $50. For a more permanent solution, tile with a natural texture or a higher coefficient of friction (0.6 or above, as rated by the American National Standards Institute) costs similar to standard tile.

### Improved Lighting

**Cost: $50 to $600**

Poor lighting causes falls. Replacing a standard light switch with a motion-sensing nightlight in hallways and bathrooms costs $20 to $40 per switch. Adding under-cabinet lighting in the kitchen and along stair treads costs $50 to $200 depending on the length of the run. For an electrician to install a new overhead fixture in a dim hallway, budget $150 to $400 including parts and labor.

### Lever Door Handles

**Cost: $20 to $80 per handle, DIY**

Round doorknobs require grip strength that diminishes with age and arthritis. Lever-style handles can be operated with a fist or elbow. Replacing all interior door handles is a quick DIY project, and lever handles cost $20 to $80 each at any hardware store.

## Bathroom Modifications: The Highest-Priority Room

The bathroom is where most home falls occur. After addressing grab bars and flooring, consider these upgrades:

### Roll-In or Walk-In Shower Conversion

**Cost: $2,000 to $15,000**

A standard tub-shower combo requires stepping over a ledge that can be 12 to 18 inches high. A zero-threshold (roll-in) shower eliminates that barrier entirely.

The cost varies widely depending on the size of your existing bathroom, whether the floor requires re-sloping for proper drainage, and the materials you choose:

| Conversion Type | Typical Cost Range |
|---|---|
| Tub to basic walk-in shower (fiberglass) | $2,000 to $5,000 |
| Tub to tile roll-in shower | $5,000 to $12,000 |
| Full bathroom gut and rebuild for accessibility | $10,000 to $25,000 |
| Addition of separate accessible bathroom | $20,000 to $50,000 |

If a full conversion is not in the budget now, a handheld showerhead ($30 to $150) and a fold-down teak shower seat ($80 to $250) make an existing shower substantially safer.

### Walk-In Tub

**Cost: $3,000 to $20,000 installed**

Walk-in tubs have a door in the side so the bather steps in, closes the door, and then fills the tub. They eliminate the high step-over entirely. Costs vary based on features: a basic model runs $3,000 to $5,000 with installation, while jetted or hydrotherapy models with heated seats and chromotherapy lighting run $10,000 to $20,000.

One consideration: you must enter the tub before filling it and wait for it to drain completely before opening the door to exit. Some people find this inconvenient. A walk-in shower is often more practical for day-to-day use.

### Comfort-Height Toilet

**Cost: $200 to $600 installed**

Standard toilets sit 15 inches from floor to seat. A "comfort height" or ADA-compliant toilet sits 17 to 19 inches high, which makes it far easier to sit and stand. Combined with a grab bar, this is one of the most effective modifications for people with knee or hip problems.

## Stair and Mobility Modifications

### Stair Lift

**Cost: $3,000 to $10,000 for straight stairs**
**Cost: $10,000 to $30,000 for curved stairs**

A stair lift is a motorized chair that glides along a rail mounted to the stairs, allowing a person to ride up and down without climbing. Straight staircase lifts are the most affordable and widely available. Curved staircases require a custom rail and cost significantly more.

Professional installation is required. The lift itself plus installation runs $3,000 to $5,000 for a straightforward straight staircase. Rental options are also available through some medical equipment suppliers at $150 to $200 per month, which makes sense if the need is temporary.

Medicare does not cover stair lifts unless they are deemed medically necessary by a physician and prescribed as durable medical equipment, which is uncommon. Medicaid coverage varies by state.

### Wheelchair Ramp

**Cost: $1,200 to $8,000**

A ramp to the front door or into the garage allows a wheelchair or walker user to enter and exit the home safely. The [ADA standard slope](https://www.ada.gov/law-and-regs/design-standards/2010-stds/) is 1 inch of rise for every 12 inches of run: a front door that is 30 inches above grade level requires a ramp at least 30 feet long. Costs depend on the height of the entrance, the material (wood, aluminum, or concrete), and whether railings are required.

| Ramp Type | Typical Cost |
|---|---|
| Portable aluminum (12 to 30 inches of rise) | $300 to $1,000 |
| Modular wood ramp (professional installation) | $1,200 to $3,500 |
| Permanent concrete ramp | $3,000 to $8,000+ |

### Home Elevator

**Cost: $20,000 to $50,000 installed**

A residential elevator is the most expensive option for multi-story access but also the most seamless. Pneumatic vacuum elevators, which require no shaft construction, run $15,000 to $30,000. Traditional cable or hydraulic elevators with a full shaft construction cost $30,000 to $50,000 or more. Some state programs offer loans or grants for elevator installation when medically necessary.

## Kitchen and Living Space Modifications

### Lowered Countertops and Cooktops

**Cost: $1,500 to $6,000**

Standard countertop height is 36 inches. For a person using a wheelchair, counters at 28 to 34 inches allow full access. This requires rebuilding cabinet sections and may involve moving plumbing for a sink. A partial modification, lowering just one section of counter for food prep, costs $1,500 to $3,000.

### Doorway Widening

**Cost: $700 to $2,500 per doorway**

A standard interior doorway is 24 to 30 inches wide. A wheelchair requires at least 32 inches of clear space, with 36 inches preferred. Widening a doorway requires a licensed contractor and may involve moving electrical wiring if the wall contains an outlet or switch. Budget $700 to $1,500 per doorway for a non-load-bearing wall or $1,500 to $2,500 for a load-bearing wall.

### Smart Home Technology

**Cost: $200 to $3,000**

Voice-controlled smart home devices let people control lights, thermostats, door locks, and appliances without physical contact or movement. A starter setup including a smart speaker, smart bulbs for key rooms, and a smart thermostat runs $200 to $400. A more comprehensive system with a video doorbell, smart door lock, and connected smoke and carbon monoxide detectors runs $800 to $3,000. These systems also provide remote monitoring for family members who live elsewhere.

## How to Pay for Aging-in-Place Modifications

### Federal Tax Deduction (IRS)

If modifications are made for a diagnosed medical condition and prescribed by a physician, you may be able to deduct the cost as a medical expense on your federal taxes. You can deduct the amount of medical expenses that exceeds 7.5 percent of your adjusted gross income (AGI). Keep documentation: a letter from a physician describing the medical necessity and all contractor invoices. See [IRS Publication 502](https://www.irs.gov/pub/irs-pdf/p502.pdf) for the full list of qualifying medical expenses.

**Note:** General home modifications that also increase property value, such as a ramp that adds to curb appeal, may only be partially deductible. The IRS allows the deduction only for the portion of the cost that exceeds the added property value.

### USDA Section 504 Home Repair Program

The [USDA Section 504 program](https://www.rd.usda.gov/programs-services/single-family-housing-programs/single-family-housing-repair-loans-grants) provides grants of up to $10,000 for very low-income homeowners age 62 or older to remove health and safety hazards. Loans of up to $40,000 are available for lower-income households of any age. This program is limited to rural areas, but "rural" by USDA definition includes many small towns and suburbs. Check eligibility at [rd.usda.gov](https://www.rd.usda.gov).

### VA Specially Adapted Housing Grants

Veterans with service-connected disabilities may qualify for two VA grant programs:

- **Specially Adapted Housing (SAH) Grant:** Up to $117,014 in 2026 for veterans with severe service-connected disabilities affecting mobility.
- **Special Housing Adaptation (SHA) Grant:** Up to $23,444 for veterans with less severe but qualifying disabilities.

Contact the [VA Housing Assistance](https://www.va.gov/housing-assistance/disability-housing-grants/) program or call 1-800-827-1000.

### Medicaid Home and Community-Based Services (HCBS)

For homeowners who qualify for Medicaid, the Home and Community-Based Services waiver program may cover home modifications that allow you to remain at home instead of entering a nursing facility. Coverage varies significantly by state. Contact your state Medicaid office or local Area Agency on Aging to find out what is covered in your state.

### State and Local Programs

Many states offer their own home modification grant or loan programs, particularly for seniors and people with disabilities. Examples include:

- **Pennsylvania:** The Weatherization Assistance Program and the LIHEAP Home Energy Efficiency Program both fund some accessibility modifications.
- **California:** The HCD CalHome Program offers low-interest loans for accessibility modifications.
- **New York:** The Affordable Home Ownership Development Program includes accessibility upgrades.

Call your local Area Agency on Aging (1-800-677-1116) to find programs specific to your state and county.

### Nonprofit and Charitable Sources

Several national nonprofits help low-income homeowners with accessibility modifications:

- **Rebuilding Together:** A national network of affiliates that provides free home repairs and modifications. Visit [rebuildingtogether.org](https://www.rebuildingtogether.org) to find your local chapter.
- **Habitat for Humanity:** Some local chapters offer home repair and modification programs for existing homeowners.
- **Easter Seals:** Provides a range of services for people with disabilities, including home modification assistance in some regions.

## DIY vs. Professional: What to Hire Out

| Modification | DIY-Friendly? | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Grab bars | Yes, with care | Must hit studs or use rated anchors. Wrong installation is dangerous. |
| Non-slip strips and mats | Yes | No tools needed. |
| Lever door handles | Yes | Simple swap, no electrical or plumbing involved. |
| Motion-sensing light switches | Yes, with caution | Turn off the breaker. If wiring is unfamiliar, hire an electrician. |
| Walk-in shower conversion | No | Involves plumbing, waterproofing, and floor re-sloping. Hire a licensed contractor. |
| Stair lift | No | Requires professional installation for safety certification and warranty. |
| Doorway widening | No | May involve structural work and electrical. Licensed contractor required. |
| Wheelchair ramp (modular) | Possibly | Modular aluminum kits are designed for homeowner assembly but check local codes. |
| Wheelchair ramp (permanent) | No | Concrete or built-in-place wood requires permits and a licensed contractor. |

## Finding a Qualified Contractor

Not all contractors have experience with accessibility modifications. Ask any contractor you interview:

1. Have you installed grab bars and roll-in showers before? Can you show me photos of past projects?
2. Are you familiar with ADA guidelines and universal design principles?
3. Do you work with occupational therapists on home modification projects?

The [National Association of Home Builders (NAHB)](https://www.nahb.org) offers a Certified Aging-in-Place Specialist (CAPS) designation for contractors who have completed specialized training. Search for CAPS-certified contractors at [nahb.org](https://www.nahb.org/find-a-member) to find someone with verified expertise.

Always get at least three written bids. Verify the contractor holds a current license in your state, carries liability insurance, and pulls the required permits for structural or plumbing work.

## A Phased Approach: Where to Start If Budget Is Limited

If you cannot afford a comprehensive renovation now, prioritize in this order:

**Phase 1 (Under $1,000):** Grab bars in bathroom and near toilet, non-slip mats, lever handles, improved lighting, remove all throw rugs.

**Phase 2 ($1,000 to $5,000):** Walk-in shower conversion (fiberglass), comfort-height toilet, handheld showerhead, fold-down shower seat, motion-sensor lighting throughout.

**Phase 3 ($5,000 to $20,000):** Stair lift or ramp, widened doorways, smart home integration, tile roll-in shower.

**Phase 4 ($20,000+):** Full bathroom rebuild, kitchen modifications, home elevator, full ADA compliance throughout the home.

Many families complete Phases 1 and 2 and find they cover the vast majority of daily needs. Start there, reassess, and add more only as the situation requires.
