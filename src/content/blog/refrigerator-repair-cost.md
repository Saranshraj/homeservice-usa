When your refrigerator stops working, every hour counts. The good news is that most common refrigerator repairs cost **$150 to $500**, with the national average landing around **$250 to $350**. Simple fixes like a broken door gasket or a clogged drain can run as low as $75. Major repairs like compressor replacement push past $1,000 and sometimes make replacement the smarter call. This guide breaks down what you'll pay for every major repair, how to find a reliable technician, and the honest answer to "should I fix it or buy a new one?"

---

## What You'll Pay: Refrigerator Repair Cost by Type

Labor is usually $75 to $150 per hour, plus parts. Most repairs take one to two hours once the technician has the parts in hand. Many companies also charge a **diagnostic or service call fee of $50 to $100**, which is often applied toward the repair if you go ahead with the work.

| Repair Type | Typical Cost Range |
|---|---|
| Service call / diagnostic fee | $50 to $100 |
| Door gasket (seal) replacement | $75 to $200 |
| Thermostat or temperature control | $100 to $300 |
| Defrost heater or defrost system | $100 to $250 |
| Evaporator or condenser fan motor | $100 to $300 |
| Ice maker repair or replacement | $100 to $300 |
| Water dispenser repair | $100 to $250 |
| Control board replacement | $200 to $600 |
| Refrigerant recharge (Freon/R-134a) | $200 to $400 |
| Compressor replacement | $300 to $1,500 |
| Full sealed system repair | $500 to $1,500+ |

Prices vary meaningfully by region. Expect to pay on the higher end in the Northeast, California, and the Pacific Northwest. Rural areas in the South and Midwest tend to come in 15 to 25 percent cheaper on labor, but you may wait longer for parts.

## The Most Common Refrigerator Problems (and What They Cost)

### Refrigerator Not Cooling or Not Cold Enough

This is the call that sends people into a panic, and understandably so. The cause could be something inexpensive or something that tips the scales toward replacement.

**Dirty condenser coils** are the most overlooked culprit. If coils on the back or bottom haven't been cleaned in years, the fridge works overtime and eventually loses cooling ability. A technician can clean them for $75 to $150, or you can do it yourself with a coil cleaning brush (about $15 at any hardware store) and a vacuum.

**A failed evaporator fan motor** prevents cold air from circulating through the freezer and refrigerator compartments. Parts and labor together run $100 to $300.

**Low refrigerant** is less common in modern sealed systems but does happen if there's a slow leak. A recharge plus finding and fixing the leak costs $200 to $400. The EPA requires licensed technicians to handle refrigerants, so this isn't a DIY job.

**Compressor failure** is the expensive one. The compressor is the heart of the cooling system. Parts alone can run $200 to $700; add $200 to $400 in labor and you're looking at $400 to $1,100 or more. At that price point, it's worth pricing out a new refrigerator before committing.

### Refrigerator Not Making Ice

Ice maker problems are extremely common, especially after three to five years of heavy use. A frozen water line is sometimes the fix, costing under $100. Replacing the whole ice maker module runs $100 to $300. If the water inlet valve that feeds the ice maker has failed, expect $100 to $250 for parts and labor.

### Water Dispenser Not Working

Usually a failed water inlet valve or a frozen line in the door. Repairs typically run $100 to $250. In some Samsung and LG models, the dispenser control board can fail as well, pushing the cost to $300 to $500.

### Refrigerator Running Constantly

A refrigerator that never shuts off drives up your electricity bill and usually signals a dirty condenser, a failing thermostat, or a worn door gasket that's letting cold air escape. Door gasket replacement costs $75 to $200 depending on the brand and how hard the gasket is to source. Thermostat replacement runs $100 to $300.

### Water Pooling Under the Refrigerator

Most of the time, a clogged defrost drain is the culprit. Cold water that can't drain properly pools under the crisper drawers or leaks onto your kitchen floor. Clearing the drain is one of the cheaper repairs: $75 to $150 and sometimes a DIY fix if you're comfortable pulling out the refrigerator and removing a panel.

## Should You Repair It or Replace It?

This is the question that matters most, and there's a widely used rule of thumb in the appliance industry: **the 50% rule**. If the estimated repair cost equals 50% or more of the cost of a comparable new refrigerator, replace it.

Here's how that plays out in practice:

| Refrigerator Age | Typical Repair Worth Considering |
|---|---|
| Under 5 years | Up to $600 to $700 in most cases |
| 5 to 10 years | Up to $400 to $500 |
| 10 to 15 years | Up to $200 to $300 |
| Over 15 years | Usually replace unless it's a very minor fix |

Average new refrigerator prices in 2026:
- Basic top-freezer model: $700 to $1,200
- French door, 25 to 30 cu. ft.: $1,200 to $2,500
- Counter-depth or built-in style: $2,000 to $5,000+

A ten-year-old refrigerator that needs a $900 compressor replacement is almost always better replaced. A three-year-old refrigerator with a $150 fan motor issue is almost always worth fixing.

### Watch for These Combinations

Beyond cost alone, consider replacing if:
- The refrigerator has needed more than one significant repair in the past two years
- The unit uses R-12 refrigerant (pre-1994 models), which is extremely expensive and hard to source
- Energy bills have crept up noticeably, since older refrigerators can use 2 to 3 times more electricity than newer ENERGY STAR models
- The refrigerator is a side-by-side built around 2005 to 2012 from a brand known for sealed-system problems (some Samsung and LG units from that era have well-documented compressor failure rates)

The [ENERGY STAR savings calculator](https://www.energystar.gov/products/appliances/refrigerators) at energystar.gov can show you exactly how much you'd save annually by switching to a certified model.

## How Refrigerator Brand Affects Repair Costs

Not all brands cost the same to fix. Part availability, complexity, and known reliability issues vary considerably.

**Whirlpool and Maytag** (same parent company): Parts are widely available, often cheaper than competitors, and most appliance technicians know them well. Generally easier and less expensive to repair.

**GE and GE Profile**: Solid parts availability, moderate repair costs. The older top-mount models are especially durable and cheap to fix.

**LG**: Has had well-publicized compressor failures in French door models from roughly 2014 to 2019. LG reached a class action settlement on some of these units. Before paying for a compressor repair on an older LG, check whether your model is covered. Visit LG's website or the CPSC recall database at [cpsc.gov](https://www.cpsc.gov) to check.

**Samsung**: Similarly, some Samsung French door and four-door models from that era experienced ice maker and cooling issues. Parts can be expensive and some repairs require Samsung-certified technicians to maintain the warranty.

**Sub-Zero, Thermador, Viking**: Premium brands typically cost more to repair, with parts sometimes running 3 to 5 times the cost of standard residential brands. Budget $400 to $1,500+ for most repairs. The upside is these units are built to last 20 to 30 years.

## How to Find a Trustworthy Appliance Repair Technician

A refrigerator is one of the appliances most targeted by scam repair services, especially services that advertise very low or free diagnostic fees and then quote inflated repair costs once they have the unit disassembled.

**Start with manufacturer-certified technicians.** Search your refrigerator brand's website for authorized service providers. These technicians receive factory training and often have faster access to genuine parts.

**Check for certification.** Look for technicians certified by the [Professional Service Association](https://www.psaworld.com) or the United Appliance Servicers Association. Certification is not required in most states, but it signals real training.

**Get a written estimate before any work begins.** A legitimate technician will give you a written repair estimate including parts and labor after diagnosing the problem. Walk away from anyone who won't put numbers in writing.

**Ask about the warranty on repairs.** Reputable shops typically warranty parts for 90 days to one year and labor for 30 to 90 days.

**Check reviews on Google, Yelp, and the Better Business Bureau.** Look specifically for reviews that mention whether the company followed through when something went wrong.

**Watch for these red flags:**
- "Free diagnostic" that morphs into a $300 charge once they're in your home
- Pressure to decide immediately without time to compare quotes
- No physical business address (just a phone number)
- Request for full payment before the work is done

If you're in a warranty period, contact the manufacturer directly first. Most refrigerators come with a one-year parts and labor warranty; some brands extend compressor warranties to five to ten years.

## What Affects the Final Price

Several factors push your repair cost up or down:

**Brand and part availability.** Obscure import brands and older discontinued models can have parts that cost 2 to 3 times more than standard and take weeks to arrive.

**Your location.** Labor costs in San Francisco or New York City run 30 to 50 percent higher than in smaller Midwestern cities. Rural areas often add a trip charge of $25 to $75 on top of the standard service call fee.

**Emergency or same-day service.** Standard scheduling is fine if you can temporarily keep food in a cooler. If you need same-day service, expect to pay a premium of $50 to $150 extra.

**Type of refrigerant.** Older units using R-12 (Freon) face much higher recharge costs because the refrigerant itself is expensive and increasingly rare. Modern units use R-134a or R-600a, which are far cheaper.

**Access and installation complexity.** A built-in refrigerator surrounded by cabinetry takes longer to service than a freestanding model. Counter-depth models sometimes require partial cabinet removal, adding to the labor cost.

## DIY Repairs Worth Trying First

Not every refrigerator problem requires a technician. These are safe, inexpensive fixes most homeowners can handle:

**Clean the condenser coils.** Pull the fridge out from the wall, unplug it, and vacuum the coils. Takes 15 minutes and costs nothing. If coils are heavily caked with dust and pet hair, cooling performance can drop significantly.

**Check and replace door gaskets.** If the rubber seal around the door is cracked, torn, or no longer sticks to the refrigerator body when you close the door, warm air is leaking in. Replacement gaskets for common brands are $20 to $60 on Amazon or at appliance parts stores like RepairClinic or PartSelect. Installation usually involves peeling off the old gasket and pressing the new one into the door groove.

**Clear a clogged defrost drain.** Remove the lower back panel inside the freezer compartment and flush the drain tube with warm water mixed with a small amount of baking soda. This resolves most cases of water pooling under the refrigerator.

**Replace the water filter.** A clogged filter reduces ice production and water dispenser flow. Filters cost $15 to $50 and snap in without tools.

**Level the refrigerator.** A fridge that isn't level can cause doors to not seal correctly and ice makers to malfunction. Adjust the leveling feet until the fridge is slightly tilted back (this helps doors close on their own).

## Extended Warranties and Home Warranties

If you purchased an extended warranty or appliance protection plan, your repair may be fully or partially covered. Check your plan documents before calling a technician, since most plans require you to use their approved service network.

**Home warranty plans** (not the same as manufacturer warranties) often cover major appliances including refrigerators. Plans typically cost $400 to $700 per year with service call fees of $75 to $150 per visit. Coverage varies: some plans cover all components, others exclude cosmetic damage, damage from improper installation, or pre-existing conditions.

Before signing up for a home warranty to cover appliances, read the exclusions section carefully. The [National Association of Home Builders](https://www.nahb.org) offers guidance on evaluating home warranty products.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Is it worth repairing a refrigerator that is 10 years old?**
It depends on the repair. Minor repairs under $200 are usually worth doing even on a 10-year-old unit. Major repairs above $400 to $500 on a 10-year-old fridge are borderline, and a repair over $600 almost always makes replacement more sensible.

**Why did my refrigerator suddenly stop cooling?**
Start with the basics: check that it's plugged in and the outlet is working, verify the thermostat setting hasn't been accidentally changed, and check that the condenser coils aren't caked with dust. If those checks don't reveal the issue, a failing evaporator fan, refrigerant leak, or compressor issue is likely, and you'll need a technician.

**How long should a refrigerator last?**
The average lifespan is 10 to 20 years depending on brand and model. Top-freezer models tend to last longer (15 to 20 years) than French door or side-by-side models (10 to 15 years), partly because of added complexity. Built-in and commercial-grade refrigerators can last 20 to 30+ years with proper maintenance.

**Can a refrigerator compressor be repaired, or does it need to be replaced?**
In almost all residential cases, a failed compressor needs to be replaced rather than repaired. Compressor replacement on a unit more than 8 to 10 years old is rarely worth the cost unless the refrigerator is a premium brand with significant remaining value.
