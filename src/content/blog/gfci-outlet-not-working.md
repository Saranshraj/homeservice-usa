If your GFCI outlet stopped working, the fix is often as simple as pressing the small RESET button on the face of the outlet. About 80% of GFCI problems are solved in under 30 seconds this way, with no tools and no cost. If pressing RESET does not restore power, one of seven specific causes is almost certainly behind it, and this guide walks you through diagnosing and fixing each one.

GFCI outlets (the ones with TEST and RESET buttons) cost $8 to $20 at any hardware store. A licensed electrician charges $100 to $350 to replace one, including labor. Knowing which situation you are in before you call saves both time and money.

---

## What Is a GFCI Outlet and Why Does It Trip?

GFCI stands for Ground Fault Circuit Interrupter. These outlets are required by the National Electrical Code (NEC) in any area where water and electricity might meet: bathrooms, kitchens within 6 feet of a sink, garages, outdoor receptacles, unfinished basements, crawl spaces, and boat docks.

The outlet contains a tiny sensor that monitors current flowing out versus current flowing back. If those values differ by as little as 5 milliamps, the GFCI cuts power in about 1/40th of a second, fast enough to prevent a lethal shock. The U.S. Consumer Product Safety Commission (CPSC) estimates that GFCIs could prevent about two-thirds of the roughly 400 electrocutions that occur in and around the home each year.

When the GFCI "trips," it cuts power both to itself and to any standard outlets wired downstream from it on the same circuit. This is why pressing RESET on a GFCI outlet in one bathroom can suddenly restore power to an outlet in a different bathroom, in a hallway, or even outside the house.

---

## Step 1: Try the RESET Button First

Before anything else, locate the RESET button. It is the smaller of the two rectangular buttons on the face of the GFCI outlet, usually labeled RESET (the other is TEST). Press it firmly until you feel or hear a click.

If the outlet powers back on, you are done. Something caused a brief ground fault, the outlet did its job, and pressing RESET restored power. This is the most common outcome.

If the RESET button will not stay in (it springs back out when you press it), or if it clicks in but the outlet still has no power, move on to Step 2.

---

## Step 2: Check for a Tripped GFCI Somewhere Else

Here is something most homeowners do not know: a single GFCI outlet can protect multiple regular outlets on the same circuit. If a GFCI outlet in your garage tripped, it might have killed power to your outdoor outlets and even some indoor ones.

Do this before calling anyone:

1. Walk through every bathroom, kitchen, garage, laundry room, and exterior outlet location in your home.
2. Look for any GFCI outlet (TEST/RESET buttons) that has a RESET button popped out or sitting flush without being clicked in.
3. Press RESET on each one you find.

This step resolves the problem for a surprising number of homeowners who think their outlet is broken. Builders sometimes wire a whole floor of regular outlets downstream from a single GFCI to save money on materials.

---

## Step 3: Check Your Electrical Panel

If no tripped GFCIs were found in Step 2, go to your main electrical panel (breaker box). Look for any breaker that is not fully in the ON position. A tripped breaker usually sits in the middle position between ON and OFF, or it may appear in the OFF position.

To reset a tripped breaker: push it firmly to OFF first, then back to ON. If it trips again immediately, stop and call an electrician. A breaker that repeatedly trips indicates a short circuit, an overloaded circuit, or a failing breaker, all of which need professional diagnosis.

If the breaker looks fine but the outlet still does not work, your issue is specific to that outlet or circuit.

## 7 Reasons a GFCI Outlet Will Not Reset

### 1. Moisture Inside the Outlet

This is the most common cause, especially in bathrooms and outdoor locations. Even minor humidity can fool the GFCI sensor into detecting a "fault."

**Fix:** Turn off the breaker for that circuit. Use a hair dryer on low heat to gently dry the outlet for 60 to 90 seconds. Wait 10 minutes, turn the breaker back on, and try RESET again.

If you found actual water inside (from a leak or flooding), do not attempt to dry it yourself. Call an electrician to inspect the wiring before restoring power.

### 2. Something Plugged In Is Causing a Fault

A damaged appliance cord, a faulty lamp, or a defective power strip can cause a persistent ground fault that prevents the GFCI from resetting.

**Fix:** Unplug every device from the GFCI outlet and from any outlets downstream from it. Then try pressing RESET. If it now holds, plug your devices back in one at a time to identify the culprit. The faulty device needs to be repaired or replaced.

### 3. The GFCI Outlet Has Failed

GFCI outlets have a lifespan of roughly 10 to 15 years. The internal sensor degrades over time and can reach a point where it no longer resets correctly, even when no fault exists. This is especially common in humid environments.

**Fix:** Replace the outlet. A GFCI outlet costs $8 to $20 at home improvement stores. Replacement is a straightforward DIY task if you are comfortable working with electrical boxes (see the DIY vs. Pro section below).

### 4. No Power Reaching the Outlet (Wiring Issue)

If the breaker is on, no other GFCIs are tripped, and the outlet still shows zero power with nothing plugged in, the problem may be in the wiring between the panel and the outlet, such as a loose wire connection at the outlet, at a junction box, or at a previous outlet in the circuit.

**Fix:** Turn off the breaker, remove the outlet from the wall box, and inspect the wire connections on the back of the GFCI. Loose wires need to be firmly re-seated under the terminal screws. If you find burned wires, melted insulation, or any signs of heat damage, call an electrician immediately.

### 5. Wired Incorrectly After a Previous Repair

GFCI outlets have LINE terminals (where power comes in from the panel) and LOAD terminals (where power goes out to protect downstream outlets). They must not be swapped. An outlet wired with these reversed will not function correctly and will not reset.

**Fix:** Look at the back of the outlet with the breaker off. The LINE terminals have a tape or label covering them on a new outlet. If your existing outlet has the black (hot) wire on the LOAD terminal and the neutral on LOAD neutral, they are reversed. Swap them to the LINE side.

### 6. End-of-Line Wiring Without a Load Connection

A GFCI outlet at the end of a circuit has no wires on the LOAD terminals. This is correct. But some older GFCI models from the 1990s and early 2000s would not function without at least a jumper or pigtail on the LOAD side. If you have a very old GFCI (pre-2006), this may be the issue.

**Fix:** Replace it with a current-generation GFCI outlet, which is designed to function with nothing on the LOAD terminals.

### 7. A Nearby Arc or Fault Upstream

If a wire is damaged in the wall behind the outlet, or if another outlet earlier in the circuit has a loose connection arcing internally, the GFCI may detect repeated faults and refuse to hold a reset.

**Fix:** This requires professional diagnosis. An electrician uses a circuit tester to identify where along the circuit the fault originates.

## DIY vs. Calling a Licensed Electrician

**You can safely DIY if:**
- The fix is pressing RESET or unplugging devices
- You are replacing a failed GFCI outlet with an identical one on a 15-amp or 20-amp circuit
- You are comfortable turning off a breaker, using a voltage tester to confirm power is off, and reconnecting wires to terminal screws

A GFCI outlet replacement typically takes 20 to 30 minutes for a confident DIYer. The outlet itself costs $8 to $20. Total savings over hiring out: $80 to $180.

**Call a licensed electrician if:**
- The breaker trips again immediately after you reset it
- You see burned wires, melted plastic, or smell burning near the outlet
- Multiple outlets on the circuit are dead and no tripped GFCI explains it
- You are uncomfortable working inside electrical boxes
- The home is older than 1985 and has aluminum wiring (requires special outlets rated for AL/CU use)
- You are dealing with 240-volt outlets (dryer, range) rather than standard 120-volt ones

The [National Electrical Code](https://www.nfpa.org/codes-and-standards/nfpa-70-standard-for-electrical-installations/70) published by the National Fire Protection Association (NFPA) sets the standards that electricians follow. All GFCI installation work must meet these standards.

## How Much Does It Cost to Have an Electrician Fix a GFCI Outlet?

Electrician rates in the US range from $50 to $100 per hour, plus a service call fee of $50 to $150. GFCI outlet replacement is usually a 30 to 60-minute job. Total cost breakdown:

| Scenario | Typical Cost Range |
|---|---|
| Simple GFCI outlet replacement | $100 to $200 |
| Replace GFCI plus troubleshoot one downstream outlet | $150 to $300 |
| Rewire or repair circuit wiring | $250 to $600 |
| Upgrade home to current GFCI code (older homes) | $400 to $1,500+ |

Costs vary significantly by state. In California, New York, and other high-cost states, labor runs 20% to 40% higher than the national average. In rural areas of the South and Midwest, rates tend to run 15% to 25% lower.

The [Department of Energy](https://www.energy.gov/energysaver/electrical-safety-tips) recommends verifying that your electrician holds a valid state license. Most state licensing boards publish an online lookup tool, so you can confirm credentials before work begins.

## When Are GFCI Outlets Required by Code?

The NEC requires GFCI protection in these locations (requirements have expanded over the years, so older homes may not have them everywhere):

- All bathrooms (required since 1975)
- Garages (required since 1978)
- Outdoor outlets (required since 1978)
- Kitchen countertop surfaces within 6 feet of a sink (required since 1987)
- Crawl spaces and unfinished basements (required since 1990)
- Boathouses and boat docks (required since 1990)
- Wet bar sinks (required since 1993)
- Laundry and utility sinks (required since 2005)
- Kitchens and bathrooms of all types (expanded requirements since 2014)

If your home was built before 1990 and you have no GFCI outlets in the garage or outdoors, those locations are not up to current code. While you are not legally required to retroactively upgrade in most states, the CPSC strongly recommends doing so for safety.

## Testing Your GFCI Outlets: Do This Twice a Year

GFCIs can fail silently, meaning the outlet provides power normally but the protection circuit no longer works. The CPSC recommends testing every GFCI outlet twice a year (a good habit to pair with changing smoke detector batteries).

To test: Plug a lamp into the GFCI outlet and confirm the lamp is on. Press the TEST button. The lamp should go off immediately. If it does not, the GFCI has failed and needs to be replaced even if it feels like it is working. Press RESET to restore power.
