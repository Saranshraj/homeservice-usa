**The short answer:** Replace your AC unit if it is over 10 to 12 years old AND the repair costs more than $1,500. For units under 8 years old, repair almost always makes more sense unless the compressor has failed. The most reliable formula is the **$5,000 rule:** multiply the unit's age by the repair cost. If the result exceeds $5,000, replace it. For example, a 10-year-old unit needing a $600 repair: 10 x $600 = $6,000. That is over $5,000, so replacement is the better investment.

We know this decision is stressful. Your AC just died, it is 95 degrees outside, your family is miserable, and a technician is standing in your driveway telling you it needs a $2,000 repair or a $7,000 replacement. You need a clear framework, not a sales pitch. That is what this guide provides.

If your AC is currently blowing warm air and you are not sure what is wrong yet, start with our [AC troubleshooting guide](/blog/ac-blowing-warm-air/) to rule out simple fixes before spending money.

## The $5,000 Rule: A Simple Decision Formula

HVAC professionals widely recommend the $5,000 rule as a starting point:

**Unit Age x Repair Cost = Decision Number**

- **Under $5,000:** Lean toward repair
- **Over $5,000:** Lean toward replacement
- **Right around $5,000:** Consider other factors listed below

**Examples:**

| Unit Age | Repair Cost | Calculation | Decision |
|---|---|---|---|
| 4 years | $800 | 4 x $800 = $3,200 | Repair |
| 7 years | $500 | 7 x $500 = $3,500 | Repair |
| 8 years | $700 | 8 x $700 = $5,600 | Replace |
| 10 years | $400 | 10 x $400 = $4,000 | Repair (borderline) |
| 12 years | $600 | 12 x $600 = $7,200 | Replace |
| 15 years | $300 | 15 x $300 = $4,500 | Replace (age alone) |

This formula is not perfect for every situation, but it gives you a defensible starting point when a technician is pressuring you to make a quick decision.

## When Repair Is Almost Always the Right Call

Some situations clearly favor repair regardless of what a sales-oriented technician might suggest:

**Repair if:**
- The unit is less than 5 years old (still under most manufacturer warranties)
- The problem is a capacitor ($150 to $350), contactor ($200 to $400), or fan motor ($300 to $600)
- You have had zero or one previous repairs
- The refrigerant charge just needs topping off (though recurring leaks are different)
- The issue is electrical, like a blown fuse, tripped breaker, or faulty thermostat wiring

These are normal maintenance items that do not indicate the unit is failing. Every AC system needs occasional component replacements during its lifetime.

## When Replacement Is Almost Always the Right Call

Some situations clearly point toward replacement:

**Replace if:**
- The compressor has failed on a unit over 8 years old (compressor replacement alone costs $1,500 to $3,000)
- The unit uses R-22 (Freon) refrigerant, which was phased out in 2020 and now costs $80 to $150 per pound
- You are making your third repair in 12 months
- The repair cost exceeds 50 percent of what a new unit would cost
- The unit is 15 years old or older, regardless of the current problem
- Your energy bills have been climbing steadily for the past 2 to 3 years

### The R-22 Factor

This deserves special attention. If your AC unit was manufactured before 2010, it likely uses R-22 refrigerant. The EPA completed the phase-out of R-22 production and import in 2020 under the [Clean Air Act](https://www.epa.gov/ozone-layer-protection). The remaining supply is limited to reclaimed and recycled R-22, which means prices have skyrocketed.

Recharging an R-22 system now costs $400 to $1,500 depending on how much refrigerant is needed. And since refrigerant leaks do not fix themselves, you will likely need another recharge within a year or two. At that point, you are paying more in refrigerant costs than a new system would cost in energy savings.

## The Real Cost Comparison: Repair vs. Replace Over 5 Years

The repair vs. replace decision is not just about today's bill. Here is a realistic five-year comparison for a 12-year-old AC unit:

### Scenario A: Repair and Keep

| Year | Cost | Details |
|---|---|---|
| Year 1 | $1,200 | Current repair (compressor capacitor + labor) |
| Year 2 | $400 | Refrigerant recharge |
| Year 3 | $800 | Fan motor replacement |
| Year 4 | $1,500 | Another major repair or full failure |
| Year 5 | $6,500 | Emergency replacement (no time to shop) |
| **Energy premium** | **$1,500** | **Older unit uses 20-40% more energy** |
| **5-Year Total** | **$11,900** | |

### Scenario B: Replace Now

| Year | Cost | Details |
|---|---|---|
| Year 1 | $6,500 | New 16 SEER2 system installed |
| Years 2-5 | $0 | Under manufacturer warranty |
| **Energy savings** | **-$1,500** | **New unit uses 20-40% less energy** |
| **5-Year Total** | **$5,000** | |

This is not always the exact math, but the pattern holds: repairing an aging unit usually costs more over 3 to 5 years than replacing it would have.

## What a New AC System Costs in 2026

Understanding replacement costs helps you evaluate whether a repair quote is reasonable:

| System Type | Equipment + Installation | SEER2 Rating |
|---|---|---|
| Basic single-stage | $4,000-$6,500 | 14-15 SEER2 |
| Mid-range two-stage | $5,500-$8,500 | 16-17 SEER2 |
| High-efficiency variable speed | $7,500-$12,000 | 18-21 SEER2 |
| Mini-split (single zone) | $2,500-$5,000 | 18-22 SEER2 |
| Mini-split (multi-zone, 3-4 heads) | $7,000-$15,000 | 18-22 SEER2 |

For context, installing a [central AC system from scratch](/blog/central-ac-installation-cost/) costs more than replacing an existing one because new ductwork, electrical, and refrigerant lines may be needed.

### Federal Tax Credits

The Inflation Reduction Act offers a tax credit of up to **$2,000** for qualifying heat pump systems. If you are considering replacing your AC with a heat pump that provides both cooling and heating, this credit significantly changes the math. A $9,000 heat pump system with a $2,000 tax credit has an effective cost of $7,000, which is competitive with a standard AC-only replacement.

Check the [IRS energy credits page](https://www.irs.gov/credits-deductions/energy-efficient-home-improvement-credit) and [ENERGY STAR's rebate finder](https://www.energystar.gov/rebates) for current eligibility.

## SEER Ratings: Why Efficiency Matters for This Decision

SEER (Seasonal Energy Efficiency Ratio) measures how efficiently an AC unit converts electricity into cooling. Higher SEER means lower operating costs.

If your current unit is 10 to 15 years old, it is likely rated 10 to 13 SEER. New units start at 14 SEER2 (the updated rating standard), with high-efficiency models reaching 20+ SEER2.

**What this means in dollars:** Upgrading from a 10 SEER unit to a 16 SEER2 unit in a climate like Dallas, TX, can save $300 to $500 per year in cooling costs. Over 15 years, that is $4,500 to $7,500 in energy savings alone.

The [Department of Energy](https://www.energy.gov/energysaver/central-air-conditioning) provides detailed efficiency comparisons and calculators.

## Red Flags: When Your HVAC Technician Is Upselling

Not every technician who recommends replacement is trying to upsell you, but some are. Watch for these red flags:

- **They diagnose replacement without a thorough inspection.** A good tech spends 30 to 60 minutes inspecting the system before recommending replacement.
- **They will not show you the failed part.** If they say a component is bad, ask to see it. Legitimate technicians are happy to show you.
- **They pressure you to decide today.** "This price is only good today" is a sales tactic, not a legitimate business practice.
- **They only quote their most expensive system.** A reputable company will offer good-better-best options.
- **They do not mention rebates or tax credits.** A tech who does not bring up available savings may not have your best interest in mind.

Always get two to three quotes for replacement. Our [contractor vetting guide](/blog/how-to-find-good-contractor/) explains how to evaluate HVAC companies specifically.

## Questions to Ask Before Deciding

Ask your technician these questions to make a more informed decision:

1. What specifically failed, and can you show me?
2. Is this a common failure for this model and age?
3. What is the realistic remaining lifespan if I do this repair?
4. Does my unit use R-22 or R-410A refrigerant?
5. What would a comparable replacement system cost?
6. Are there any rebates or tax credits I should know about?
7. Can I get a second opinion before deciding?

A trustworthy technician will welcome these questions. Someone who gets defensive or dismissive is not someone you want working on a $5,000 to $10,000 decision.

## The Seasonal Timing Factor

When you replace your AC matters almost as much as whether you replace it:

- **Best time to replace:** Spring (March to May) or fall (September to November). HVAC companies are less busy, and many run off-season promotions. You can save 10 to 20 percent.
- **Worst time to replace:** July and August. Demand is highest, wait times are longest, and you have no leverage to negotiate.

If your unit is showing warning signs in the spring, do not wait until it dies in August. A planned replacement on your schedule always costs less than an emergency replacement in peak season.

## Home Warranty Considerations

If you have a home warranty, check your coverage before making this decision. Most home warranties cover AC repair and replacement, but the actual experience varies wildly. Some warranties cover the full replacement cost; others cap payouts at $1,500 to $3,000, leaving you responsible for the rest.
