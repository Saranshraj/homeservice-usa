**The short answer:** Radon mitigation costs between **$800 and $2,500** for most US homeowners, with the national average sitting around **$1,200**. Before you get there, radon testing costs **$15 to $30 for a DIY kit** or **$150 to $300 for a professional test**. If your test results come back at **4 pCi/L or higher**, the [EPA recommends immediate mitigation](https://www.epa.gov/radon/consumers-guide-radon-reduction). The good news: a properly installed mitigation system reduces indoor radon levels by up to **99%** in most homes, and the fix is more affordable than most people expect.

If you are reading this, you probably just got a test result back and you are scared. Or maybe a home inspector flagged radon during a sale and you do not know what to do next. Either way, take a breath: radon is the second leading cause of lung cancer in the US, but it is also one of the most solvable problems a home can have. This guide will walk you through exactly what you will pay, what the numbers on your test actually mean, and how to get it fixed the right way.

## What Is Radon and Why Does It Matter?

Radon is a naturally occurring radioactive gas that forms when uranium in soil and rock breaks down. It seeps into homes through cracks in foundations, gaps around pipes, and even through concrete block walls. You cannot see it, smell it, or taste it. The only way to know if you have a problem is to test.

According to the [EPA's radon data](https://www.epa.gov/radon), roughly **1 in 15 US homes** has elevated radon levels. The Midwest and Appalachian regions tend to have the highest concentrations, but elevated radon has been found in all 50 states. Even new construction can have a problem if the site sits on radon-prone soil.

The [EPA estimates radon causes about 21,000 lung cancer deaths per year](https://www.epa.gov/radon/health-risk-radon) in the United States, second only to cigarette smoking. Non-smokers who live in high-radon homes have a roughly 1-in-20 lifetime risk of developing lung cancer. Smokers living with elevated radon face dramatically higher odds.

The fix is not glamorous, but it works. A properly installed mitigation system is essentially a fan and a pipe that draws radon out from under your home before it enters your living space.

## Step 1: Radon Testing Costs

You cannot address a radon problem you do not know about. Testing is always the first step, and it is far cheaper than most homeowners expect.

| Testing Option | Cost | Turnaround | Best For |
|---|---|---|---|
| Short-term DIY kit (2-7 days) | $15 to $30 | 2-4 weeks (mail-in lab) | Initial screening |
| Long-term DIY kit (90-365 days) | $25 to $50 | After test period | Most accurate baseline |
| Professional short-term test | $150 to $300 | Same-day or next-day results | Real estate transactions |
| Continuous electronic monitor | $150 to $500 | Real-time | Ongoing monitoring post-mitigation |

**DIY kits** are sold at hardware stores and online. You place the kit in the lowest livable area of your home (usually the basement or first floor), leave it for the specified time, then mail it to a certified lab. Results come back by email in a week or two. These kits are accurate enough for initial screening and use the same charcoal canister or alpha-track detection technology that professionals use.

**Professional tests** make the most sense when you are buying or selling a home and need results fast. A certified radon measurement professional places an electronic monitor in your home and retrieves it 48 hours later. Results are available the same day, and the report is legally defensible for real estate transactions.

Look for testers certified by the [National Radon Proficiency Program (NRPP)](https://nrpp.info/) or the [National Radon Safety Board (NRSB)](https://www.nrsb.org/).

### Understanding Your Results

Radon is measured in picocuries per liter of air (pCi/L). Here is what the numbers mean according to EPA guidance:

| Radon Level | EPA Recommendation |
|---|---|
| Below 2 pCi/L | No action needed (average US indoor level is about 1.3 pCi/L) |
| 2 to 3.9 pCi/L | Consider mitigation; EPA recommends fixing at this range if possible |
| 4 pCi/L and above | Fix your home; this is EPA's action level |
| 10 pCi/L and above | Mitigate as soon as possible |
| 20 pCi/L and above | Consider temporary relocation during mitigation |

The EPA's action level of 4 pCi/L is the threshold where the health risk becomes clearly unacceptable. But even levels between 2 and 4 pCi/L carry meaningful risk. If you have a choice, getting below 2 pCi/L is the goal.

## Step 2: Radon Mitigation System Costs

If your test results are at or above 4 pCi/L, you need a mitigation system. Here is what you will pay:

| Home Type / System | Average Cost | Range |
|---|---|---|
| Sub-slab depressurization (most homes) | $1,100 to $1,500 | $800 to $2,500 |
| Crawl space encapsulation with vent | $1,500 to $3,000 | $1,200 to $4,000 |
| Drain tile depressurization | $1,000 to $1,500 | $800 to $2,000 |
| Block wall suction (basement walls) | $1,200 to $2,000 | $1,000 to $2,500 |
| Sump-hole suction | $900 to $1,300 | $700 to $1,500 |

### The Most Common Fix: Sub-Slab Depressurization (SSD)

About 90% of homes with radon problems are fixed using **sub-slab depressurization**, also called active soil depressurization (ASD). Here is how it works:

1. A contractor drills one or more holes through your basement floor or slab.
2. A PVC pipe is inserted and routed up through the home (through an interior wall or garage) or along an exterior wall.
3. A continuously running fan is mounted near the top of the pipe, usually in the attic or outside.
4. The fan creates negative pressure under the slab, pulling radon-laden air out before it enters the house.
5. The pipe exhausts that air above the roofline, safely outdoors.

A typical SSD installation takes 4 to 8 hours and the system runs 24/7 afterward. The fan is quiet (similar to a bathroom exhaust fan) and uses about the same electricity as a light bulb: roughly **$30 to $100 per year** in added utility costs.

### What Drives the Price

Several factors push your mitigation quote higher or lower:

**Foundation type:** Homes with full basements and poured concrete slabs are the easiest and cheapest to mitigate. Homes with crawl spaces, block foundations, or multiple foundation types cost more because more complex approaches are needed.

**Number of suction points:** One pipe with one fan handles most homes. If the suction field does not reach all areas under the slab (common in older homes or homes with additions), a second pipe and fan may be needed, adding **$300 to $600**.

**Pipe routing:** Running the pipe through an interior wall (preferred aesthetically) takes more labor than attaching it to an exterior wall. Expect to pay an extra **$100 to $300** for a cleaner interior route.

**Regional labor rates:** Contractor rates vary significantly by state. In the Midwest and rural South, mitigation systems often run **$800 to $1,200**. In California, the Northeast, and major metro areas, costs tend to be **$1,500 to $2,500**.

**Post-installation testing:** A good contractor will retest after installation to verify the system is working. This is often included in the quote, but confirm before signing.

## State-by-State Radon Risk

Some areas of the country have much higher average radon concentrations than others. The [EPA's radon zone map](https://www.epa.gov/radon/find-information-about-local-radon-zones-and-state-contact-information) divides the US into three zones:

**Zone 1 (Highest Risk, predicted average above 4 pCi/L):** Iowa, North Dakota, South Dakota, Nebraska, Kansas, Minnesota, Wisconsin, Michigan, Ohio, Pennsylvania, and parts of Colorado, Wyoming, Montana, and New England.

**Zone 2 (Moderate Risk, 2 to 4 pCi/L):** A broad band of states across the South, Mountain West, and mid-Atlantic.

**Zone 3 (Lower Risk, below 2 pCi/L):** Much of California, coastal states in the Southeast, and Hawaii.

Living in Zone 2 or 3 does not mean you are safe. Local geology matters enormously. A neighborhood in a Zone 2 state can have pockets of very high radon. Testing your specific home is the only reliable answer.

**States with mandatory radon disclosure laws** (meaning sellers must disclose known radon results) include: Iowa, Indiana, Ohio, Pennsylvania, and several others. Laws change frequently, so check with your state's real estate board for current requirements.

## DIY Radon Mitigation: Is It Worth It?

DIY radon mitigation systems are sold online for **$200 to $400** (fan, pipe, fittings, and supplies). Some experienced DIYers install them successfully. But there are real reasons most experts recommend hiring a certified contractor:

**Suction field diagnostics:** Certified mitigators use a "communication test" or manometer to map how air moves under your slab. Without this step, you might install a pipe that does not actually reach the radon source.

**Code compliance:** Many local building codes require permits for radon mitigation. An unpermitted system can cause problems during a future home sale.

**Post-installation testing:** Certified contractors are required by professional standards to retest after installation. If the system does not get results below 4 pCi/L, they come back and add suction points.

**Liability:** If you DIY and the system does not work, you own the problem. A certified contractor's work comes with professional accountability.

If budget is a serious constraint, a DIY approach using [EPA guidelines](https://www.epa.gov/radon/consumers-guide-radon-reduction) can work on simple slab-on-grade homes. But for most homeowners, the cost difference between DIY and professional installation ($800 to $1,100 gap) is worth the peace of mind.

## Ongoing Costs After Installation

A radon mitigation system is not fully installed and forgotten. Here is what to budget going forward:

| Ongoing Cost | Frequency | Estimated Cost |
|---|---|---|
| Post-installation retest | Once (after installation) | Included or $50 to $150 |
| Annual radon testing | Yearly (recommended by EPA) | $15 to $30 (DIY kit) |
| Fan replacement | Every 5 to 10 years | $75 to $200 (fan only) |
| Fan electricity usage | Monthly (ongoing) | $2.50 to $8 per month |
| System inspection | Every 2 years | Often DIY visual check |

The EPA recommends retesting your home every two years after mitigation, and any time you make structural changes to the foundation or lower levels of your home. Long-term alpha-track test kits are the most reliable option for ongoing monitoring.

A manometer (pressure gauge) is often installed on the pipe during mitigation. If you see the indicator fluid shift significantly from its initial position, the fan may be failing and it is time to call your contractor.

## Does Homeowners Insurance Cover Radon?

In most cases, no. Standard homeowners insurance policies treat radon as a pre-existing environmental condition rather than sudden, accidental damage. Radon remediation is almost always an out-of-pocket expense.

A few things to know:

- If you are buying a home and the seller agreed to pay for radon mitigation as a negotiated condition of the sale, that is the most common way buyers get this covered.
- Some home warranty plans offer optional environmental add-ons, but radon mitigation is rarely included in standard coverage. See our [home warranty guide](/blog/home-warranty-worth-it/) for more on what these plans actually cover.
- The IRS does not currently offer a federal tax credit specifically for radon mitigation. Check your state: some states, including Iowa and New York, have offered rebates or tax incentives for radon reduction in certain years. Check your state's department of health or environmental quality for current programs.

## How to Find a Certified Radon Contractor

Radon mitigation is a licensed trade in many states. Here is how to find someone trustworthy:

1. **Search certified contractors:** Use the [NRPP contractor database](https://nrpp.info/) or the [NRSB directory](https://www.nrsb.org/) to find certified mitigators in your area.
2. **Get at least two quotes:** Prices for similar work can vary by several hundred dollars between contractors.
3. **Ask what is included:** Make sure the quote covers installation, post-installation testing, and the diagnostic suction field test before drilling.
4. **Confirm permits:** Ask whether they will pull the required permits. A contractor who skips permits is a red flag.
5. **Check your state's requirements:** Many states have their own licensing requirements beyond national certification. Check with your [state radon contact](https://www.epa.gov/radon/find-information-about-local-radon-zones-and-state-contact-information) for specifics.

## Quick Decision Guide

Not sure where you stand? Here is a simple framework:

**Test result below 2 pCi/L:** Retest in two years. No immediate action needed.

**Test result 2 to 3.9 pCi/L:** Consider mitigation, especially if you have young children or someone in the home who smokes. The health risk is real but lower. If you plan to sell the home in the next few years, mitigating now avoids a future negotiating headache.

**Test result 4 pCi/L or above:** Get a certified contractor out for a quote within the next 30 days. At this level, the EPA says fix it. Budget $800 to $2,500 for the system.

**Test result 10 pCi/L or above:** Prioritize this. Consider spending time in lower-risk areas of the home (upper floors) until the system is installed. At 10 pCi/L, the cumulative exposure is significant.
