Air duct cleaning costs **$300 to $1,000** for most US homes in 2026, with the national average landing around **$450 to $550** for a typical 1,500 to 2,500 square foot house. Most companies price by the number of vents, charging **$25 to $50 per supply or return vent**, plus a base fee of $75 to $150 for the main trunk line and equipment setup. A 3-bedroom home with 15 to 20 vents typically comes out to **$400 to $700** all-in when booked through a reputable NADCA-certified company. If you are seeing ads for "$79 whole-house duct cleaning," stop right there. That price is almost always a bait-and-switch, and we will explain exactly how that scam works later in this guide.

These ranges assume a single-story or two-story home with a standard [central HVAC system](/blog/central-ac-installation-cost/) and accessible ductwork. Homes with complicated layouts, partially blocked ducts, or significant debris buildup may cost more.

## What Drives the Price

Understanding the cost breakdown helps you evaluate quotes and push back when something does not add up.

**Number of vents.** This is the biggest cost driver. Most companies charge per vent (supply registers and return air grilles separately). A home with 25 vents at $35 each equals $875 before any add-ons. Always ask for a per-vent price before agreeing to anything.

**Type of ductwork.** Flexible duct (the accordion-style silver tubing common in newer homes) is easier to clean than older rigid sheet metal ductwork with sharp bends and turns. Sheet metal costs more to service but holds up better over time. Fiberglass duct board requires extra care and the right equipment.

**Degree of contamination.** A system with light dust and a few pet hair clumps costs much less than one with a decade of buildup, rodent droppings, or active mold. If the technician discovers mold, you are looking at antimicrobial fogging or treatment on top of the cleaning cost.

**System configuration.** Homes with multiple HVAC zones, dual systems (one for upstairs and one for downstairs), or hard-to-reach ducts in a crawl space cost more than a simple single-zone setup.

**Location and labor market.** Prices in New York City, San Francisco, or Boston run 30 to 50 percent higher than in mid-size Midwestern cities. Rural areas can go either way, since fewer competitors sometimes drives prices up.

**The company's equipment.** Legitimate NADCA-certified companies use truck-mounted or high-powered portable vacuums with HEPA filtration, plus rotary brushes. Companies offering dramatically low prices often use shop-vac-level equipment that stirs up debris without actually removing it.

## The $79 Duct Cleaning Scam: How It Works and How to Spot It

Let us talk about something that trips up thousands of homeowners every year. You have probably seen the ads: "Whole House Duct Cleaning: $79!" or "10 Vents for $49!" These offers are almost never legitimate, and here is the playbook they follow:

1. You call and book the appointment based on the advertised price.
2. The technician arrives and starts "inspecting" your ducts with a camera or flashlight.
3. They show you photos (sometimes of other homes, sometimes blurred or distorted) of "mold," "heavy buildup," or "biological contamination."
4. The original $79 price suddenly does not cover what you "really need." The true bill is $800 to $2,000.
5. If you resist, they may leave your system partially disassembled or simply refuse to honor the original quote.

The [Federal Trade Commission (FTC)](https://consumer.ftc.gov/) has published warnings about fraudulent duct cleaning operations. Red flags include: pressure to decide immediately, prices that seem impossibly low, requests for cash only, no written estimate before work begins, and technicians who show up without marked company vehicles.

**How to protect yourself:**

- Get at least 2 to 3 written quotes before booking.
- Ask whether the company is certified by NADCA (the National Air Duct Cleaners Association). NADCA members follow the ACR (Assessment, Cleaning, and Restoration) Standard.
- Ask for references from recent local customers.
- Never pay the full amount upfront.
- If a technician tries to upsell mold treatment without an independent air quality test, be very skeptical.

## Does Air Duct Cleaning Actually Help? What the EPA Says

Here is where we have to be honest with you, because a lot of duct cleaning companies are not.

The [U.S. Environmental Protection Agency (EPA)](https://www.epa.gov/indoor-air-quality-iaq/should-you-have-air-ducts-your-home-cleaned) has reviewed the research on air duct cleaning and concluded that **there is no conclusive evidence** that cleaning ducts prevents health problems in most homes. The EPA's position is that duct cleaning is not routinely necessary, and it does not recommend it unless one of these specific conditions applies:

- There is **visible mold growth** inside hard surface ducts or on other components of your heating and cooling system.
- Ducts are **infested with vermin** such as rodents or insects.
- Ducts are **clogged with excessive debris** that is visibly releasing particles into the home through supply registers.

Outside of these three situations, cleaning your ducts may not make a measurable difference in air quality or system efficiency. The EPA also warns that improper cleaning can actually increase dust levels by disturbing settled particles without fully removing them.

That said, many homeowners report that duct cleaning helped after they noticed increased dust on furniture, after a major renovation that produced drywall or wood dust, or after buying an older home where the system had never been serviced. There is a real difference between "scientifically proven to improve health outcomes" and "makes practical sense in my situation."

**Bottom line:** If none of the three EPA conditions apply to your home, you likely do not need to rush out and clean your ducts. If they do apply, professional cleaning is worth the cost.

## When Duct Cleaning Makes Real Sense

Even without a specific problem triggering the EPA's three conditions, there are circumstances where cleaning provides genuine value.

**After buying an older home.** If you [moved into a house](/blog/first-year-homeowner-guide/) where the HVAC system was never serviced, cleaning the ducts gives you a fresh start and lets you visually inspect the system for problems.

**After major renovation or construction.** Drywall dust, sawdust, and [insulation fibers](/blog/attic-insulation-cost/) from a remodel can coat duct interiors and circulate through the home for months. Cleaning after major work is standard practice for many contractors.

**After rodent or pest infestation.** Once exterminators have addressed the infestation, cleaning and sanitizing the ductwork removes droppings, nesting material, and odors.

**Severe pet dander situation.** If you have multiple pets and an older system, hair and dander accumulation can reach a point where cleaning helps. This is more of a quality-of-life call than a health necessity.

**Musty odors coming from vents.** A persistent musty smell when the system runs often indicates mold or moisture in the ductwork. Get an inspection before assuming you need a full cleaning.

## DIY vs. Hiring a Pro

| | DIY | Professional |
|---|---|---|
| Cost | $50 to $150 (equipment rental or purchase) | $300 to $1,000 |
| Equipment | Shop vacuum, brush kit, vent covers | Truck-mounted HEPA vacuum, rotary brushes, cameras |
| Effectiveness | Surface level only | Full depth cleaning |
| Risk of damage | Moderate (flexible duct tears easily) | Low if NADCA-certified |
| Mold treatment | Not equipped | Can treat and certify |
| Time | 3 to 6 hours | 2 to 4 hours |

DIY duct cleaning is an option for light maintenance. You can remove vent covers, wipe them down, and vacuum several inches into the duct with a long-hose attachment. However, you will not reach the main trunk line, deep bends, or the air handler itself. For a thorough cleaning, especially if you are dealing with mold or rodent contamination, [hire a professional](/blog/how-to-find-good-contractor/).

If you do go DIY, change your air filter first and again after cleaning. A clogged filter is often responsible for more of your air quality problems than the ducts themselves. The [U.S. Department of Energy](https://www.energy.gov/energysaver/maintaining-your-air-conditioner) recommends checking your filter every month and replacing it every 1 to 3 months depending on use and filter type.

## What a Legitimate Cleaning Should Include

When you hire a reputable company, here is what you should expect to happen:

1. **Pre-inspection.** The technician inspects accessible portions of the duct system visually or with a camera before starting work.
2. **System protection.** Furniture and floors near return air grilles are protected.
3. **Negative air pressure.** The technician creates negative pressure in the duct system using powerful vacuum equipment so that debris is drawn out rather than blown around.
4. **Mechanical agitation.** Rotary brushes or pneumatic whips loosen debris from duct walls while the vacuum removes it.
5. **All components cleaned.** A full cleaning covers not just the ducts but also supply and return air registers, grilles, diffusers, the [heat exchanger](/blog/furnace-replacement-cost/), fan motor and housing, and the drip pan.
6. **Post-inspection.** Before leaving, the technician should show you before and after photos or video.
7. **Written documentation.** You receive a written summary of what was done.

Ask for this scope of work in writing before signing anything. If a company refuses to commit to this standard or brushes off your questions, find someone else.

## Add-On Services: Worth It or Not?

| Service | Typical Cost | Worth It? |
|---|---|---|
| Dryer vent cleaning | $75 to $150 | Yes, always. Clogged dryer vents are a leading cause of house fires. |
| Antimicrobial fogging/sanitizing | $150 to $500 | Only if mold is confirmed, not as a preventive upsell. |
| UV light installation in air handler | $150 to $400 | Has some evidence for reducing mold and bacteria in the air handler itself. |
| Duct sealing (Aeroseal or mastic) | $1,000 to $3,000 | Worth it if duct leakage is diagnosed; saves significantly on energy bills. |
| Coil cleaning | $100 to $300 | Recommended if the evaporator coil is visibly dirty; often included in HVAC tune-ups. |

The dryer vent cleaning add-on is almost always a good idea. The [U.S. Fire Administration](https://www.usfa.fema.gov/) reports that clothes dryers cause about 2,900 residential fires per year, with failure to clean being the leading cause. If your dryer is taking longer than usual to dry a load or the exterior vent flap is not opening fully, cleaning is overdue.

## How Often Should You Clean Your Ducts?

The [EPA](https://www.epa.gov/indoor-air-quality-iaq/should-you-have-air-ducts-your-home-cleaned) does not recommend a specific cleaning interval because, as discussed above, routine cleaning is not supported by strong evidence. NADCA suggests every 3 to 5 years as a general guideline, but this is a recommendation from an industry group with a financial interest in regular cleanings.

A more practical approach: clean when there is a reason to, not on a rigid schedule. Inspect your filters and vent covers regularly. If you notice unusual dust accumulation on furniture, reduced airflow from certain vents, or unexplained allergy flare-ups indoors, that is a signal to have the system inspected. Many HVAC companies will perform a duct inspection as part of an annual tune-up, which is a good way to catch problems without committing to a full cleaning every year.

## How to Find a Reputable Duct Cleaning Company

1. **Search NADCA's member directory.** Visit [nadca.com](https://www.nadca.com) to find certified Air Systems Cleaning Specialists in your area. NADCA certification requires training, testing, and adherence to industry standards.
2. **Check state licensing.** Some states require HVAC contractors to hold a state license to perform duct cleaning. Contact your state contractor licensing board to verify.
3. **Read reviews carefully.** Look for reviews on Google and the BBB (Better Business Bureau) that specifically mention the final price compared to the quoted price.
4. **Get everything in writing.** A legitimate company will give you a written estimate with per-vent pricing and a scope of work before starting.
5. **Ask about their vacuum equipment.** Truck-mounted vacuums are the gold standard. Portable HEPA vacuums are acceptable. Shop vacs are a red flag.
