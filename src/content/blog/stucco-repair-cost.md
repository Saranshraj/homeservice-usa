Stucco repair costs between **$8 and $50 per square foot**, with most homeowners paying **$500 to $2,500** for a typical job. Small crack fills run as low as $150, while large sections with underlying water damage can top $6,000 or more. The final price depends heavily on how deep the damage goes, whether the lath or framing underneath is compromised, and what type of stucco you have on your home.

If your stucco is showing hairline cracks after a hot summer or a freeze-thaw cycle, you are not alone. Stucco is one of the most popular exterior finishes in the country, covering an estimated 12 million U.S. homes, mostly in the Southwest, Southeast, and older neighborhoods across the country. It is durable, but it does crack, chip, and bubble over time. Knowing what a repair should cost before you call a contractor can save you hundreds.

## What Drives the Cost of Stucco Repair

### 1. How Deep the Damage Goes

Stucco is applied in layers: a scratch coat, a brown coat, and a finish coat. If only the top finish coat is cracked, that is the cheapest fix. If the crack runs all the way through to the wire lath or the building paper underneath, labor and material costs jump significantly because the contractor has to rebuild multiple layers.

Water infiltration is the worst scenario. When moisture gets behind stucco and sits there, it rots the wood lath or sheathing, and you may also end up with mold. That changes a $400 patch job into a $3,000-plus remediation.

### 2. Type of Stucco on Your Home

**Traditional three-coat stucco** (the kind on homes built before the 1990s) is the gold standard. Matching its texture and color is a skill, and experienced plasterers charge accordingly.

**One-coat stucco** is thinner and found on many homes from the 1980s through 2000s. It is faster to apply but also more prone to cracking and water intrusion.

**Exterior Insulation and Finish System (EIFS)**, sometimes called synthetic stucco, is a foam-based system that feels soft when you press it. EIFS repairs require a specialist because the system has drainage layers that must be maintained. Expect to pay 20 to 40 percent more for EIFS repair than for traditional stucco.

### 3. Texture Matching

Stucco comes in dozens of textures: smooth, lace, dash, sand, skip trowel, and more. A contractor who does not specialize in stucco may patch a hole but leave a texture mismatch that looks worse than the original crack. Skilled plasterers who can blend textures charge more, but the result is worth it.

### 4. Paint and Color Match

If your stucco is painted, the patched area will need to be painted to match. This adds $1 to $3 per square foot for materials and labor. If the paint has faded significantly over time, a full recoat of the wall may be the only way to get a seamless result, adding $500 to $2,500 to the job.

### 5. Location and Accessibility

Ground-level cracks are cheap to fix because no scaffolding is needed. Second-story and gable-end repairs require ladders or scaffolding, which adds $200 to $800 in setup costs alone.

## Common Stucco Problems and What They Cost to Fix

### Hairline Cracks

Hairline cracks, those thinner than 1/16 of an inch, are almost always normal. Stucco expands and contracts with temperature, and fine surface cracks are cosmetic in most cases.

**What to expect:** A contractor can fill hairline cracks with elastomeric caulk or a stucco patch product and apply a color-matched finish for **$50 to $150 per crack**. If you have dozens of them spread across a wall, a full recoat may be more cost-effective.

**DIY viability:** High. You can buy stucco crack filler at any home center for $10 to $20. Matching the texture is the hard part.

### Stair-Step Cracks

Stair-step cracks follow the mortar lines in block construction, creating a staircase pattern. They often signal settling or minor foundation movement.

**What to expect:** Repair runs **$200 to $800** depending on length. If the foundation is the cause, that issue must be addressed first, which is a separate and much larger cost.

**DIY viability:** Low to medium. If the foundation is not involved, a skilled DIYer can fill and texture the area. But if the cracks keep coming back, call a structural engineer before spending money on cosmetic fixes.

### Bulging or Bubbling Stucco

Bulges mean water is trapped behind the stucco and separating it from the substrate. This is urgent. Left alone, bulging stucco eventually falls off in sheets, and the moisture causes wood rot behind it.

**What to expect:** Small bubble: **$400 to $900**. Large area or one with rot: **$1,500 to $5,000+**. The contractor removes the damaged section, replaces the building paper or moisture barrier, allows everything to dry, and re-applies stucco in multiple coats.

**DIY viability:** Very low. Improper waterproofing on a stucco repair is worse than leaving it alone.

### Chipped or Missing Stucco

Impact damage from a basketball, a weed trimmer, or hail can knock chunks off. This is a straightforward patch, as long as the underlying layers are intact.

**What to expect:** **$150 to $600** for most impact chips. The contractor removes loose material, applies bonding agent, rebuilds the coats, and textures.

**DIY viability:** Medium. With the right products and patience, handy homeowners can patch small chips. The texture match is the challenge.

## Stucco Repair Costs by State

Labor rates vary considerably across the country. Stucco is especially common in California, Arizona, New Mexico, Texas, and Florida, where there are more experienced plasterers.

| Region / State | Typical Cost Modifier |
|---|---|
| California (Bay Area, LA) | 25 to 50% above national average |
| Arizona, New Mexico | Near national average |
| Texas (metro areas) | 10 to 20% above average |
| Florida | At or slightly above average |
| Southeast (non-Florida) | 5 to 15% below average (less common, fewer specialists) |
| Midwest and Northeast | 10 to 25% above average (fewer specialists, higher labor) |

If you are in the Midwest or Northeast, stucco specialists may be hard to find. A general contractor who "does stucco" is not the same as a licensed plasterer. Ask for references and photos of previous texture-match work before hiring anyone.

## Three-Coat vs. One-Coat Stucco Repair: Why It Matters

**Three-coat stucco** was the standard until the late 1980s. It is typically 7/8 inch thick. Repairs must be done in stages because each coat must cure before the next is applied. A proper three-coat repair takes two to three days minimum. Rushing it causes the top coat to crack.

**One-coat stucco** (also called "Portland-based one-coat") is thinner, around 3/8 to 1/2 inch. It can be repaired faster, but the material is less forgiving about texture blending.

**EIFS/synthetic stucco** looks like stucco but is fundamentally different. It is a multilayer system with rigid insulation board, a base coat, fiberglass mesh, and a finish coat. EIFS must be repaired by someone who understands the drainage plane. The Consumer Product Safety Commission ([CPSC](https://www.cpsc.gov)) issued guidance in the late 1990s on EIFS moisture problems, and lawsuits related to improperly installed EIFS were common for years. If you have EIFS, hire a certified EIFS inspector or contractor.

## DIY Stucco Repair: When It Makes Sense

You can do DIY stucco repair if:

- The crack is hairline or the chip is small (under 1 square foot)
- There is no sign of moisture intrusion (no dark staining, soft spots, or mold smell)
- You are comfortable working on a ladder
- The area is at or near ground level

**What you will need:**

- Pre-mixed stucco patch or stucco repair caulk ($10 to $30 at any home center)
- Bonding agent or liquid bonding additive ($15 to $25)
- A margin trowel and pool trowel
- Texture tools (sponge, brush, or spray gun depending on your finish)
- Masonry paint for touch-up ($20 to $50)

The U.S. Department of Energy ([DOE](https://www.energy.gov)) notes that sealing exterior cracks is one of the most cost-effective ways to reduce air infiltration and improve home energy efficiency. Even if the patch is not perfect, sealing gaps is worthwhile.

## When You Must Hire a Pro

Call a licensed stucco contractor if:

- **The stucco is soft or spongy to the touch.** That means moisture is already inside.
- **You see dark staining around the crack.** Water has been getting in, possibly for years.
- **Multiple cracks are appearing in a pattern.** Structural movement or foundation issues may be the root cause.
- **The repair is on a second story or higher.** Scaffolding, working heights, and texture matching at elevation require professional skills and insurance.
- **You have EIFS or synthetic stucco.** Improper repair creates water traps.
- **The cracked area is near a window or door.** Flashing and sealing around penetrations must be done correctly to prevent long-term water intrusion.

## How to Find and Vet a Stucco Contractor

**Get at least three quotes.** Stucco repair pricing varies widely, and so does quality. The cheapest bid is often not the best value.

**Ask about their licensing.** Most states require plastering or masonry contractors to be licensed. Check your state's contractor licensing board. California's Contractors State License Board ([CSLB](https://www.cslb.ca.gov)) is a good model for how to verify credentials. Arizona, Florida, Texas, and other states have similar boards searchable online.

**Request photos of similar texture-match jobs.** Anyone can fill a hole. Matching your home's specific texture is the skill that matters.

**Confirm they carry liability insurance and workers' comp.** Stucco work involves ladders, power tools, and materials that can damage neighboring surfaces if something goes wrong.

**Ask about their curing and weather requirements.** Stucco applied in direct sun above 90 degrees or when rain is expected within 24 hours will not cure properly. A contractor who rushes this step creates a future problem for you.

The National Association of Home Builders ([NAHB](https://www.nahb.org)) recommends getting written contracts for any exterior repair over $500, specifying materials, number of coats, and cure time between applications.

## How to Prevent Stucco from Cracking Again

- **Caulk around all windows, doors, and penetrations every few years.** These are the entry points for water.
- **Maintain gutters and downspouts.** Water that pours down a stucco wall from an overflowing gutter is the leading cause of stucco moisture damage.
- **Repaint on schedule.** A good quality elastomeric paint adds a water-resistant barrier to stucco and helps seal micro-cracks before they grow.
- **Watch for cracks after earthquakes or large temperature swings.** Even minor seismic activity or an unusually cold or hot season can open new cracks.
- **Schedule a stucco inspection every 5 to 10 years.** An experienced eye catches developing problems before they become expensive ones.

## Is Stucco Repair Tax Deductible?

Standard stucco repair for a primary residence is not tax deductible. However, if the damage was caused by a federally declared disaster, you may be able to claim a casualty loss deduction. The IRS ([IRS.gov](https://www.irs.gov)) provides guidance on casualty losses under IRS Publication 547.

If your stucco repair is part of a home office, it may be partially deductible as a business expense. And certain energy-efficiency improvements tied to exterior sealing could qualify under the Residential Clean Energy Credit. Talk to a tax professional if the repair is part of a larger improvement project.
