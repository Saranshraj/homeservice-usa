A new patio costs **$1,800 to $10,500** for a typical 200 to 400 square foot project, with most homeowners landing around **$4,200 to $5,800** fully installed. Concrete runs the cheapest at $6 to $20 per square foot, while natural stone and high-end pavers can push to $30 to $50 per square foot. Where you live matters almost as much as what material you choose: labor in California or New York runs 30 to 50 percent higher than the national average, while costs in the Midwest and South tend to fall below the midpoint.

If you are one of the 70 million US homeowners who uses their backyard regularly, you already know the difference a proper patio makes. Lawn chairs on bare grass are fine; a level, durable surface where your kids can ride bikes, you can grill without gravel in your shoes, and guests actually want to linger is a different thing entirely. This guide breaks down exactly what you will pay, what you can control, and how to get the job done right without getting taken for a ride.

## Patio Cost by Material

Material choice is the single biggest driver of your patio price. Here is a realistic look at what each option costs installed, including labor.

| Material | Cost per Sq Ft (Installed) | Best For | Lifespan |
|---|---|---|---|
| Poured concrete | $6 to $20 | Budget-friendly, large areas | 25 to 50 years |
| Stamped concrete | $12 to $28 | Looks of stone at lower cost | 25 to 50 years |
| Concrete pavers | $10 to $20 | DIY-friendly, repairable | 30 to 50 years |
| Brick pavers | $14 to $25 | Classic look, high durability | 25 to 40 years |
| Natural stone (flagstone, bluestone) | $18 to $50 | Premium aesthetics | 50+ years |
| Travertine | $20 to $45 | Upscale look, cooler surface | 30 to 50 years |
| Gravel/decomposed granite | $1 to $5 | Drainage, budget projects | 5 to 15 years |
| Composite decking (raised patio) | $25 to $60 | Sloped yards, raised surfaces | 25 to 35 years |

### Poured Concrete

Plain poured concrete is the most affordable hard surface. A 300 square foot concrete patio runs **$1,800 to $6,000** installed. Contractors pour a slab 4 inches thick over a compacted gravel base, which handles most residential foot traffic and outdoor furniture loads. The trade-off: cracking is nearly inevitable over time, especially in climates with freeze-thaw cycles (New England, the Midwest, the Mountain West). Hairline cracks do not affect function, but wider cracks eventually need patching.

### Stamped Concrete

Stamped concrete uses the same pour but the contractor presses texture patterns into the wet surface to mimic flagstone, brick, or wood grain. Color pigments get mixed into the pour or applied as a surface stain. A 300 square foot stamped patio runs **$3,600 to $8,400**. It looks striking when new. The honest downside: color fades without regular resealing every two to three years, and repairs that chip or crack rarely match the original color perfectly.

### Concrete Pavers

Individual precast concrete units set in a sand base are the sweet spot for most homeowners. They look better than plain concrete, cost less than natural stone, and are genuinely repairable: if a paver cracks, you pull it out and drop in a new one. A 300 square foot paver patio runs **$3,000 to $6,000** installed. Concrete pavers also handle freeze-thaw cycles better than poured slabs because individual units shift slightly without cracking the whole surface.

### Brick Pavers

Genuine fired clay bricks are a classic that never fully goes out of style. They are denser and harder than concrete pavers, handle heavy loads well, and the color is baked in rather than applied, so fading is less of an issue. Cost runs **$4,200 to $7,500** for a 300 square foot patio. Brick is particularly popular in the Mid-Atlantic and Southeast.

### Natural Stone (Flagstone, Bluestone, Slate)

Irregular flagstone or cut bluestone gives patios an organic, upscale look that concrete cannot convincingly replicate. The irregular joints planted with ground cover or polymeric sand look genuinely beautiful. The price reflects it: **$5,400 to $15,000** for a 300 square foot flagstone patio. Installation is labor-intensive because each piece has to be hand-fit. Natural stone is worth the money if you plan to stay in the home long term and value curb appeal and resale.

## Patio Cost by Size

Size multiplies everything. A contractor pricing a 100 square foot patio is not simply charging one-third the price of a 300 square foot patio, because mobilization, base prep, and equipment costs are spread over more material as the project grows.

## What Else Adds to the Cost

The base slab or paver field is just the start. Most patios also need one or more of these additions:

**Site preparation: $500 to $2,500.** Contractors must dig out and compact a gravel sub-base (typically 4 to 6 inches) before installing anything. Sloped yards that need grading add another $500 to $1,500. Tree roots, existing concrete removal, or poor drainage all push this number up.

**Concrete removal: $1 to $3 per square foot.** If you have an old slab to tear out, add $300 to $1,200 for most residential projects.

**Permits: $50 to $400.** Many municipalities require a permit for poured concrete slabs and some paver installations, particularly if the patio is attached to the house. Your contractor should pull this, but factor it into your budget.

**Retaining walls: $3,000 to $8,000+.** Sloped backyards often need a retaining wall to level the patio area. Costs vary widely by height and material. For a more detailed breakdown, see our [retaining wall cost guide](/blog/retaining-wall-cost).

**Steps: $700 to $2,500 per set.** If your back door sits above grade, you need steps down to the patio. Pre-cast concrete steps run cheaper; custom stone or brick steps cost more.

**Drainage: $500 to $2,000.** Patios that pool water or sit below the surrounding grade need French drains or channel drains to direct runoff away from the house.

**Lighting: $300 to $1,500.** Low-voltage landscape lighting adds ambiance and safety. Most homeowners add it as an upgrade when the contractor is already on-site.

**Seating walls and fire pit pads: $1,500 to $5,000+.** Built-in seating walls from brick or stone are a popular upgrade. A reinforced concrete pad for a fire pit runs $300 to $700 on its own.

## Patio Costs by Region

Labor and material costs vary significantly across the US. Here are realistic installed price ranges for a 300 square foot concrete paver patio by region:

| Region | Typical Installed Cost |
|---|---|
| Pacific (CA, OR, WA) | $5,500 to $9,500 |
| New England (MA, CT, NY, NJ) | $5,000 to $8,500 |
| Mountain West (CO, UT, AZ) | $4,500 to $7,500 |
| Mid-Atlantic (PA, VA, MD) | $4,000 to $7,000 |
| Midwest (IL, OH, MN, MO) | $3,500 to $6,500 |
| Southeast (FL, GA, TX, NC) | $3,200 to $6,000 |
| Great Plains (KS, NE, OK) | $2,800 to $5,500 |

Contractors in high cost-of-living states like California and New York charge more for labor, and some materials (especially natural stone) carry higher freight costs in rural areas. Texas and the Southeast have a dense enough contractor market that competition keeps prices reasonable. In Florida, moisture and humidity make stamped concrete a risky long-term choice: concrete pavers are generally the smarter call.

## DIY vs. Hiring a Pro

You can DIY a small gravel or paver patio. You should not DIY a poured concrete slab.

Poured concrete requires rented forms, a concrete pump or wheelbarrow crew, proper curing, and timing everything with the weather. A bad pour that cracks unevenly is worse than no patio at all, and fixing it costs as much as doing it right the first time. Leave poured and stamped concrete to professionals.

Concrete paver patios are genuinely DIY-friendly if you are physically able and patient. A 200 square foot paver patio done yourself costs roughly $1,000 to $2,000 in materials (pavers, gravel base, sand, edging, polymeric sand) compared to $4,000 to $6,000 installed by a contractor. The main risk: an improperly compacted base leads to settled, uneven pavers in two to three years. Rent a plate compactor from a home center for $80 to $120 per day. Do not skip this step.

The [NAHB (National Association of Home Builders)](https://www.nahb.org) notes that professionally installed patios return an average of 60 to 80 percent of their cost at resale, with natural stone and high-quality pavers at the top of that range.

## How to Find and Vet a Patio Contractor

Patio installation is not a licensed trade in most states the way electrical or plumbing work is, which means nearly anyone with a truck and a wheelbarrow can hang out a sign. This makes vetting especially important.

**What to look for:**
- At least 3 to 5 years in business with verifiable local references for patio projects specifically
- Proof of general liability insurance (ask for a certificate of insurance naming you as additional insured)
- Detailed written contract specifying materials (exact paver brand and product, concrete PSI, base depth), total price, payment schedule, and start and completion dates
- Warranty on workmanship, typically 1 to 2 years for most contractors

**What to watch out for:**
- Asking for full payment upfront before work begins. Legitimate contractors typically ask for 10 to 30 percent down and the balance on completion.
- Unusually low bids. If one estimate is 40 percent lower than the others, ask exactly what they are leaving out. Common shortcuts: skimping on base depth (should be 4 to 6 inches of compacted gravel, not 2 inches), using lower-grade pavers, skipping joint sand compaction.
- No written contract or vague scope of work.

## Do You Need a Permit?

The answer depends on your municipality and the type of patio. As a general rule:

- **Freestanding paver patios** at grade: permits often not required, but check with your local building department
- **Poured concrete slabs attached to the house**: permits commonly required in cities and suburbs
- **Raised patios or deck-patio combinations**: permits almost always required
- **Patios with drainage modifications or retaining walls over 30 inches**: permits required in most jurisdictions

Always verify with your local building department before starting work. Unpermitted improvements can cause problems when you sell, and some homeowners insurance policies exclude coverage for structures built without permits. The [HUD website (hud.gov)](https://www.hud.gov) has resources on local building departments for all 50 states.

## Maintenance Costs to Plan For

Your patio is not a one-time expense. Plan for these recurring costs:

**Concrete patio sealing: $150 to $400 every 2 to 3 years.** Sealer protects against staining, freeze-thaw damage, and UV fading. You can DIY this with a $50 to $80 product from a home center.

**Paver joint sand replacement: $100 to $300 every 5 to 10 years.** Polymeric sand in the joints washes out over time. Refilling and compacting it keeps pavers stable and prevents weeds.

**Power washing: $100 to $250 annually.** Removes algae, mildew, and stains. See our [pressure washing cost guide](/blog/pressure-washing-cost) for detail.

**Crack repair (concrete): $200 to $600 as needed.** Surface cracks in concrete can be filled with polyurethane or epoxy products. Structural cracks that widen need professional evaluation.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**How long does patio installation take?**
A 300 square foot concrete patio takes 1 to 2 days to pour and form, plus 7 to 10 days of curing before you can use it. A paver patio of the same size takes 2 to 4 days of installation and is usable immediately after completion.

**What is the cheapest patio option?**
Gravel or decomposed granite is cheapest at $300 to $1,500 for a 300 square foot area, but it shifts, tracks into the house, and is hard to use with outdoor furniture. For a hard surface, plain poured concrete at $1,800 to $6,000 is the most affordable.

**Does a patio increase home value?**
Yes. A well-done patio consistently ranks among the top outdoor improvements for return on investment. According to the [National Association of Realtors](https://www.nar.realtor) 2024 Remodeling Impact Report, an outdoor patio project recovers an average of 70 percent of its cost in resale value, with some markets recovering 100 percent or more.

**Should I use pavers or concrete?**
For most homeowners, pavers are the better long-term choice. They handle frost heave better, individual pieces are repairable, and they hold their appearance longer than concrete. Concrete is cheaper upfront but repair-heavy over 20 to 30 years. Stamped concrete splits the difference aesthetically but carries concrete's maintenance disadvantages.

**When is the best time to install a patio?**
Spring and fall are ideal in most of the US: temperatures are moderate (concrete needs to be poured between 50 and 90 degrees Fahrenheit), contractors are busy but not overbooked, and your patio will be ready for summer or next year's use. Avoid pouring concrete in very hot (above 90 F) or freezing conditions.
