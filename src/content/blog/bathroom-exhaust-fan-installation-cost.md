Installing a bathroom exhaust fan runs **$150 to $550** for most homeowners when you include both the fan unit and professional labor. A basic replacement fan in an existing location lands at $150 to $250, while adding a brand-new fan with a new ceiling box, dedicated circuit, and exterior vent runs $300 to $550 or more. If you go for a combination fan with a heater or Bluetooth speaker, expect $400 to $700 installed. Labor alone typically costs $75 to $200 depending on your region and the complexity of the job.

If your bathroom has no fan, or your existing one sounds like a jet engine and does nothing, you are not alone. Bathroom ventilation is one of the most overlooked systems in a home, yet it quietly protects your walls, ceiling, air quality, and even your family's health every single day.

## Why Bathroom Ventilation Actually Matters

Every shower you take dumps roughly a pint of water vapor into the air. Without a working exhaust fan, that moisture clings to walls, seeps into drywall, collects behind tile grout, and eventually feeds mold and mildew. Beyond the cosmetic damage, mold can trigger respiratory problems, allergies, and in serious cases, costly remediation.

The [Environmental Protection Agency](https://www.epa.gov/mold/mold-course-chapter-2) identifies moisture control as the single most important factor in preventing indoor mold growth. Bathrooms are ground zero for that moisture. A properly sized exhaust fan solves the problem at the source.

There is also a code angle. The [International Residential Code](https://codes.iccsafe.org/) (IRC), adopted by most US states, requires either a window or a mechanical ventilation fan in every bathroom. If your bathroom has no window, a working exhaust fan is not optional: it is the law.

Brands like Broan, Panasonic, and Delta are widely trusted by electricians. Panasonic in particular has a strong reputation for quiet, durable motors.

### Labor Costs

Labor is where costs diverge sharply. Here is what affects the electrician's bill:

**Simple replacement (existing fan, same location):** $75 to $150. The ductwork is already run, the wiring is in place, and the electrician just swaps the unit. This is a 1 to 2 hour job.

**New installation in a finished ceiling:** $200 to $400 in labor. Cutting into a finished ceiling, running new ductwork to an exterior vent, and wiring a new switch circuit is a multi-hour project. In some homes, the attic access makes this easier; in others, the electrician has to work from below.

**Adding a dedicated circuit:** If your bathroom only has one circuit and it is already near capacity, code may require or your electrician may recommend a dedicated 20-amp circuit. That adds $150 to $300 to the project.

**Exterior venting:** A bathroom fan must vent to the outside, not into the attic (a common mistake in older homes that causes serious moisture damage in the attic). If the fan currently vents into the attic, rerouting it adds $100 to $250 to the job.

## DIY vs. Hiring an Electrician

Here is the honest breakdown:

**DIY is reasonable if:**
- You are doing a direct swap of an existing fan in the same spot
- The wiring and ductwork are already in place
- You are comfortable turning off the breaker and working in an attic space
- Your local permit requirements allow homeowner electrical work

A straightforward swap takes about 45 minutes and is genuinely beginner-friendly. YouTube tutorials abound, and most fans come with clear instructions. The main safety rule: shut off the breaker, not just the switch, and confirm power is off with a non-contact voltage tester before touching any wires.

**Hire an electrician if:**
- You are adding a fan where there was none before
- You need new ductwork run to the exterior
- You are adding a new switch or circuit
- Your attic is not accessible or you are in a two-story home
- You are not comfortable with electrical work at any level

The [Consumer Product Safety Commission](https://www.cpsc.gov) notes that electrical failures and malfunctions are a leading cause of home fires. Improperly wired fans are a real risk, especially in the damp environment of a bathroom. When in doubt, call a licensed electrician.

**Always pull a permit for new installations.** Most jurisdictions require a permit for adding a new circuit or new electrical fixture. Your electrician should handle this. If they tell you a permit is not needed for brand-new electrical work, that is a red flag worth investigating with your local building department.

## How to Choose the Right Fan: CFM and Sone Ratings

Two numbers matter most when picking a bathroom exhaust fan:

**CFM (cubic feet per minute):** This measures how much air the fan moves. The [Home Ventilating Institute](https://www.hvi.org/) recommends a minimum of 1 CFM per square foot of bathroom floor space. So a 60-square-foot bathroom needs at least a 60 CFM fan. For bathrooms over 100 square feet, add 50 CFM per toilet, 50 CFM per shower, and 100 CFM per jetted tub.

**Sone rating:** This measures how loud the fan is. One sone equals roughly the noise of a quiet refrigerator. Anything under 1.0 sone is very quiet. Older budget fans often run at 3 to 4 sones, which is noticeably loud. For a master bath or any bedroom-adjacent bathroom, aim for 1.0 sone or lower.

[ENERGY STAR](https://www.energystar.gov/products/ventilating_fans) certifies bathroom fans that meet efficiency standards. ENERGY STAR-rated fans use at least 60% less energy than standard models, which translates to real savings on your electric bill over time. Look for the ENERGY STAR label when shopping.

## Features Worth Paying For

**Humidity sensor:** The fan turns on automatically when humidity spikes and turns off when the air is dry. You never have to remember to run it. For homeowners who consistently forget to turn on the fan (or have kids who do the same), this feature pays for itself in mold prevention within a year or two.

**Motion sensor:** The fan turns on when someone enters and runs for a set time after they leave. Good for households where people routinely forget.

**Timer switch:** Not a fan feature but a switch upgrade worth mentioning. Replacing the standard on/off switch with a countdown timer switch costs about $20 and ensures the fan runs long enough after a shower. This alone improves most bathrooms more than a fan upgrade does.

**LED light combo:** Most modern fan/light combos now include LED fixtures. The energy savings over a bathroom light used multiple times daily add up, and the bulbs last years.

**Combination heater:** In drafty older homes or cold climates, a fan with a built-in heater is genuinely appreciated. They typically produce 1500 watts of heat, enough to warm a small bathroom quickly on a cold morning.

## Signs Your Existing Fan Needs Replacing

- Noticeable condensation on the mirror that takes more than 20 minutes to clear after a shower
- Mold or mildew appearing regularly on the ceiling or grout, even after cleaning
- The fan runs but you cannot feel any suction at the grille
- Loud rattling, grinding, or squealing from the motor
- The fan was installed before 1990 (older fans lose efficiency dramatically over time)
- You can see daylight through the grille (the damper is stuck open, wasting conditioned air)

## Permits and Inspections

For a replacement fan with no new wiring, most jurisdictions do not require a permit. For any new installation or new circuit, a permit is almost certainly required. Unpermitted electrical work creates real problems when you sell your home: an inspector will note it, your buyer may demand it be brought up to code, and your homeowner's insurance could deny a claim tied to unpermitted work.

The [National Electrical Code](https://www.nfpa.org/codes-and-standards/nfpa-70-standard-for-the-installation-of-electrical-wiring-and-equipment) (NFPA 70), published by the National Fire Protection Association, governs electrical installations including bathroom fans. Your local building department enforces a version of this code. When in doubt, call your building department before starting work.

## Getting the Best Price

**Get three quotes.** Electrician pricing varies significantly even within the same city. Three quotes take a few phone calls and can save you $100 to $200.

**Ask about bundling.** If you have other small electrical work (a GFCI outlet, a light fixture swap, a dimmer switch), ask your electrician to price it all together. The trip charge is already paid.

**Buy the fan yourself.** Some electricians mark up materials significantly. Buying the fan at a home improvement store and supplying it to the electrician is perfectly acceptable and often saves $20 to $50.

**Check for utility rebates.** Some local utilities offer rebates on ENERGY STAR-certified ventilation fans. Check your utility's website or the [ENERGY STAR rebate finder](https://www.energystar.gov/rebate-finder) before purchasing.
