Fireplace installation costs **$1,000 to $10,000 or more** depending on the type, with the most popular choice, a gas fireplace insert, averaging **$3,000 to $5,500 fully installed**. A brand-new wood-burning fireplace with a masonry surround runs **$5,000 to $15,000** because it requires building a chimney. Electric fireplaces are the most affordable option at **$500 to $2,500 installed**, while high-end linear gas fireplaces with custom surrounds can easily exceed **$10,000**. Most homeowners adding a fireplace for the first time spend somewhere in the **$3,000 to $7,000 range** total.

If you are thinking about a fireplace this fall, you are in good company. Fireplace projects spike every October as homeowners realize they want that warmth and ambiance before the cold really sets in. The good news is that today's options are far more flexible and affordable than they were a decade ago. You do not need a full brick chimney to get a beautiful, functional fireplace, and some options qualify for federal energy efficiency tax credits that can soften the upfront cost considerably.

## Fireplace Installation Cost by Type

The single biggest factor in your total cost is the type of fireplace you choose. Here is an honest breakdown of what each type actually costs installed in 2026:

| Fireplace Type | Unit/Equipment Cost | Installation Cost | Total Installed |
|----------------|---------------------|-------------------|-----------------|
| Electric fireplace (wall mount) | $200 to $1,500 | $100 to $500 | $500 to $2,500 |
| Gas fireplace insert (into existing fireplace) | $1,500 to $4,500 | $500 to $1,500 | $2,500 to $6,500 |
| Gas fireplace (freestanding or direct-vent, no existing chimney) | $1,500 to $4,000 | $1,500 to $4,000 | $3,500 to $8,000 |
| Wood-burning fireplace insert | $1,200 to $3,500 | $500 to $1,500 | $2,000 to $5,500 |
| New masonry wood-burning fireplace (with chimney) | $3,500 to $8,000 | $4,000 to $10,000 | $8,000 to $20,000 |
| Prefab/factory-built wood-burning fireplace | $1,500 to $4,000 | $1,500 to $4,000 | $3,500 to $8,500 |
| Pellet stove (freestanding) | $1,500 to $4,000 | $500 to $1,500 | $2,500 to $6,000 |
| Two-sided (see-through) gas fireplace | $3,000 to $8,000 | $2,500 to $5,000 | $6,500 to $14,000 |
| Linear gas fireplace (custom) | $4,000 to $12,000 | $3,000 to $7,000 | $7,000 to $20,000+ |

**Note:** These costs assume permits and standard installation. A custom surround, tile, mantel, or decorative facade can add $500 to $5,000 or more on top of these figures.

## Breaking Down the Options: Which Type Is Right for You?

### Electric Fireplaces: The Easy Choice

Electric fireplaces are the simplest and cheapest to install, because they require no venting, no gas line, and no chimney. You plug them in, or have an electrician add a dedicated circuit, and they work. They produce realistic flame effects and some real heat (typically 4,000 to 5,000 BTUs), but they will not heat a large room on their own.

**Best for:** Renters, apartments, condos, or any homeowner who wants ambiance without the complexity of a vented unit.

**Honest limitation:** Most homeowners find electric fireplaces satisfying for ambiance but not as a primary heat source. If you want real heat output, a gas or wood unit is a better investment.

---

### Gas Fireplace Inserts: The Most Popular Choice

If you already have a wood-burning fireplace that you never use, a gas insert is almost certainly your best value. The insert slides into your existing firebox, connects to your gas line, and uses a liner in your existing chimney for venting. You get real flame, real heat (typically 20,000 to 40,000 BTUs), and a remote control.

The [U.S. Department of Energy](https://www.energy.gov/energysaver/gas-fireplaces) notes that gas inserts are significantly more efficient than open wood-burning fireplaces, which can actually draw warm air out of your home while burning.

**Best for:** Homeowners with an existing masonry fireplace who want to convert to gas for convenience and efficiency.

**Key cost driver:** Whether your existing chimney liner needs to be relined. If it does, budget an extra $900 to $3,500. A certified chimney sweep or NFI-certified gas fireplace technician should inspect the chimney before installation.

---

### New Gas Fireplaces (No Existing Chimney)

Adding a gas fireplace to a room that has never had one is more involved but very doable. Modern direct-vent gas fireplaces vent through a small co-axial pipe that exits through an exterior wall or the roof, so you do not need a full masonry chimney. This makes them practical for almost any exterior wall.

**Best for:** New construction, additions, or any room where you want to add a fireplace from scratch.

**Important:** Gas line extension adds $250 to $1,500 depending on distance from your existing gas line. A licensed plumber or gas fitter must do this work. Do not let anyone skip the permit for gas line work.

---

### Wood-Burning Fireplaces: The Classic Choice

A traditional masonry wood-burning fireplace is still the most coveted feature in many real estate markets, particularly in the Northeast and Mountain West. The problem is that building one from scratch requires constructing a chimney, which is the most labor-intensive and expensive part of the project.

The [National Fire Protection Association (NFPA 211)](https://www.nfpa.org/codes-and-standards/nfpa-211-standard-for-chimneys-flues-vents-solid-fuel-burning-appliances-and-cooking-appliances/content/211) sets the standards for chimney construction. A prefabricated (factory-built) wood-burning fireplace and zero-clearance metal chimney system offers a more affordable middle ground at $3,500 to $8,500 installed.

**Best for:** Homeowners who love the smell and crackling of real wood and are willing to invest in maintenance.

**Honest consideration:** Wood-burning fireplaces require annual chimney cleaning ([$100 to $350 per year](/blog/chimney-cleaning-cost/)), seasoned firewood ($200 to $500 per cord), and more attention than gas or electric. They are more of a lifestyle choice than a convenience appliance.

## What Drives the Final Price

Beyond the type of unit, several factors explain why two neighbors can get dramatically different quotes for what looks like the same project:

**Gas line work:** If your gas meter or line is on the opposite side of the house from where you want the fireplace, running a new branch line is expensive. A short 15-foot run might cost $250 to $400; a 60-foot run through finished walls can be $1,000 to $1,500 or more.

**Chimney or venting installation:** A new masonry chimney costs $3,000 to $12,000 on its own. A prefab metal chimney for a factory-built wood fireplace runs $1,500 to $4,500. Direct-vent pipe for a gas unit is usually $500 to $2,000.

**Surround, mantel, and hearth:** The fireplace unit itself is just the starting point. A custom mantel, tile surround, or stone hearth can add significantly to your budget. Many homeowners spend as much on the surround as on the unit itself.

**Permits:** Fireplace installations almost universally require a building permit. Expect $75 to $350 depending on your municipality. Never skip the permit: an unpermitted fireplace can void your homeowners insurance and create problems when you sell.

**Electrical work:** Gas and wood fireplaces with blowers, thermostats, or ignition systems may need a dedicated 120V circuit. Budget $150 to $400 for a local electrician to add an outlet if one is not already present nearby.

## State-by-State Cost Differences

Labor rates, permit fees, and contractor availability vary significantly across the country. Here is what homeowners in different regions typically pay for a gas fireplace insert installation (mid-tier unit):

| Region | Typical Installed Cost (Gas Insert) |
|--------|--------------------------------------|
| Northeast (MA, NY, NJ, CT, PA) | $4,000 to $7,500 |
| Midwest (IL, OH, MI, MN, WI) | $3,000 to $5,500 |
| South (TX, GA, FL, NC, VA) | $2,800 to $5,000 |
| Mountain West (CO, UT, AZ, NV) | $3,200 to $6,000 |
| Pacific Coast (CA, WA, OR) | $3,500 to $7,000 |

California, Massachusetts, and New York consistently run highest due to labor costs and stricter permitting requirements. Southern states often come in lower, partly because fireplace demand is lower and competition keeps contractors' prices in check.

## Tax Credits and Rebates in 2026

This is one area where good planning can put real money back in your pocket.

**Federal tax credit (Inflation Reduction Act):** Biomass stoves and boilers (pellet stoves that meet an 75% efficiency standard) qualify for the Energy Efficient Home Improvement Credit of 30% of the cost, up to $2,000 per year. Check the current credit terms at [IRS.gov Energy Credits](https://www.irs.gov/credits-deductions/energy-efficient-home-improvement-credit).

**Gas fireplaces:** Standard gas fireplaces and inserts do not currently qualify for the 30% credit, but high-efficiency heat pump equivalents do. If you are replacing a wood-burning or electric heat source with a high-efficiency gas fireplace, ask your tax professional about what may qualify.

**State and utility rebates:** Many state energy offices and gas utilities offer rebates for efficient heating appliances. Check your state's energy office or visit the [ENERGY STAR rebate finder](https://www.energystar.gov/rebate-finder) for what may be available in your area.

## How to Get the Best Price: Practical Tips

**Get three quotes minimum.** Fireplace installation pricing varies more than almost any other home service. A single quote can be significantly off from the market rate, in either direction. Three quotes give you a real baseline.

**Ask about off-season pricing.** Fireplace contractors get very busy from September through December. If you can plan ahead and schedule for February or March, some contractors will offer lower prices during their slow season.

**Understand what is included.** Ask each contractor to itemize the quote: unit, liner/venting, gas line extension (if any), surround, mantel, permit, and cleanup. An apples-to-apples comparison is only possible when the quotes cover the same scope.

**Check licensing and insurance.** Gas fireplace work should be done by a licensed contractor who is also certified by the National Fireplace Institute (NFI). Ask to see the NFI certification card. For chimney work, look for a Chimney Safety Institute of America (CSIA) certified sweep. Our [contractor vetting guide](/blog/how-to-find-good-contractor/) walks through the full vetting process.

**Verify the permit is pulled.** Your contractor should pull the permit, not you. If they suggest you get it yourself or ask you to pull it "to save time," that is a red flag. Permits protect you if something goes wrong.

## Fireplace vs. Space Heater vs. Heat Pump: What Actually Heats Better?

A lot of homeowners get a fireplace for heat, but a realistic comparison helps set expectations:

| Heating Option | BTU Output | Coverage Area | Annual Operating Cost |
|----------------|-----------|---------------|-----------------------|
| Electric fireplace | 4,000 to 5,000 BTU | 400 sq ft max | $100 to $250 |
| Gas fireplace insert | 15,000 to 40,000 BTU | 600 to 1,500 sq ft | $200 to $600 |
| Wood-burning fireplace | Variable, often inefficient | 300 to 800 sq ft effectively | $200 to $500 (wood cost) |
| Pellet stove | 30,000 to 60,000 BTU | 1,000 to 2,500 sq ft | $300 to $700 |
| Mini-split heat pump | 9,000 to 36,000 BTU | 250 to 1,500 sq ft | $200 to $600 |

The [U.S. Environmental Protection Agency (EPA)](https://www.epa.gov/burnwise) recommends modern EPA-certified wood-burning or pellet-burning appliances for anyone using biomass for heat, both for efficiency and for indoor air quality reasons. Older open fireplaces actually pull conditioned air out of your house through the chimney even when you are not burning a fire, which can increase your heating bill rather than reduce it.

## Safety Requirements You Cannot Skip

Fireplaces and heating appliances are the leading cause of home fires in the US, according to the [National Fire Protection Association](https://www.nfpa.org). Before installation is complete, verify the following:

- **Carbon monoxide detectors:** Required within 15 feet of any combustion appliance in most states. The [Consumer Product Safety Commission (CPSC)](https://www.cpsc.gov) recommends a CO detector on every level of your home.
- **Clearances:** Combustible materials must be kept the required distance from any fireplace opening or heat-producing surface. Your inspector will verify these during the permit inspection.
- **Annual maintenance:** Even a gas fireplace needs an annual inspection by an NFI-certified technician to check the venting, gas connections, and firebox for cracks or deterioration.
- **Creosote management:** Wood-burning fireplaces require annual cleaning by a CSIA-certified chimney sweep. Creosote buildup is the primary cause of chimney fires.

## Questions to Ask Before Signing a Contract

1. Are you NFI-certified for gas appliance installation? (or CSIA-certified for chimney and wood-burning work?)
2. Will you pull the building permit?
3. What warranty do you offer on labor, separate from the manufacturer warranty on the unit?
4. Does this quote include the gas line extension and a new chimney liner, if needed?
5. What happens if you open the wall and find a problem that adds cost? How do you handle that?
6. Can you provide references from fireplace installations in the past 12 months?
