Replacing a single circuit breaker typically costs **$150 to $300**, including labor and parts. If your panel needs a full replacement, expect to pay **$1,500 to $4,000 or more**, depending on your home's size and your location. These aren't fun numbers to hear, but catching a faulty breaker early is far cheaper than dealing with an electrical fire or a failed home inspection. If your breaker keeps tripping, feels warm to the touch, or smells burnt, do not wait: get an electrician out quickly.

## What Affects Circuit Breaker Replacement Cost?

A few key factors push that number up or down:

**Type of breaker:** A standard 15-amp single-pole breaker costs $5 to $20 for the part. A GFCI (ground fault circuit interrupter) breaker runs $40 to $80, and an AFCI (arc fault circuit interrupter) breaker costs $20 to $70. If you have a specialty or older panel that uses proprietary breakers, parts alone can run $100 or more.

**Labor rates:** Electricians charge $50 to $130 per hour depending on your region. The job itself typically takes one to two hours, so labor runs $100 to $260 for a straightforward single breaker swap.

**Permits:** Some states and municipalities require a permit for electrical panel work, even a breaker swap. Permit fees range from $50 to $150. Your electrician should handle pulling the permit; if they tell you a permit isn't necessary when it clearly is, that's a red flag.

**Age and condition of your panel:** If your panel is old, corroded, or already shows signs of overheating, an electrician may recommend replacing the whole thing rather than patching individual breakers. That conversation is worth having honestly, because a compromised panel is a genuine safety risk.

**Your location:** Costs in California, New York, and Massachusetts run 20% to 40% higher than national averages. In the Midwest and South, prices tend to be lower.

Prices above reflect national averages for 2026. If you are in a high-cost state like California or Massachusetts, add 20% to 35%.

## When a Single Breaker Won't Fix the Problem

Sometimes a breaker fails because it's just old, which happens after 15 to 25 years of service. But sometimes it's a symptom of something bigger:

**Overloaded panel:** If your home runs on a 100-amp service panel and you've added a hot tub, EV charger, or major appliances since it was installed, you may need to upgrade to 200-amp service. That costs $1,300 to $3,000, but it's the right long-term fix.

**Multiple breakers failing:** If two or more breakers are going bad at the same time, your panel may be deteriorating. Full panel replacement runs $1,500 to $4,000 for most homes, plus $1,000 to $2,000 more if the electrical service needs upgrading.

**Recalled panels:** Certain panel brands have known safety issues and are considered fire hazards. Federal Pacific Electric (FPE) Stab-Lok panels, Zinsco panels, and some Pushmatic panels fall into this category. If your home has one of these, the U.S. Consumer Product Safety Commission (CPSC) recommends replacement regardless of whether the breakers are tripping. See [cpsc.gov](https://www.cpsc.gov) for guidance on recalled electrical products.

## Signs You Need a Breaker Replaced Right Now

You should call an electrician today, not "eventually," if you notice:

- A breaker that trips repeatedly even after you reduce the load on that circuit
- A breaker that won't reset or won't stay in the "on" position
- A burning smell near your electrical panel
- Visible scorch marks, melted plastic, or discoloration on or around any breaker
- A breaker that feels hot or warm to the touch
- Flickering or dimming lights tied to a specific circuit
- Buzzing or crackling sounds coming from the panel

These signs mean the breaker is not just annoying, it may be unsafe. Electrical fires are the leading cause of home fires in the United States, according to the National Fire Protection Association ([nfpa.org](https://www.nfpa.org)).

## DIY Circuit Breaker Replacement: Honest Talk

You will find plenty of YouTube videos showing homeowners replacing their own breakers. Technically, it's not impossible if you are careful and comfortable working near high-voltage equipment. But here's the honest version:

The main breaker does NOT cut power to the service entrance wires. Those wires coming in from the utility are always live, even with everything switched off. Touching them is fatal. Professional electricians are trained to work around this safely. Most homeowners are not.

Beyond the safety issue, many jurisdictions require a licensed electrician to perform panel work by code, and unpermitted electrical work can cause problems when you sell your home or file an insurance claim.

Our recommendation: DIY is not worth it here. The labor cost is relatively low ($100 to $200), and the professional does the job safely, pulls the permit if needed, and can spot any other problems while they're in the panel.

## How to Keep Circuit Breaker Replacement Costs Down

A few things you can do to get a fair price:

**Get at least three quotes.** Electrician pricing varies widely, and a 30-minute phone call to compare quotes can save you $75 to $150 easily.

**Ask if your panel needs anything else while they're there.** It costs less to address multiple issues in one service call than to have an electrician return a second time.

**Check for home warranty coverage.** Some home warranty plans cover electrical panel components. Review your contract before paying out of pocket. See our guide on [whether a home warranty is worth it](/blog/home-warranty-worth-it) for help evaluating your policy.

**Bundle with an inspection.** If you haven't had an electrical inspection in several years, bundling it with the breaker replacement is usually a small add-on cost and gives you peace of mind about the rest of the panel.

## What to Expect on the Day of Service

A reputable electrician will:

1. Turn off the main breaker and verify circuits are de-energized before opening the panel
2. Identify the failing breaker and note the brand, amperage, and pole configuration
3. Source a compatible replacement (they typically carry common types on their truck)
4. Install the new breaker, verify it seats correctly, and test the circuit
5. Document the work and provide you with a receipt noting the breaker type and amperage

The whole process usually takes 60 to 90 minutes for a single breaker swap. If they find other issues inside the panel, expect that timeline to extend and get any additional work quoted in writing before they proceed.

## How to Find and Hire a Licensed Electrician

Your state's electrical licensing board is the best place to verify that a contractor holds a valid license. Licenses for electricians are typically issued at the state level, not federal. The National Association of Home Builders maintains a directory of contractors at [nahb.org](https://www.nahb.org).

When calling for quotes, ask:
- Are you licensed and insured in my state?
- Will you pull a permit for this work if required?
- What brand of replacement breaker do you plan to use, and is it compatible with my panel?
- What is your hourly rate, and do you charge a service call fee?

A contractor who is vague about permits or pushes you to skip one is a contractor to avoid. For more guidance, see our full article on [how to find a good contractor](/blog/how-to-find-good-contractor).
