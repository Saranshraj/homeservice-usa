A professional home energy audit costs **$150 to $800**, with most homeowners across the US paying **$300 to $500** for a thorough assessment. If your utility company offers a subsidized or free audit, you could pay as little as $0 to $100. The audit typically takes 2 to 4 hours and pinpoints exactly where your home is losing energy and costing you money every month.

If your energy bills feel unreasonably high or your home never seems to stay comfortable, a home energy audit is one of the smartest investments you can make before spending thousands on upgrades. This guide breaks down what audits cost, what they cover, and how to get your money back through rebates and tax credits.

---

## What Is a Home Energy Audit?

A home energy audit is a professional assessment of how your home uses energy and where it loses it. A certified energy auditor walks through your home with specialized equipment, checks your insulation, heating and cooling systems, windows, doors, appliances, and air sealing, and delivers a detailed report showing exactly where your money is going.

Think of it like a checkup for your house. Just as a doctor orders tests before recommending treatment, an energy audit diagnoses the real problems before you invest in upgrades.

There are two main types of audits:

**Basic walkthrough audit:** A visual inspection with limited testing. Often offered free or low-cost by utilities. Good for identifying obvious problems.

**Comprehensive diagnostic audit:** Uses blower door tests, infrared cameras, and combustion safety testing to find hidden air leaks and insulation gaps. This is the type most homeowners benefit from most.

## What Affects the Cost?

Several factors push the price up or down:

**Home size:** Larger homes take longer to assess. Expect auditors to charge $0.10 to $0.20 per square foot in many markets.

**Geographic location:** Audits tend to cost more in the Northeast and West Coast, where energy costs are higher and the market supports premium pricing. Southern states often have lower audit fees.

**Level of testing:** A basic visual walk-through costs far less than a full blower door test with infrared imaging.

**Auditor certification:** Auditors certified through the Building Performance Institute (BPI) or RESNET/HERS raters typically charge more but deliver higher quality reports and qualify you for more rebate programs.

**Utility rebates:** Many utility companies cover all or most of the cost if you schedule through their program. Check with your local electric and gas provider before paying full price.

## What Does a Professional Energy Audit Include?

When you hire a certified auditor, here is what they typically do:

**Blower door test:** A large fan is mounted in your front door to depressurize your home. This reveals exactly how much air is leaking in and out, measured in air changes per hour. The Department of Energy says most older homes leak at a rate of 1 to 2 air changes per hour, much higher than the recommended 0.35 for newer homes.

**Infrared camera scan:** An infrared (thermal imaging) camera shows exactly where insulation is missing or thin and where air is leaking through walls, ceilings, and floors. This is one of the most valuable parts of a comprehensive audit because it finds problems invisible to the naked eye.

**HVAC inspection:** The auditor checks your furnace, air conditioner, and ductwork for efficiency, safety, and proper sizing. Leaky ducts can waste 20 to 30 percent of the air your HVAC system pushes through them, according to the Department of Energy.

**Insulation check:** Attic, wall, and basement insulation levels are measured and compared to your climate zone recommendations from ENERGY STAR and the International Energy Conservation Code.

**Window and door assessment:** Air leaks around frames are checked with a smoke pencil or infrared camera.

**Appliance and lighting review:** Your water heater, refrigerator, dishwasher, and lighting are assessed for efficiency.

**Combustion safety testing:** Gas appliances are tested for carbon monoxide risks and proper venting.

At the end, you receive a detailed written report ranking recommended upgrades by their estimated return on investment.

## Federal Tax Credits for Energy Upgrades

Here is the good news: after your audit recommends specific upgrades, many of them qualify for federal tax credits under the Inflation Reduction Act. The IRS allows homeowners to claim:

- **30% tax credit** on insulation and air sealing improvements, up to $1,200 per year
- **30% tax credit** on heat pump installations, up to $2,000 per year
- **30% tax credit** on new windows and doors (up to $600 for windows, $500 for doors)
- **30% tax credit** on efficient water heaters, up to $600 per year

The Residential Clean Energy Credit covers solar panels, battery storage, and heat pump water heaters at 30 percent with no annual dollar cap through 2032.

You must use qualified equipment and file IRS Form 5695 to claim these credits. See the IRS website for the full eligibility rules and current credit amounts.

## Utility Rebates and State Programs

Beyond federal credits, many states and utility companies offer rebates that can cover a large portion of upgrade costs after an audit. Here are some examples of what is available:

**Weatherization Assistance Program (WAP):** The Department of Energy runs this federally funded program that provides free energy audits and weatherization upgrades for income-eligible homeowners. If you qualify based on income, you could get thousands of dollars in improvements at no cost.

**ENERGY STAR rebate finder:** The ENERGY STAR rebate finder at energystar.gov lets you search by zip code for local rebates on appliances, HVAC systems, and insulation.

**State-specific programs:** Massachusetts MassSave program covers 75 to 100 percent of insulation costs and offers 0 percent interest loans for other upgrades. New York's Assisted Home Performance program covers up to $10,000 in energy upgrades for qualifying homeowners. California's Energy Upgrade California offers rebates up to $3,000 for qualifying improvements.

Check your state energy office and your local utility company for what is available in your area before scheduling an audit. In many cases, scheduling through a utility-sponsored program instead of independently can save you $200 to $400 on the audit itself.

## The Return on Investment: Is It Worth It?

Let's be direct: a home energy audit almost always pays for itself, often within a few years.

The typical US household spends about $2,000 per year on energy according to the Department of Energy. An audit commonly identifies opportunities to reduce that by 15 to 30 percent, which means **$300 to $600 in annual savings**.

If you pay $400 for an audit and then implement $2,000 in recommended upgrades (air sealing, attic insulation, and a programmable thermostat), you might save $500 per year. That is a payback period of under 5 years, plus the home becomes more comfortable immediately.

The upgrades most often recommended after an audit, and their typical paybacks:

| Upgrade | Average Cost | Estimated Annual Savings | Payback Period |
|---|---|---|---|
| Air sealing (DIY) | $50 to $200 | $100 to $300 | Under 1 year |
| Attic insulation | $1,500 to $3,500 | $200 to $600 | 3 to 8 years |
| Smart thermostat | $150 to $300 | $100 to $200 | 1 to 2 years |
| Duct sealing | $300 to $1,000 | $150 to $400 | 2 to 5 years |
| New windows | $300 to $900 per window | $30 to $70 per window | 10 to 30 years |

Notice that new windows almost never have a strong financial payback on their own. An audit will show you what actually moves the needle in your specific home.

## How to Find a Qualified Energy Auditor

Not all energy auditors are equal. Here is how to find someone you can trust:

**Look for BPI or RESNET certification.** The Building Performance Institute (BPI) and RESNET both certify home energy professionals to consistent national standards. Certified auditors are required to follow a proven testing protocol. You can search for BPI-certified contractors at bpi.org and RESNET-certified raters at resnet.us.

**Start with your utility company.** Many utilities maintain lists of approved contractors who use consistent methods and qualify you for their rebates. This is often the fastest path to a quality audit at a fair price.

**Ask for a written report.** A professional audit should always result in a prioritized written report with estimated savings for each recommendation. If an auditor cannot commit to a written deliverable, keep looking.

**Get at least two quotes.** Audit prices vary widely. Two to three quotes help you understand what is standard in your market.

**Check reviews.** Look for reviews that mention the auditor being thorough, explaining findings clearly, and following up with a useful report. Yelp, Google, and Angi are all reasonable places to start.

**Avoid conflict of interest.** Some contractors offer "free audits" and then pressure you to hire them for the recommended work. A truly independent auditor does not sell the upgrades. If they do both, verify they offer multiple contractor options and are not steering you to inflate their own sales.

## Questions to Ask Before Scheduling

Before you book an audit, ask:

- Are you BPI or RESNET certified?
- Will you perform a blower door test and infrared scan?
- What does the written report include and how long will I have it?
- Do you work with my utility company's rebate program?
- How long will the audit take?
- What is included in your fee?

## DIY Energy Audit: A Starting Point

If you are not ready to hire a professional, you can do a basic self-assessment first. Walk your home and:

- Hold your hand near electrical outlets and switch plates on exterior walls on a cold day. Drafts are common here.
- Check your attic insulation depth. Recommended levels are R-38 to R-60 in most US climate zones.
- Look at your energy bills from the past year. Compare your usage (kWh for electricity, therms for gas) to similar homes in your area through your utility's comparison tool.
- Check your HVAC filter. A clogged filter reduces efficiency significantly.
- Look for visible gaps around pipes and wires where they penetrate exterior walls.

The US Department of Energy offers a free online Home Energy Saver tool at energy.gov that can estimate your home's energy use and the savings potential of common upgrades without a formal audit.

However, a DIY walk-through cannot replace the blower door test and infrared imaging that a professional audit provides. You will likely miss the biggest problems, which are often hidden inside walls and ceilings.

## When Should You Get a Home Energy Audit?

Consider scheduling an audit if:

- Your energy bills have increased significantly with no obvious cause
- Your home feels drafty or has rooms that are hard to heat or cool
- Your HVAC system runs constantly without reaching the set temperature
- You are buying an older home, especially one built before 1980
- You are planning a major renovation and want to address energy issues at the same time
- You want to reduce your carbon footprint and are not sure where to start

Fall and winter are actually ideal times to schedule an audit. Cold weather makes air leaks and insulation problems much easier to detect with infrared imaging, and you will have time to make improvements before the peak heating season.
