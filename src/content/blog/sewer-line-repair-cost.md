Sewer line repair costs between **$1,500 and $5,000** for most US homeowners dealing with a single crack, root intrusion, or partial blockage in 2026. A full sewer line replacement runs significantly more: **$3,000 to $25,000** depending on the length of pipe, the repair method, and where you live. The national average for a complete sewer line replacement sits around **$7,500 to $12,000**. If you are facing this repair right now, the most important thing to know is that trenchless repair methods can save you **$3,000 to $10,000** compared to traditional dig-and-replace, and they are now available in most US markets.

For general plumbing labor rates, see our [plumber cost guide](/blog/how-much-does-a-plumber-cost/). If frequent clogs led you here, check whether the real culprit might be your [water heater](/blog/signs-water-heater-failing/) or a slow drain issue rather than the main line itself before committing to a major repair.

## Why Sewer Line Problems Are Among the Most Stressful Home Repairs

If you have ever had sewage backing up into your bathtub, or noticed a mysterious swampy patch in your yard that smells faintly of something terrible, you already know this feeling. Sewer line problems do not give much warning, and they escalate fast. What starts as a slow drain can become a full sewage backup inside your home within days.

The good news: technology has genuinely improved in this space. Trenchless sewer repair methods introduced over the past two decades mean that in many cases, a licensed plumber can rehabilitate your sewer line without excavating your entire yard, driveway, or landscaping. Whether that option is available to you depends on the condition of your pipe, but it is worth knowing about before you get quotes.

## Sewer Line Repair Cost by Method

The repair method is the single biggest driver of your final cost. Here are the four main approaches, from least to most invasive.

| Repair Method | Average Cost | What It Fixes | Disruption Level |
|---|---|---|---|
| Hydro-jetting (cleaning) | $300 to $600 | Grease buildup, soft blockages | Very low |
| Camera inspection only | $250 to $800 | Diagnosis only | None |
| Spot repair (traditional) | $1,500 to $4,000 | Single crack or section | Low to moderate |
| Trenchless pipe lining (CIPP) | $3,000 to $12,000 | Cracks, root intrusion, minor corrosion | Low |
| Trenchless pipe bursting | $3,000 to $10,000 | Heavily damaged or collapsed pipe | Low |
| Traditional full replacement | $6,000 to $25,000 | Collapsed, severely corroded, or outdated pipe | High (full excavation) |

**Hydro-jetting** uses high-pressure water to blast clogs and grease out of your sewer line. It is not a repair, but it is the first thing a good plumber will try. If your problem is a grease or soap buildup rather than a structural issue, $400 can solve what you feared would be a $10,000 job.

**Trenchless pipe lining (CIPP)** inserts a flexible liner coated in epoxy resin into the existing damaged pipe. The liner is inflated and cured in place, creating a new pipe inside the old one. Your yard stays intact. The new liner typically carries a **50-year warranty** and resists root intrusion better than original clay or cast iron pipe.

**Trenchless pipe bursting** threads a bursting head through the damaged pipe, fracturing it outward while simultaneously pulling a new HDPE pipe into place. Like pipe lining, it leaves your yard largely undisturbed. It works well when the old pipe is too deteriorated for lining.

**Traditional replacement** (open-cut excavation) is still sometimes necessary when the pipe has collapsed, shifted significantly, or lies under a structure. Expect your driveway, landscaping, or hardscaping to be disturbed. Budget separately for restoration of those surfaces if needed.

## Cost Per Linear Foot

Sewer lines typically run **50 to 150 feet** from your home's foundation to the municipal connection at the street. Cost per linear foot varies by method.

| Method | Cost Per Linear Foot |
|---|---|
| Trenchless pipe bursting | $60 to $200 |
| Trenchless pipe lining (CIPP) | $80 to $250 |
| Traditional open-cut replacement | $50 to $250 |
| Spot repair (per section) | $100 to $400 |

To estimate your project: measure the approximate distance from where your main drain exits the house to the street (or ask a plumber to camera-inspect and measure). A 75-foot run with trenchless lining at $150 per foot would run around $11,250. A traditional replacement on the same run might be $7,500 to $18,750 depending on depth, soil conditions, and local labor.

## What Adds to the Cost

Several factors can push your quote higher than the baseline numbers above.

**Pipe material and age:** Cast iron pipe (common in homes built before 1960) corrodes and fails differently than clay pipe or PVC. Trenchless lining is often still possible, but the assessment takes more care. Orangeburg pipe (a pressed-paper product common from the 1940s to 1970s) is so deteriorated that it almost always requires full replacement.

**Depth of the pipe:** Deeper pipes require more excavation time and shoring if traditional methods are used. Pipes deeper than 8 feet add meaningfully to labor costs.

**Access to the pipe:** A pipe under your driveway, a concrete patio, or a mature tree root system all complicate the job. Restoring a concrete driveway after excavation can add **$2,000 to $8,000** to your total project cost. Trenchless methods avoid this.

**Permits:** Most municipalities require a permit for sewer line work, which your plumber should pull. Permit costs range from **$100 to $800** depending on your jurisdiction. Any licensed plumber should include this in their quote; be cautious of contractors who skip the permit to cut costs.

**Soil conditions:** Rocky soil, clay-heavy soil, or a high water table all slow excavation and add cost.

**Tree root damage:** Root intrusion is one of the most common causes of sewer line problems, particularly in homes with mature oak, willow, or maple trees. Trenchless lining handles minor root intrusion well. Severe root infiltration that has crushed or displaced the pipe may require full replacement.

## Does Homeowners Insurance Cover Sewer Line Repair?

This is one of the most common questions homeowners ask, and the answer is frustrating: **standard homeowners insurance typically does not cover sewer line repair** if the cause is age, wear and tear, tree roots, or general deterioration. These are considered maintenance issues, not sudden accidents.

However, there are situations where coverage may apply:

- **Sudden and accidental damage:** If a covered peril (like a collapse from a falling tree or ground shifting from a named storm) directly caused the sewer line damage, your standard policy may cover it.
- **Service line coverage add-ons:** Many insurance companies now offer a "service line protection" endorsement or rider for an extra **$5 to $15 per month**. This covers the repair or replacement of buried utility lines, including sewer lines, water supply lines, and electrical conduits.
- **Home warranty:** Some home warranty plans include sewer line coverage. Read the fine print carefully. The [home warranty industry](https://www.hud.gov) is heavily regulated at the state level, and exclusions vary widely.

The [National Association of Insurance Commissioners](https://www.naic.org) recommends reviewing your policy declarations page and calling your insurer before work begins. A quick call can confirm whether any coverage applies and whether you need to use an approved contractor.

## What Every Homeowner Should Know Before Calling a Plumber

**Start with a camera inspection.** Before any plumber recommends a repair method, they should run a sewer camera through your line. This takes about 30 to 60 minutes and costs **$250 to $800**. Some plumbers charge separately; others include it in the quote. The camera footage tells you exactly what is wrong, where the problem is, and whether trenchless repair is viable. Never agree to a major repair without seeing the camera footage.

**Get at least three quotes.** Sewer line work pricing can vary by 50 percent or more between contractors for the same job. A quote of $15,000 from one company and $7,500 from another for the same trenchless pipe lining job is not unusual. Always compare quotes that specify the same method and scope.

**Ask about the warranty.** Quality trenchless lining carries a 25- to 50-year warranty. Traditional pipe replacement should be warrantied for at least 1 to 2 years on labor and the manufacturer's warranty on pipe materials. Get this in writing.

**Verify licensing.** Sewer line work requires a licensed plumber in virtually every US state. Verify your contractor's license through your state's plumbing licensing board. The [National Association of Home Builders](https://www.nahb.org) maintains a directory of licensed contractors by state. Unlicensed sewer work can result in failed inspections, fines, and liability if the repair causes problems downstream.

**Understand the municipal boundary.** Your responsibility for the sewer line typically ends at the point it connects to the public main in the street. From there, your municipality is responsible. If a plumber's camera finds that the problem is at or beyond that connection point, contact your local public works department before spending money on private repairs.

## Signs Your Sewer Line Needs Attention Now

Do not wait for a backup inside your home. These early warning signs give you time to act before the situation becomes an emergency.

- **Multiple slow drains at once:** If your kitchen sink, bathroom sink, and tub are all draining slowly at the same time, the problem is downstream in the main line, not in individual branch lines.
- **Gurgling sounds from drains or toilet:** Air trapped in a partially blocked sewer line creates gurgling when you run water elsewhere in the house.
- **Sewage odor in basement or yard:** A properly functioning sewer line is completely sealed. Any sewage smell is a sign of a crack, break, or unsealed joint.
- **Lush patches of grass over your sewer line:** Sewage leaking into the soil is a fertilizer for grass. An unusually green strip across your yard is often the first visible sign of a leaking sewer line.
- **Wet or soft spots in your yard:** Saturated ground directly above a buried pipe, without a recent rainfall explanation, points to a leak.
- **Repeated clogs in the same drain:** One clog can be debris. Three or more clogs in the same line within a year suggest a structural problem.
- **Home is 40-plus years old with original plumbing:** Clay and cast iron sewer pipes have a typical service life of 50 to 75 years. If your home was built before 1985 and has never had sewer work, a camera inspection is a wise investment.

## Helpful Assistance Programs

Several programs can help lower-income homeowners offset sewer repair costs.

The [U.S. Department of Housing and Urban Development](https://www.hud.gov) administers the Community Development Block Grant program, which many cities and counties use to fund low-interest loans or grants for critical home repairs including sewer infrastructure. Contact your local HUD office or city housing department to ask what is available in your area.

The [EPA WaterSense program](https://www.epa.gov/watersense) can point you to local utility rebates for sewer-related repairs that reduce inflow and infiltration into municipal systems. Some utilities pay homeowners directly for fixing private laterals that leak stormwater into the sewer system.

State emergency home repair programs also exist in most states. Search your state's housing finance agency website for "emergency repair assistance" or "critical home repair grants."
