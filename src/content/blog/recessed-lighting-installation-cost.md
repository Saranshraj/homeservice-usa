Installing recessed lighting costs **$125 to $300 per fixture**, all-in with labor, for a retrofit LED project in an existing home. A typical living room with 6 lights runs **$750 to $1,800**. If you are doing new construction or need new wiring runs, costs climb to **$200 to $450 per fixture**, since the electrician is doing more work inside your walls and ceiling. These ranges come from licensed electricians across the country, and your final number depends mostly on where you live, how many fixtures you want, and what is already in your ceiling.

If you have been looking at those sleek, flush lights in model homes and thinking your house could look like that too, you are not alone. Recessed lighting is one of the most popular electrical upgrades homeowners ask for, and for good reason: it opens up a room, eliminates the dated ceiling fan light that came with the house, and dramatically improves how your home photographs for resale. Here is everything you need to know before you call an electrician.

## What Drives the Price Up or Down

### 1. Retrofit vs. New Install

This is the single biggest factor. A **retrofit LED kit** uses your existing ceiling holes (if you are replacing old can lights) or requires minimal new holes. The electrician is not cutting into walls or fishing wire through finished ceilings. Labor for a retrofit job runs **1 to 2 hours per fixture**, depending on access.

A **new installation** means the electrician needs to run new wire from your panel or an existing junction box, cut new holes in the ceiling, and in many cases work around insulation and vapor barriers. This easily doubles the labor time per fixture.

### 2. Attic Access

If your electrician can walk around in your attic with a drill and wire fish tape, new recessed lights are straightforward. If you have a finished room above the ceiling (a second floor, for example), the job gets significantly harder. The electrician may need to open up small sections of drywall, work through the ceiling cavity, and patch everything back. Always ask about this before getting a quote.

### 3. Your Local Electrician's Rates

Electricians charge **$50 to $130 per hour** nationally, but rates vary a lot by region:

| Region | Average Hourly Rate |
|---|---|
| California, New York, Massachusetts | $85 to $130 |
| Texas, Florida | $65 to $95 |
| Midwest (Ohio, Indiana, Illinois) | $55 to $80 |
| Southeast (Alabama, Mississippi, Tennessee) | $45 to $70 |

Most electricians charge a **minimum service call fee of $75 to $150** on top of hourly rates. Getting more fixtures done in one visit is almost always more cost-efficient per light.

### 4. Number of Fixtures

Electricians love efficiency. If you are getting 10 lights installed in one visit, the per-light cost drops because setup, permitting, and trip costs are shared across more work. A single fixture installation might run $250; six in the same room might average $175 each.

### 5. Dimmer Switches

Most homeowners want their recessed lights on a dimmer, and they should, because it dramatically affects ambiance and can cut your electricity bill by 10 to 50 percent. A basic dimmer switch adds **$25 to $75 in parts** and maybe 30 minutes of labor. If you are installing a smart dimmer (Lutron Caseta, Leviton Decora Smart), expect to pay **$50 to $120 for the switch** plus labor. Make sure any dimmer you buy is rated for LED bulbs; a mismatch causes flickering and shortens bulb life.

### 6. Permits

Most localities require a permit for any new electrical circuit or wiring. Retrofit jobs often skip the permit if no new wiring is being run, but your electrician should advise you. Permits typically cost **$50 to $150** and require an inspection. Skipping a required permit can cause issues when you sell your home, so do not let a contractor talk you out of pulling one. The [Consumer Product Safety Commission (CPSC)](https://www.cpsc.gov) tracks electrical fire incidents, and improper electrical work is a leading cause, so permitted work matters.

## Types of Recessed Lights: What You Are Actually Buying

### Retrofit LED Kits (Wafer Lights)

These are the thin, disk-shaped lights you see everywhere now. Brands like Halo, Lithonia Lighting, and Sunco make them. They clip directly into your existing drywall hole and plug into a quick-connect adapter. No can housing needed.

**Pros:** Quick to install, very thin profile, no heat buildup, integrated LED lasts 25,000+ hours  
**Cons:** Cannot replace the bulb separately (the whole unit is the LED), some models have lower dimming range

**Cost for the fixture itself:** $15 to $40 per light

### Traditional Can Lights (Recessed Housing + Trim + Bulb)

The original style. A metal cylinder ("can") is installed in the ceiling, then a trim ring and a separate bulb go inside. More parts, more installation time, but you can swap out bulbs and trim styles over the years.

**Cost for the fixture:** $20 to $60 for the can and trim, plus bulb ($5 to $20 for an LED)

### Key Ratings to Understand

When your electrician talks about fixtures, they will mention two key ratings. Understanding them helps you avoid future problems.

**IC-rated (Insulation Contact):** Means the fixture can be in direct contact with insulation. If your ceiling has insulation above it (which most do), you need IC-rated fixtures. Non-IC fixtures need a 3-inch air gap around them from insulation, and the [Department of Energy (DOE)](https://www.energy.gov) notes that gaps in insulation are a major source of home heat loss.

**Airtight-rated:** Means the fixture has a gasket that prevents air from leaking between your living space and the attic. This is huge for energy efficiency. The DOE estimates that non-airtight recessed lights in insulated ceilings can account for up to 10 percent of your home's air leakage. Look for fixtures labeled ASTM E283 certified for airtightness.

**For most homes:** Get fixtures that are both IC-rated and airtight. They cost about the same as non-rated versions, and they keep your energy bills lower.

## How Many Lights Do You Actually Need?

A common mistake is under-lighting a room. Lighting designers use a formula based on lumens, but here is a practical rule of thumb:

**General ambient lighting:** Place one 4-inch fixture every 4 feet, or one 6-inch fixture every 6 feet, measured from walls and from each other.

| Room Size | Recommended Fixtures | Typical Cost (Retrofit) |
|---|---|---|
| Small bathroom (50 sq ft) | 2 to 3 lights | $250 to $675 |
| Kitchen (150 sq ft) | 6 to 8 lights | $750 to $1,800 |
| Living room (200 sq ft) | 8 to 10 lights | $1,000 to $2,250 |
| Master bedroom (200 sq ft) | 6 to 8 lights | $750 to $1,800 |
| Basement (500 sq ft) | 12 to 16 lights | $1,500 to $3,600 |

For task lighting over a kitchen island or bathroom vanity, add focused lights positioned directly above the work surface.

## DIY vs. Hiring an Electrician

Retrofitting a wafer-style recessed light into an existing ceiling box or junction is genuinely a DIY-friendly job if you are comfortable turning off your breaker and working with basic wiring. The snap-in connectors on modern wafer lights are designed for homeowners.

Where you should absolutely hire a licensed electrician:

- **New circuit required:** Adding lights to a new circuit means working in your electrical panel. This is dangerous and is illegal to do yourself in most states without a license.
- **Wiring fish through walls or ceilings:** Fishing wire through finished spaces is a skill that takes practice. Done wrong, you end up with visible patches or worse, a fire hazard.
- **Aluminum wiring in older homes:** If your home was built before 1972, there is a chance it has aluminum wiring. This requires special connectors and handling, as the [CPSC warns](https://www.cpsc.gov/Safety-Education/Safety-Guides/Home/Aluminum-Wiring) that improperly handled aluminum wiring is a significant fire risk.
- **Permit required:** In most jurisdictions, any new wiring requires a licensed electrician to pull the permit.

If you are a competent DIYer doing a true retrofit (same hole, no new wire runs, just swapping out old can lights for new wafer lights), you can save $60 to $100 per fixture in labor.

## Signs You Need to Upgrade Your Wiring First

Sometimes the conversation about recessed lighting opens up a bigger one: whether your home's electrical system is up to the job.

Signs you may need electrical work before or alongside new lighting:

- **Breakers trip frequently** when you run multiple lights or appliances
- **Flickering lights** throughout the house
- **Your home was built before 1960** and has never had a panel upgrade
- **Your panel is still 100-amp service** (modern homes need 200-amp for typical loads)
- **You have knob-and-tube or aluminum wiring** throughout

An electrical panel upgrade costs **$1,500 to $4,000** on its own. If you are heading down this path, bundle it with your lighting project for efficiency. See our guide on [electrical panel upgrade costs](/blog/electrical-panel-upgrade-cost) for more detail.

## Finding a Good Electrician

Do not just go with whoever is cheapest. Electrical work that is done wrong is a fire hazard, and it will fail inspection when you sell your home.

**Check for licensing:** Every state requires electricians to be licensed. You can verify a license through your state's contractor licensing board. The [National Association of Home Builders (NAHB)](https://www.nahb.org) maintains a resource directory. Some states have their license lookup online (search "your state + electrician license lookup").

**Get three quotes:** Prices vary widely even in the same city. Getting at least three written quotes takes an afternoon but can save you $300 to $800 on a moderate job.

**Ask what the quote includes:** Does it include permits? Patching drywall if needed? Hauling away old fixtures? Get it in writing.

**Ask about their approach to access:** "How will you run the wire?" is a great question. The answer tells you a lot about whether the electrician has done this before in a finished home.

For more guidance on vetting contractors, see our guide on [how to find a good contractor](/blog/how-to-find-good-contractor).

## Energy Savings From Upgrading to LED Recessed Lighting

If you are replacing old halogen or incandescent can lights, the energy savings are real and measurable.

A 65-watt incandescent BR30 bulb costs about $0.065 per hour to run at the national average of $0.13/kWh. An equivalent LED uses about 9 watts, costing $0.012 per hour.

**Example:** 8 recessed lights, on 4 hours per day

| Bulb Type | Daily Cost | Annual Cost |
|---|---|---|
| 65W Incandescent | $2.08 | $759 |
| 9W LED | $0.29 | $105 |
| **Annual Savings** | | **$654** |

At that savings rate, even a $1,500 professional installation pays for itself in about 2.3 years in electricity alone. The [ENERGY STAR program](https://www.energystar.gov) certifies LED fixtures that meet strict efficiency and quality standards. Look for the ENERGY STAR label when buying fixtures.

## Tax Credits and Rebates for Lighting Upgrades

As of 2026, standalone lighting upgrades are not eligible for the federal residential energy tax credit under the Inflation Reduction Act. However, if your lighting project is part of a larger energy efficiency project (such as adding attic insulation, air sealing, or upgrading to a heat pump), you may qualify for credits on those components. Check [IRS Form 5695](https://www.irs.gov/forms-pubs/about-form-5695) and consult your tax advisor.

Many utility companies offer rebates for switching to LED lighting. Check your local utility's website or the [ENERGY STAR Rebate Finder](https://www.energystar.gov/rebate-finder) for programs in your area. Rebates of $5 to $25 per qualifying LED fixture are common.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**How long does it take to install recessed lighting?**  
A retrofit job with an electrician typically takes 30 to 60 minutes per fixture. A room with 6 lights takes about half a day. New installs requiring new wiring take longer, sometimes a full day for the same room.

**Can I add recessed lighting without an attic?**  
Yes, but it is harder and more expensive. Electricians use wire fishing tools and may need to open small access holes in drywall, which adds $150 to $400 in patching costs per access point.

**What size recessed light should I use?**  
4-inch fixtures work well in kitchens, bathrooms, and hallways where you want focused light. 6-inch fixtures are better for general ambient lighting in living rooms and bedrooms. Mixing sizes in one room can look awkward, so pick one and stick with it.

**Will adding recessed lights increase my home's value?**  
Updated lighting is consistently listed as a desirable feature in real estate listings and can improve perceived value, though it is not captured the way a kitchen remodel is. The bigger financial argument is energy savings if you are replacing inefficient fixtures.
