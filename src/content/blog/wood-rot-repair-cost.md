Wood rot repair costs most homeowners **$300 to $2,500**, with minor surface patches running as low as $100 and extensive structural damage easily reaching $10,000 or more. If you just spotted soft, discolored, or crumbling wood somewhere on your home, you are not alone. The EPA estimates that wood decay and moisture damage affect millions of US homes every year, and the sooner you act, the smaller that bill stays.

This guide breaks down exactly what you will pay, where rot hides, and how to protect your wallet from a small problem becoming a very expensive one.

---

## Why Wood Rot Is Such a Big Deal for Homeowners

Rotting wood is not just an eyesore. It is a structural threat. Wood rot is caused by fungi that feed on cellulose and lignin inside wood fibers. Those fungi need four things to survive: oxygen, moisture, warmth, and food (the wood itself). Take away moisture and you stop rot cold. Let it linger and the damage spreads fast.

The two main types you will encounter are:

- **Dry rot (Serpula lacrymans):** Despite the name, dry rot starts with moisture. Once established, it can spread to nearby dry wood and is harder to eliminate.
- **Wet rot:** More common and easier to treat. It stays in areas that stay wet, like window sills, door frames, and deck boards.

Both types weaken the wood fibers. In structural locations like floor joists, roof rafters, or load-bearing posts, this is a safety issue, not just a cosmetic one.

## What Factors Drive Wood Rot Repair Costs

### 1. Location on the Home

Some rot is cheap to fix. Some is not. The location matters enormously:

- **Exterior trim and fascia:** Easy access, cosmetic damage. Often the least expensive repairs.
- **Window and door frames:** Moderate cost. These need to seal properly, so sloppy repairs create new problems.
- **Decks and porches:** Varies widely. A few soft boards are straightforward. Rotted ledger boards or structural posts get expensive fast.
- **Roof sheathing and rafters:** Requires roofing work on top of carpentry. Costs climb quickly.
- **Subfloor and floor joists:** Labor-intensive because they require working in tight spaces. Often the most expensive category aside from sill plates.
- **Sill plates (where the house frame meets the foundation):** These are critical structural members. Repair or replacement can run $1,000 to $6,000 or more and may require a structural engineer.

### 2. Extent of the Damage

Contractors typically charge by the square foot for siding or sheathing, and by the linear foot for framing and trim. A single soft spot on a door casing might be a quick patch. Rot that has migrated from the frame into the wall cavity behind it is a completely different job.

Probe the surrounding wood with a screwdriver. If the tip sinks in easily, the damage has already spread. That test is free and can save you from being surprised mid-project.

### 3. Labor Rates in Your State

Carpenter labor rates vary significantly across the country. Here is a general sense of hourly ranges:

| Region | Carpenter Hourly Rate |
|---|---|
| Southeast (AL, MS, AR, TN) | $45 to $75 |
| Midwest (OH, IN, MO, KS) | $55 to $85 |
| Southwest (TX, NM, AZ, NV) | $60 to $90 |
| Mid-Atlantic (VA, NC, PA) | $65 to $95 |
| Northeast (NY, CT, MA, NJ) | $80 to $130 |
| Pacific Northwest (WA, OR) | $75 to $115 |
| California | $85 to $140 |

A repair that takes a carpenter two hours in Alabama costs less than half what it costs in San Francisco, even with identical materials.

### 4. Material Choice

When replacing rotted wood, you have options beyond standard dimensional lumber:

- **Pressure-treated lumber:** The go-to for decks, joists, and anywhere moisture is likely. Adds 20 to 50 percent to material cost but lasts far longer.
- **PVC trim boards (like Azek or KOMA):** Never rots, takes paint well, holds up against moisture. Higher upfront cost but dramatically reduces future rot risk.
- **Fiberglass composite:** Common for doors and window frames. Expensive but rot-proof.
- **Epoxy wood filler:** For minor surface damage only. Brands like Abatron WoodEpox or Minwax High Performance Wood Filler can work well on window sills and decorative trim when the structural integrity is intact.

### 5. Associated Work

Rot does not exist in isolation. You often pay for related repairs alongside it:

- Paint or stain (always necessary after rot repair)
- Caulking and weatherstripping
- Insulation replacement if moisture reached wall cavities
- Mold remediation if the moisture also created mold growth
- Plumber or roofer if the source of moisture was a leak

These add-ons can double the original estimate. Ask contractors to inspect for moisture sources before quoting only the rot repair.

## Common Places Homeowners Find Rot

You do not have to wait for obvious collapse to catch wood rot. Here are the spots to inspect twice a year:

**Around windows and doors.** The casing, sill, and threshold area are the most common locations for rot in most homes. Water pools here, especially if caulk has cracked or separated. Press the wood with your thumb. Soft spots mean rot is already there.

**Fascia and soffit boards.** These run along the roofline. Clogged gutters overflow and drench them repeatedly. Check after heavy rains and look for dark staining or peeling paint.

**Deck ledger board.** This is the board that connects your deck to the house. When improperly flashed, water infiltrates behind it and causes rot that you cannot see until the deck is actually dangerous. The [Consumer Product Safety Commission (CPSC)](https://www.cpsc.gov) reports hundreds of deck-related injuries annually, many from structural failure.

**Wood siding.** Horizontal lap siding holds moisture at the bottom edges. Check the lowest two or three rows at the foundation level especially.

**Garage door frames and threshold.** These stay wet, especially in northern climates with snow and ice melt.

**Porch columns and posts.** The bottom of any vertical post is vulnerable, especially if it sits in or near soil.

**Basement rim joists.** These are the joists that rest directly on the sill plate around the perimeter of your foundation. Dark or soft wood here means a serious moisture problem.

## Can You DIY Wood Rot Repair?

For minor cosmetic rot on window trim, door casings, or decorative elements, yes. Here is what that looks like:

1. Dig out all soft material with a chisel or oscillating tool. You need firm wood underneath.
2. Treat the cavity and surrounding wood with a liquid consolidant (like Abatron LiquidWood) to harden what remains.
3. Fill with two-part epoxy filler. Pack it, shape it, and let it cure per instructions.
4. Sand smooth, prime, and paint.
5. Caulk all seams around the repair.

This approach works well for surface and trim rot where the underlying structure is still sound. It is a genuine repair, not a coverup, when done correctly.

**Do not DIY:**

- Anything structural: joists, rafters, sill plates, load-bearing posts
- Rot that involves wall cavities (you need to understand what is behind the wall)
- Rot near electrical wiring or plumbing
- Subfloor repairs in kitchens or bathrooms (often involves tile, plumbing connections, and more)

If you push a screwdriver into wood and it goes in more than half an inch without resistance, call a professional. Do not guess on structural members.

## How to Hire the Right Contractor for Rot Repair

Wood rot repair is done by carpenters, general contractors, and sometimes specialized restoration contractors. Here is how to vet them:

**Get three quotes.** Costs vary wildly. A single quote gives you no baseline for comparison.

**Ask them to identify the moisture source.** Any contractor who wants to just patch the rot without addressing why it rotted is setting you up for the same problem in two years. The source could be a leaky gutter, a failed flashing, a clogged downspout, or inadequate caulking.

**Check their license.** Most states require carpenters and general contractors doing structural work to be licensed. Verify at your state's contractor licensing board. The [National Association of Home Builders (NAHB)](https://www.nahb.org) maintains a directory of licensed members.

**Ask about material choice.** A contractor who automatically reaches for standard pine for exterior repairs may not be thinking long-term. Ask why they are recommending each material.

**Avoid "cash only" or no-permit scenarios.** Structural work in most jurisdictions requires a permit. Unpermitted structural work creates problems when you sell the home.

## Does Homeowners Insurance Cover Wood Rot?

Usually no, and this surprises a lot of people. Standard homeowners insurance covers sudden and accidental damage. Wood rot is considered gradual deterioration and is almost always excluded.

The exception is if rot resulted directly from a covered peril. For example, if a storm caused roof damage that allowed water to enter and eventually rotted your rafters, you might have a claim for the rot as a secondary consequence of the storm damage. Document everything and consult your policy or agent before making assumptions.

## Preventing Wood Rot: The Best Investment You Can Make

Prevention costs far less than repair. Here is what actually works:

**Keep gutters clean and properly pitched.** Overflowing gutters are the single biggest cause of exterior rot on homes. Clean them at least twice a year. [Learn more in our guide to gutter maintenance](/blog/gutter-replacement-cost).

**Caulk every exterior seam.** Windows, doors, trim transitions, and anywhere two materials meet are all entry points for water. Check caulk annually and re-apply wherever it is cracked or separated. Use a paintable exterior silicone or polyurethane caulk.

**Keep soil and mulch away from wood siding.** Six inches of clearance between soil or mulch and any wood on your home is the minimum. Soil contact is a guaranteed pathway to rot.

**Paint and seal exterior wood on schedule.** Paint does more than look good. It is the moisture barrier. Peeling paint means exposed wood. Re-paint when the finish begins to fail, not when it has already peeled away.

**Fix roof and flashing issues immediately.** Even a small leak allows enough recurring moisture to rot wood within one to three years. See our guide on [roof leak causes and fixes](/blog/roof-leak-causes-and-fixes).

**Ventilate crawl spaces and attics properly.** Inadequate ventilation creates condensation that rots wood from the inside out. [Crawl space encapsulation](/blog/crawl-space-encapsulation-cost) can address severe moisture issues in crawl spaces.

## Red Flags That Signal Bigger Problems

Some situations point to a larger moisture or structural issue that needs more than rot repair:

- Multiple rot locations on different parts of the home at the same time
- Rot returning in the same location you already repaired
- Soft floors or springy spots when you walk through the house
- Doors or windows that suddenly stick or do not close properly (can signal foundation or structural movement)
- Visible mold alongside the rot
- Pest activity in or near rotted areas (carpenter ants and wood-boring beetles colonize rot)

These scenarios warrant a full inspection by a licensed contractor or structural engineer before you commit to a repair scope.

## How Much Should You Budget?

Here is a practical budgeting framework for common scenarios:

**You found soft spots on one window sill and a corner of a door frame.** Budget $300 to $700 for a carpenter to assess, repair with epoxy filler or partial replacement, and re-caulk and paint.

**Two or three deck boards are soft and the ledger board looks suspect.** Get a full deck inspection first. Budget $500 to $3,000 depending on what the inspection finds.

**Your home inspector flagged rot in the crawl space rim joists.** This is serious. Budget $2,000 to $8,000 depending on how much of the rim joist is affected and whether the sill plate is also compromised. Address the moisture source at the same time or it will return.

**Rot under bathroom or kitchen floor.** This almost always involves subfloor replacement and potentially flooring removal and reinstallation. Budget $1,500 to $5,000.
