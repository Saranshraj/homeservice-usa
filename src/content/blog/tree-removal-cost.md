Tree removal costs between **$300 and $2,000 per tree** for most US homeowners in 2026, with the national average landing around **$950** including cutting, lowering the pieces, and cleanup. A small tree under 30 feet tall typically runs **$200 to $500**. A large oak or pine over 80 feet can hit **$2,500 or more**, especially if it is close to your house or tangled in power lines. Emergency removal after a storm adds a 25 to 50 percent premium on top of standard rates. If a tree in your yard is making you nervous, you are in the right place. This guide breaks down exactly what drives the price so you can get fair quotes and avoid being overcharged when you are most vulnerable.

---

## Why Tree Removal Costs So Much

If you have never hired a tree service before, the quote might feel like a gut punch. A crew of two to four people, a bucket truck, and a wood chipper running for half a day adds up fast. You are also paying for serious risk. Tree work consistently ranks among the most dangerous jobs in the US, according to the [Bureau of Labor Statistics](https://www.bls.gov), with fatality rates far higher than most construction trades. Certified arborists carry expensive liability insurance and workers' compensation, and that cost is factored into every quote.

What you are actually paying for:

- **Labor:** Two to four trained climbers or bucket truck operators
- **Equipment:** Aerial lift or climbing gear, chainsaws, wood chipper, dump trailer
- **Disposal:** Hauling logs and debris off your property (or chipping on-site)
- **Insurance:** Liability and workers' comp that protects you if something goes wrong
- **Permits:** Required in many municipalities, especially for mature or "heritage" trees

## Tree Removal Cost by Tree Size

Tree size is the single biggest price driver. Height and trunk diameter together determine how long the job takes and what equipment is needed.

| Tree Size | Height | Trunk Diameter | Average Cost Range |
|-----------|--------|----------------|--------------------|
| Very small | Under 25 ft | Under 6 in | $150 to $400 |
| Small | 25 to 40 ft | 6 to 12 in | $300 to $600 |
| Medium | 40 to 60 ft | 12 to 20 in | $600 to $1,100 |
| Large | 60 to 80 ft | 20 to 30 in | $1,100 to $1,800 |
| Very large | Over 80 ft | Over 30 in | $1,800 to $3,000+ |

These ranges assume reasonable access, no power lines, and at least 10 feet of clearance from structures. Costs climb quickly once any of those conditions changes.

### Small Trees: $150 to $600

Small trees like ornamental cherries, young maples, dogwoods, and fruit trees are usually a half-day job for two workers. Straightforward removal from an open yard with easy truck access lands at the low end. A small tree wedged between a fence and your house, or one leaning toward a neighbor's property, will push you toward the high end even at this size.

### Medium Trees: $600 to $1,100

Medium trees, think a 50-foot birch or a mid-size spruce, are a full crew's work for most of a day. At this size, climbers typically section the tree from the top down rather than felling it in one piece, which takes more time and skill. A dead tree of this size costs more, not less, because brittle wood is unpredictable to work with safely.

### Large Trees: $1,100 to $1,800

Large trees, 60-foot oaks, tall pines, mature elms, require either specialized climbing equipment or a bucket truck. In tight spaces near a house or driveway, the crew may need to lower every section on a rope to avoid damaging your property. That precision work takes time, and time is money.

### Very Large Trees: $1,800 to $3,000+

Very large trees, the 80-foot cottonwoods and century-old oaks, are projects that can take a full day or more with a crew of four plus a crane in some cases. These jobs require the most experienced tree companies and the highest-capacity equipment. Getting three quotes is especially important at this level because pricing varies widely.

## Factors That Push the Price Up or Down

### Location and Proximity to Structures

The biggest price jump comes from where the tree stands, not just how tall it is. A 60-foot pine in the middle of a large yard can cost half as much as the same tree 8 feet from your roof line. Tight spaces, sloped terrain, and trees growing into structures or utility lines add 25 to 75 percent to base removal costs.

If a tree is touching or tangled in utility lines, call your utility company first. In most states, the utility company handles wires touching their lines, sometimes at no cost. Never let a tree service touch a line that may be energized.

### Emergency and Storm Damage Removal

After a major storm, demand for tree services spikes and so do prices. Expect to pay 25 to 50 percent above normal rates for emergency removal in the first 48 to 72 hours. If a tree has fallen on your house, it becomes a [homeowners insurance claim](/blog/homeowners-insurance-claims/) for most people. Call your insurer before calling a tree company so you understand what is covered.

The [Federal Emergency Management Agency (FEMA)](https://www.fema.gov) offers assistance for disaster-related tree damage in federally declared disaster areas. After a named hurricane or severe storm event, check FEMA's disaster assistance site before paying out of pocket.

### Number of Trees

Getting multiple trees removed in a single visit almost always saves money per tree. A company that charges $600 for one medium tree may charge $1,500 for three, rather than $1,800. Mention all the trees you want removed when getting quotes, not just the urgent one.

### Tree Health: Dead vs. Living

It seems like a dead tree would be cheaper to remove because it is already gone. The opposite is often true. Dead trees lose structural integrity and become unpredictable when cut. Branches can fail without warning. Climbers charge more because the risk is higher. Budget 15 to 25 percent extra for dead or severely diseased trees.

### Time of Year

Tree companies are busiest from late spring through early fall. In colder regions, winter is the off-season, and some companies offer 10 to 20 percent discounts for scheduling removal between December and February. There is a practical advantage too: without leaves, it is easier to see the tree's structure and work more efficiently. Frozen ground also means less lawn damage from equipment.

## Stump Removal and Grinding Costs

Taking down the tree is only half the job. The stump left behind has its own cost.

| Service | Average Cost |
|---------|-------------|
| Stump grinding (per stump) | $100 to $400 |
| Additional stumps (same visit) | $50 to $150 each |
| Full stump removal (excavation) | $300 to $700 |
| Root removal | $100 to $200 extra |

**Stump grinding** is the most common approach. A machine grinds the stump down 6 to 12 inches below grade, leaving wood chips behind. This is enough for most purposes, including replanting grass over the area. **Full removal**, which involves digging out the entire root ball, is rarely necessary unless you are building a structure on that spot.

Most tree companies offer a bundled price if you combine tree removal and stump grinding at the same appointment. Get the bundle quote upfront; adding stump grinding as an afterthought always costs more.

## Additional Costs to Budget For

**Log splitting and firewood processing:** Some companies will split logs and stack them for you at $50 to $150 extra. If you have a fireplace or wood stove, this is often worth it.

**Haul-away vs. leaving wood:** Many companies include debris haul-away in their base price. Others leave logs and brush, or charge extra to take them. Clarify this in writing before the crew starts. Cleaning up a full-size tree's worth of debris yourself is a weekend project.

**Permits:** Many cities and counties require permits before removing trees, especially large or "heritage" trees. Costs range from free to $150 or more. Your tree company should know local requirements and can often pull the permit for you, sometimes included in the quote, sometimes as an add-on. Check with your local parks department or building department if you are unsure. Large trees that overhang your roofline can also pack [gutters](/blog/gutter-replacement-cost/) with leaves and debris year-round, accelerating wear on the gutter system — worth factoring into your decision about trimming versus full removal.

**Emergency boarding or tarping:** If a fallen tree has breached your roof or a window, a tree company may charge separately for temporary weatherproofing while the structural damage is assessed. A tree impact can cause damage well beyond what is visible on the surface — our guide to [roof replacement costs](/blog/roof-replacement-cost/) explains what to expect if the structure needs significant work.

## Tree Removal Cost by State

Regional labor costs, permit requirements, and local demand all affect prices. Here is a rough regional breakdown based on 2026 market data.

| Region | Small Tree | Medium Tree | Large Tree |
|--------|------------|-------------|------------|
| Southeast (FL, GA, SC) | $250 to $550 | $600 to $1,000 | $1,000 to $1,600 |
| Midwest (IL, OH, MN) | $200 to $500 | $500 to $900 | $900 to $1,500 |
| Southwest (TX, AZ, NM) | $200 to $600 | $550 to $1,100 | $1,000 to $1,800 |
| Pacific Coast (CA, OR, WA) | $350 to $700 | $700 to $1,300 | $1,300 to $2,500 |
| Northeast (NY, MA, CT) | $300 to $650 | $650 to $1,200 | $1,200 to $2,200 |

California runs higher due to elevated labor costs, strict regulations around certain protected tree species, and high insurance costs for tree crews. Florida prices are influenced by year-round storm demand. The Midwest tends to have the most competitive market.

## How to Hire a Tree Service Without Getting Scammed

Storm chasers and fly-by-night tree outfits cause more damage to American homeowners than almost any other contractor category. After a big storm, unmarked trucks roll through neighborhoods offering cheap, fast removal. Many of these crews are uninsured and unqualified. If one of them drops a limb through your roof, you pay for it. The same due diligence that applies to any home contractor applies double here — our guide on [how to find a good contractor](/blog/how-to-find-good-contractor/) covers the verification steps that can protect you.

**Here is how to protect yourself:**

**1. Verify credentials.** Look for [ISA Certified Arborists](https://www.isa-arbor.com/find-an-arborist/) (International Society of Arboriculture). ISA certification requires documented experience, passing a difficult exam, and ongoing education. It is not required by law in most states, but it is the gold standard.

**2. Check licensing.** Many states require tree services to hold a landscape contractor or tree trimmer license. Check your state licensing board before hiring. Your state's department of agriculture or labor licensing board can verify credentials.

**3. Demand proof of insurance.** Ask for a certificate of insurance showing general liability (at least $1 million) and workers' compensation. Call the insurer's number on the certificate to verify it is current. If a worker is hurt on your property and the company has no workers' comp, you can be held liable.

**4. Get three written quotes.** One quote is not a market. Three quotes let you see what is reasonable and catch outliers on both ends. A quote significantly below market often signals missing insurance or inexperience.

**5. Never pay in full upfront.** A reasonable deposit is 20 to 30 percent. Paying in full before work starts removes your leverage if the job goes wrong or the crew disappears.

**6. Get scope of work in writing.** The contract should specify what is included: stump grinding, debris removal, log disposal, any hauling fees. Verbal promises about "taking everything" often become disputes.

## Do You Actually Need the Tree Removed?

Before spending $1,000 or more, it is worth asking a certified arborist whether removal is really necessary. Sometimes the right answer is:

- **Crown reduction or trimming:** Reduces a tree's height and spread without full removal. Cost is $200 to $800 for most trees.
- **Cabling and bracing:** For structurally weak or split trunks, steel cables can extend a tree's life. Cost is $500 to $1,500.
- **Disease treatment:** Some diseased trees can be treated and saved. An arborist can assess whether treatment is viable.

Getting an arborist's opinion on large trees near your home is a step that any solid [first-year homeowner guide](/blog/first-year-homeowner-guide/) recommends as part of an annual outdoor safety check.

Full removal makes sense when a tree is dead, structurally unsound and cannot be safely stabilized, in a location that cannot be managed safely over time, or when a structure is being built in its footprint.

## Does Homeowners Insurance Cover Tree Removal?

Homeowners insurance typically covers tree removal **only if the tree has fallen on a covered structure**, like your house, garage, or fence. If a tree falls in your yard without hitting anything, most policies do not cover removal, even if the storm that toppled it was a covered event.

Coverage limits vary. Many policies cap tree removal at $500 to $1,000 per tree even when it is covered. Review your policy or call your agent before assuming insurance will handle it. The [Insurance Information Institute](https://www.iii.org) has a useful breakdown of what standard homeowners policies typically cover for tree and storm damage.
