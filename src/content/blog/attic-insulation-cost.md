Adding or replacing attic insulation costs **$1,500 to $6,000** for most US homeowners in 2026, with a national average around **$2,800 to $3,500** for a 1,200 to 1,500 sq ft attic. Blown-in insulation is the most affordable option at **$1.00 to $1.70 per square foot installed**, while spray foam costs **$3.00 to $7.00 per square foot** but delivers the best air sealing. The good news: if you upgrade to properly insulated levels recommended by the [Department of Energy](https://www.energy.gov/energysaver/insulation), you qualify for a **30% federal tax credit up to $1,200 per year** under the Inflation Reduction Act. That credit alone can cover a third or more of a typical project.

This is one of the highest-return upgrades a US homeowner can make. Proper attic insulation can cut your heating and cooling costs by **15 to 25 percent annually**, according to [ENERGY STAR](https://www.energystar.gov/campaign/seal_insulate/identify_problems_you_want_fix/diy_checks_inspections/check_your_insulation_levels). For most homes, that means saving **$200 to $600 per year** on energy bills. When you factor in the tax credit, the payback period for most attic insulation projects is just 3 to 6 years.

## Average Attic Insulation Costs at a Glance

| Project Type | Cost Range | Best For |
|---|---|---|
| Top-up existing insulation (blown-in) | $800 to $2,000 | Homes with some insulation but below recommended R-value |
| Full attic blown-in insulation | $1,500 to $3,500 | Older homes with little or no insulation |
| Batt insulation (fiberglass or mineral wool) | $1,200 to $3,000 | New construction or open joist bays |
| Spray foam (closed-cell) | $4,000 to $9,000 | Best air sealing, unfinished attics, crawl spaces |
| Rigid foam board | $2,000 to $5,000 | Attic hatches, knee walls, specialty applications |
| Radiant barrier installation | $1,000 to $2,500 | Hot climates, reduces cooling load |

Prices above include both materials and professional labor for a 1,200 sq ft attic.

## Cost by Insulation Type

### Blown-In Insulation: $1.00 to $1.70 per Sq Ft

Blown-in insulation (also called loose-fill) is the most popular choice for attic floors because it fills every gap and settles into irregular spaces. It comes in two main materials:

**Fiberglass blown-in:** $0.90 to $1.50 per sq ft. Lightweight, non-combustible, and does not absorb moisture. A 1,500 sq ft attic runs roughly **$1,350 to $2,250**.

**Cellulose blown-in:** $1.00 to $1.70 per sq ft. Made from recycled newspaper treated with fire retardants. Slightly better at reducing air infiltration than fiberglass. The same 1,500 sq ft attic runs **$1,500 to $2,550**. Many insulation contractors prefer cellulose because it is denser and settles less over time.

Both blown-in types can be DIYed if you rent a blower machine (typically free with purchase of bags at home improvement stores), but professional installation ensures the correct depth and even coverage. Hiring a pro typically adds **$300 to $600** in labor.

### Fiberglass Batt Insulation: $0.64 to $1.20 per Sq Ft

Fiberglass batts (those pink or yellow rolls you see at the hardware store) are cheaper per square foot but work best in open joist bays without obstructions. They are harder to fit around pipes, wiring, and other attic obstacles.

A 1,500 sq ft attic with straightforward joist bays runs **$960 to $1,800** installed. The key downside: batts leave more gaps than blown-in, which lets conditioned air leak out and outside air sneak in.

[Mineral wool batts](https://www.energystar.gov/products/building_products/thermal_insulation) cost slightly more at **$1.10 to $1.80 per sq ft** but offer better fire resistance and soundproofing. They are worth considering in finished attic spaces.

### Spray Foam Insulation: $3.00 to $7.00 per Sq Ft

Spray foam is the premium option because it air-seals and insulates in one step. It comes in two types:

**Open-cell spray foam:** $0.44 to $0.65 per board foot (about $1.50 to $3.50 per sq ft at 3 inches thick). Softer, absorbs sound, but lets some moisture through. Good for interior applications.

**Closed-cell spray foam:** $1.00 to $1.50 per board foot (about $3.00 to $7.00 per sq ft at 2 to 3 inches thick). Creates a vapor barrier and a rigid air seal. The best performer, but the most expensive.

For a full attic conversion using closed-cell spray foam, budget **$6,000 to $12,000** or more for a 1,500 sq ft attic. Spray foam is most cost-effective when applied to the **roof deck and rafters** in an unventilated (conditioned) attic, making the attic space part of your home's thermal envelope.

## Costs by R-Value and Climate Zone

The [Department of Energy](https://www.energy.gov/energysaver/insulation) recommends specific R-values for attic insulation based on your climate zone. R-value measures thermal resistance: higher numbers mean better insulation.

| DOE Climate Zone | Recommended R-Value | Typical Depth (Blown-In) | Approximate Cost (1,500 sq ft) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Zone 1-2 (Florida, Gulf Coast) | R-30 to R-49 | 8 to 12 inches | $1,200 to $2,500 |
| Zone 3 (Southeast, Southwest) | R-38 to R-60 | 10 to 16 inches | $1,500 to $3,200 |
| Zone 4 (Mid-Atlantic, Pacific NW) | R-38 to R-60 | 10 to 16 inches | $1,500 to $3,200 |
| Zone 5-6 (Midwest, New England) | R-49 to R-60 | 14 to 18 inches | $2,000 to $4,000 |
| Zone 7 (Northern Minnesota, Alaska) | R-49 to R-60+ | 16 to 20 inches | $2,200 to $4,500 |

If your attic already has some insulation, you only need to pay for the difference. A home in Ohio (Zone 5) with R-19 existing insulation needs to add about R-30 to reach the recommended R-49. That is roughly 8 additional inches of blown-in cellulose, costing around **$1,000 to $1,800** rather than a full install.

Use the [ENERGY STAR Home Advisor](https://www.energystar.gov/campaign/seal_insulate) to look up the exact recommendation for your zip code.

## What Makes Attic Insulation Projects Cost More

Several factors can push your project above the typical range:

**Removing old insulation:** If your attic has wet, moldy, or vermiculite-containing insulation (common in homes built before 1980), it must be removed before new insulation goes in. Removal costs **$1 to $2 per sq ft** or **$1,500 to $3,000** for a typical attic. Vermiculite may contain asbestos and requires licensed abatement contractors; that can add **$3,000 to $10,000** depending on the volume and your state. The [EPA recommends testing](https://www.epa.gov/asbestos) before disturbing any suspected vermiculite insulation.

**Air sealing first:** Before blowing in insulation, a good contractor will seal air leaks around light fixtures, top plates, plumbing penetrations, and attic hatches. This step costs an extra **$500 to $1,500** but dramatically improves the result. Ask your contractor whether air sealing is included in the quote.

**Difficult attic access:** Low-slope roofs, knee walls, and attics with limited headroom take longer to insulate and cost more in labor. Expect to add **$300 to $800** for a challenging attic layout.

**Attic hatch or pull-down stairs:** These are major sources of heat loss. Adding an insulated attic hatch cover costs **$50 to $350** as a DIY project or **$150 to $500** installed. Some contractors include this; many do not.

**Existing pest damage:** Mice, squirrels, and raccoons can contaminate attic insulation with droppings and urine. A pest remediation and cleanup before re-insulating can add **$500 to $3,000** depending on the extent of the damage.

## The 30% Federal Tax Credit: Real Numbers

This is the part that often surprises homeowners. Under the [Inflation Reduction Act](https://www.irs.gov/credits-deductions/energy-efficient-home-improvement-credit), you can claim **30% of the cost of qualifying insulation improvements**, up to **$1,200 per year** for insulation and air sealing combined.

Here is what that means in real money:

- Project cost: $3,500
- 30% credit: $1,050
- Your actual out-of-pocket cost: **$2,450**

- Project cost: $5,000
- 30% credit: $1,500 (capped at $1,200)
- Your actual out-of-pocket cost: **$3,800**

The credit is nonrefundable (it reduces your tax liability but does not generate a refund if it exceeds what you owe), but it does carry forward in some circumstances. The credit is available through 2032.

**What qualifies:** Insulation and air sealing materials that meet or exceed the [International Energy Conservation Code (IECC)](https://www.energy.gov/energysaver/insulation) standards. Most blown-in and batt insulation from major manufacturers qualifies. Your contractor should provide documentation showing the product meets the IECC requirements. File [IRS Form 5695](https://www.irs.gov/forms-pubs/about-form-5695) with your federal return.

**State incentives stack on top:** Many states offer additional rebates or credits. California, New York, Massachusetts, Minnesota, and Illinois all have active utility rebate programs that can add **$200 to $800** in savings on top of the federal credit. Check [ENERGY STAR's rebate finder](https://www.energystar.gov/rebate-finder) for your state and utility.

## Financing Your Attic Insulation Project

If the upfront cost feels steep, you have solid options for spreading it out:

**Utility on-bill financing:** Many utilities offer zero-interest or low-interest financing for energy efficiency upgrades, repaid through your monthly bill. Programs like the PACE (Property Assessed Clean Energy) program are available in about 35 states. Ask your utility company if they offer weatherization financing.

**FHA Energy Efficient Mortgage (EEM):** If you are buying or refinancing a home, an FHA EEM lets you roll the cost of energy upgrades directly into your mortgage. The [HUD website](https://www.hud.gov/program_offices/housing/sfh/eem/energy-r) has full details on qualifying improvements.

**Weatherization Assistance Program:** Low- and moderate-income households may qualify for **free insulation** through the [DOE Weatherization Assistance Program](https://www.energy.gov/scep/wap/weatherization-assistance-program). This federal program covers insulation, air sealing, and other efficiency upgrades at no cost for eligible homeowners.

**Personal loans and HELOCs:** For homeowners with equity, a home equity line of credit (HELOC) offers the lowest interest rates for home improvement projects. Personal loans through credit unions or online lenders typically run **6 to 12% APR** for well-qualified borrowers.

## DIY vs. Hiring a Pro

For most homeowners, **blown-in insulation on the attic floor** is a realistic DIY project, especially as a top-up over existing insulation. Home Depot and Lowe's both rent insulation blowers free with the purchase of a minimum number of bags (usually 10 or more). A DIY project for a 1,500 sq ft attic runs about **$700 to $1,200** in materials, saving you **$800 to $2,000** compared to professional installation.

When to definitely hire a pro:

- You suspect mold, asbestos, or pest contamination
- Your attic has complex knee walls or unusual geometry
- You want spray foam (DIY spray foam requires specialized equipment and rarely performs as well as professional application)
- You want the work to qualify for a utility rebate (many programs require contractor installation)
- You are air sealing along with insulating (getting it right matters a lot here)

If you do hire a pro, verify their credentials. Insulation contractors should hold a license in states that require one (check your [state contractor licensing board](https://www.contractors-license.org/)). The [Building Performance Institute (BPI)](https://www.bpi.org) certifies contractors in home energy efficiency work, which is a good credential to look for.

## How to Get the Best Price

**Get three quotes in writing.** Insulation pricing varies more than almost any other trade. One contractor may quote $2,200 while another quotes $4,500 for the same attic. Ask each one to specify the R-value they are quoting, the material and brand, the depth they will achieve, and whether air sealing is included.

**Ask about rebates.** A good contractor will know about utility rebates and DOE programs in your area. If they look blank when you mention the 30% federal tax credit, that is a red flag. They should provide a product data sheet to confirm the insulation meets IECC standards.

**Time your project well.** Late fall and early spring are slower seasons for insulation contractors in most markets. Prices can be 10 to 15 percent lower than peak summer months.

**Bundle with other air sealing work.** If you are also replacing weatherstripping, sealing around outlets, or addressing other air leaks, bundling that work with your insulation project gives you negotiating leverage and often saves money on mobilization costs.

## Signs Your Attic Insulation Needs Attention

You probably need new or additional attic insulation if:

- Your home was built before 1980 (insulation standards have changed dramatically)
- Your energy bills are high compared to neighbors with similar-sized homes
- Rooms on the top floor are noticeably hotter in summer or colder in winter
- You can see the ceiling joists from inside your attic (the insulation should be deep enough to cover them)
- Ice dams form along your roof edge in winter (a sign of heat escaping through the attic floor)
- Your existing insulation is less than 10 to 11 inches deep for fiberglass or 12 to 13 inches deep for cellulose (those depths approximate R-38)

You can also do a quick check with a ruler: just stick it into the insulation at a few points and measure the depth. Use the [DOE's online calculator](https://www.energy.gov/energysaver/insulation) to compare your current R-value to what is recommended for your zone.
