Professional mold remediation costs between **$1,500 and $9,000** for most US homeowners in 2026, with the national average landing around **$3,500** for a single affected area like a bathroom, basement, or crawl space. Small contained areas under 10 square feet can run as little as **$500 to $1,500**. Severe whole-house mold infestations, often following flooding or a slow roof leak, can reach **$10,000 to $30,000 or more**. If you just discovered mold in your home, take a breath. The cost varies enormously based on where the mold is, how large the affected area is, and what type of mold you are dealing with. This guide gives you real numbers so you can make smart decisions fast.

## What You Are Actually Paying For

Before breaking down the numbers, it helps to understand what remediation companies actually do. The word "remediation" means controlled removal combined with treating the underlying moisture source. Companies that only scrub visible mold without fixing the moisture problem are setting you up for a return visit in six months.

A legitimate mold remediation job includes:
- Inspection and moisture testing to find the source
- Containment with plastic sheeting and negative air pressure
- Removal and disposal of contaminated materials
- HEPA air scrubbing
- Treatment of affected surfaces with EPA-registered antimicrobial products
- Post-remediation clearance testing to confirm the job is done

That sequence takes time and trained labor, which is why the cost is what it is.

## Mold Remediation Cost by Location in Your Home

Where the mold lives is the biggest driver of cost, because it determines how much material needs to be removed and how accessible the space is.

| Location | Typical Cost Range | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Bathroom (shower/tile) | $500 to $1,500 | Often surface-level; tile and grout usually saved |
| Basement | $500 to $4,000 | Depends heavily on finished vs. unfinished |
| Crawl space | $500 to $4,000 | Tight access drives up labor |
| Attic | $1,000 to $9,000 | Roof leaks, poor ventilation; decking often replaced |
| HVAC ductwork | $3,000 to $10,000 | Entire system must be cleaned or replaced |
| Wall cavity (hidden) | $2,500 to $7,500 | Drywall must be cut out and replaced |
| Whole house | $10,000 to $30,000+ | Post-flood or long-term leak scenarios |

### Bathroom Mold: $500 to $1,500

Bathroom mold is the most common call remediation companies get in summer. High humidity from showers, inadequate ventilation, and caulk that has seen better days create perfect conditions for mold on tile, grout, drywall, and ceiling corners. Surface mold on non-porous tile is often cleaned in a single visit. If mold has penetrated the drywall behind the tile, that area needs to come out, which pushes the cost toward the high end of the range.

### Crawl Space Mold: $500 to $4,000

Crawl spaces are the most overlooked mold hotspot in American homes, particularly in the South and Pacific Northwest. Moisture rises from the ground, condenses on floor joists, and mold follows. The cost varies based on how much of the floor framing is affected. Many remediation companies will also recommend encapsulating the crawl space with a vapor barrier after treatment, which is a separate project running **$1,500 to $5,000** but dramatically reduces recurrence.

### Attic Mold: $1,000 to $9,000

Attic mold almost always traces back to poor ventilation, a bathroom exhaust fan venting into the attic instead of outside, or a roof leak. The mold typically grows on the underside of the roof decking. Remediation includes treatment and sometimes full decking replacement, which pushes costs to the high end. If your roof is already due for replacement, remediation is often done in tandem to save money on labor.

### HVAC and Ductwork Mold: $3,000 to $10,000

Mold in your HVAC system is the most serious scenario because the system actively circulates mold spores throughout your home every time it runs. Remediation requires cleaning or replacing all duct sections, treating the air handler, and potentially replacing the evaporator coil. The [EPA advises](https://www.epa.gov/mold/mold-resources) that if mold is present on insulated ductwork, the ductwork should be replaced entirely since the insulation cannot be adequately cleaned.

## Mold Remediation Cost by Severity

Professionals typically assess mold by the square footage of affected material, not just visible surface area.

| Severity Level | Affected Area | Typical Cost |
|---|---|---|
| Minor | Under 10 sq ft | $500 to $1,500 |
| Moderate | 10 to 100 sq ft | $1,500 to $5,000 |
| Extensive | 100 to 300 sq ft | $5,000 to $10,000 |
| Severe | Over 300 sq ft | $10,000 to $30,000+ |

The **10 square foot rule** comes directly from the [EPA's guide on mold remediation](https://www.epa.gov/mold/brief-guide-mold-moisture-and-your-home). Areas smaller than a 3x3 foot patch can often be handled by a careful homeowner using proper protective equipment. Any larger than that, or any mold hidden inside walls, in HVAC systems, or in crawl spaces, warrants a professional.

## What Is Black Mold and Does It Cost More to Remove?

"Black mold" is one of the most searched and most misunderstood terms in home care. When people say black mold, they usually mean *Stachybotrys chartarum*, a specific species associated with long-term water damage to cellulose materials like drywall and wood. It is dark greenish-black and has a slimy texture.

The truth: many mold species can appear black. Color alone does not tell you what you are dealing with. That said, if testing confirms *Stachybotrys*, the remediation process is more intensive because the mold requires more prolonged wet conditions to grow, which means the underlying materials are typically more heavily saturated and degraded.

**Black mold remediation cost: $2,000 to $6,000** for a contained area, with whole-house scenarios running $10,000 to $30,000.

The [CDC notes](https://www.cdc.gov/mold/faqs.htm) that all mold should be removed regardless of species, and that the health risks from mold exposure are primarily linked to quantity of exposure, not species type.

## Mold Testing Cost: $200 to $700

Before remediation, many homeowners want testing to confirm what they are dealing with and where the mold is hiding. Here is what testing typically costs:

| Test Type | Cost Range | What It Tells You |
|---|---|---|
| Visual inspection (professional) | $200 to $600 | Identifies visible mold areas and moisture sources |
| Air quality sampling | $250 to $400 | Spore types and counts in your air vs. outdoors |
| Surface swab test | $100 to $300 | Identifies mold species on a specific surface |
| Bulk/material sample | $150 to $400 | Mold in building materials (drywall, wood) |
| Full home inspection + lab testing | $400 to $700 | Comprehensive: air, surfaces, moisture mapping |

A note of caution: testing is useful when you need to verify the extent of hidden mold or confirm a remediation job is complete. But if you can already see mold, spending $500 on testing before you spend $3,000 on remediation just delays fixing the problem. Test after remediation (called clearance testing) to confirm the job was done right. This usually costs **$150 to $300** and is worth every penny.

## State-by-State Cost Differences

Mold remediation costs vary significantly by region, driven by two factors: labor rates and how common mold problems are (which affects local competition and supply of remediation companies).

| Region | Cost Range vs. National Average | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Southeast (FL, GA, AL, MS, LA) | 15 to 30% higher | Extreme humidity; very high demand |
| Pacific Northwest (OR, WA) | 10 to 20% higher | High rainfall and wet seasons |
| Gulf Coast (TX, LA) | 20 to 35% higher post-storm | Hurricane season drives demand spikes |
| Mountain West (CO, UT, WY) | 10 to 20% lower | Drier climate; lower baseline demand |
| Midwest (MN, IA, WI) | 5 to 15% lower | Lower labor costs; seasonal demand |
| Northeast (NY, MA, CT) | 10 to 25% higher | High labor costs; older housing stock |
| Mid-Atlantic (DC, MD, VA) | 5 to 15% higher | Humid summers; mixed housing age |

If you are in Florida or along the Gulf Coast, you are likely to pay a premium. Humidity is relentless, mold remediation companies are busy, and post-hurricane demand surges can push prices 50 to 100 percent above normal in affected areas. Build your list of vetted contractors now, before you need one urgently.

## Does Homeowners Insurance Cover Mold Remediation?

This is where many homeowners get surprised, and not in a good way. Standard homeowners insurance policies typically do not cover mold remediation unless the mold resulted directly from a covered peril, such as a burst pipe or appliance malfunction. Even then, insurers often cap mold coverage at **$1,000 to $10,000**, well below the actual cost of severe remediation.

Mold that developed slowly from a leaky roof, poor ventilation, or ongoing humidity is almost always classified as a maintenance issue, which policies explicitly exclude.

**When insurance might pay:**
- Mold from a sudden, accidental pipe burst
- Mold discovered during a covered water damage claim
- Mold that developed after a covered storm event

**When insurance almost certainly will not pay:**
- Mold from a slow, unnoticed roof leak
- Mold from condensation or high indoor humidity
- Mold from a crawl space without vapor barrier
- Mold that predates your policy (not disclosed at purchase)

Before filing a claim, document everything with photos and dates. Call your insurer to understand your policy's mold endorsement before authorizing remediation work, because some companies require prior authorization. The [National Association of Insurance Commissioners](https://www.naic.org) has a consumer guide to homeowners policies with state-specific coverage information.

Some insurers offer mold endorsements as policy add-ons for **$50 to $150 per year**. If you live in a high-humidity state, this is worth pricing out.

## DIY Mold Removal: When It Is OK and When to Stop

The EPA's guidance is clear: areas under 10 square feet (roughly a 3x3 foot patch) on non-porous surfaces like tile can often be cleaned by a homeowner using the right protective equipment. Areas larger than that, mold on porous materials like drywall or insulation, and any mold in HVAC systems require a professional.

**For DIY-appropriate surface mold, you need:**
- N-95 respirator (not a dust mask)
- Safety goggles (no vent holes)
- Disposable gloves
- EPA-registered mold cleaner or a 1-cup-bleach-per-gallon-of-water solution for non-porous surfaces
- Sealed plastic bags for all debris

The [EPA warns](https://www.epa.gov/mold) against using bleach on porous surfaces like wood, drywall, or concrete, because bleach does not penetrate; it kills surface mold while leaving the root structure alive. On those materials, the material must come out.

**Never attempt DIY if:**
- The affected area is larger than 10 square feet
- Mold is inside walls, under floors, or in the HVAC system
- You have respiratory conditions, a compromised immune system, or allergies
- The mold appeared after flooding (potential sewage contamination)
- You cannot identify and fix the moisture source

Trying to DIY a serious mold problem and doing it wrong does not just fail. It can spread spores through your home and make the eventual professional job far more expensive.

## What to Look for When Hiring a Mold Remediation Company

Mold remediation is not a licensed trade in most states, which means anyone can call themselves a remediation company. That makes vetting extra important.

**Minimum standards to verify:**
- IICRC certification (Institute of Inspection, Cleaning and Restoration Certification) or NORMI certification (National Organization of Remediators and Mold Inspectors)
- Proof of liability insurance and worker's compensation
- Written scope of work before any work begins
- Plan to fix the moisture source, not just remove visible mold
- Post-remediation clearance testing included or offered
- References from recent local jobs you can actually call

**Red flags to walk away from:**
- Unusually low bid (often means cutting corners on containment or disposal)
- Pressure to sign a contract same day
- Recommending testing AND remediation themselves (conflict of interest; use separate companies for each)
- No written warranty on their work
- Cash only payment

Get at least three written quotes. Pricing varies significantly even within the same city, and the cheapest bid is rarely the right choice for something that affects your family's health.

## How to Reduce the Cost of Mold Remediation

You cannot negotiate mold away, but you can reduce costs through timing and preparation.

**Before the contractor arrives:**
- Remove furniture and belongings from the affected area (many companies charge to move items)
- Document everything with photos for your insurance file
- Identify and stop the moisture source if you can (shut off a leaking supply line, patch a roof temporarily)
- Get competing quotes before choosing

**Choices that lower total project cost:**
- Addressing small mold problems before they spread (a $1,500 job today beats a $7,000 job next year)
- Installing bathroom exhaust fans that vent outside (prevents recurrence)
- Adding a crawl space vapor barrier as part of the same project (saves a second mobilization charge)
- Scheduling in off-peak months if the problem is not urgent (winter and early spring are slower for remediation companies in most regions)

## How Long Does Mold Remediation Take?

| Job Size | Typical Timeline |
|---|---|
| Minor (bathroom, small area) | 1 to 3 days |
| Moderate (basement, crawl space) | 3 to 5 days |
| Extensive (attic, multiple rooms) | 1 to 2 weeks |
| Severe (whole house, post-flood) | 2 to 6 weeks |

Your family should not occupy rooms being actively remediated. For extensive jobs, plan to stay elsewhere for at least part of the project. Some contractors can work room by room to minimize disruption, but containment protocols require sealing off work areas completely.

## After Remediation: Preventing Mold from Coming Back

Remediation without prevention is just buying yourself a few years before the next outbreak. The root cause is always moisture, and fixing moisture is cheaper than fighting mold twice.

The most effective prevention steps, ranked by impact:

1. **Fix the moisture source first.** No remediation holds if the leak, condensation, or groundwater intrusion is still there.
2. **Control indoor humidity.** The [EPA recommends](https://www.epa.gov/mold/controlling-mold) keeping indoor relative humidity between 30 and 60 percent. In humid climates, a whole-house dehumidifier (**$1,200 to $3,000 installed**) is a sound investment.
3. **Ensure bathroom fans vent outside.** Many older homes have fans ducted into the attic, which is a direct path to attic mold.
4. **Encapsulate the crawl space.** A sealed crawl space with a vapor barrier eliminates the most common mold vector in single-family homes.
5. **Check roof and gutter drainage.** Water pooling against the foundation is often the culprit in basement mold cases.
6. **Service your HVAC annually.** A clean evaporator coil and drain pan prevent HVAC mold from developing.
