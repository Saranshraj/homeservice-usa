Installing a French drain costs **$1,500 to $6,500 for most homeowners**, with the national average landing around $3,500. Interior basement systems run higher, from $3,000 to $18,000 for a full perimeter, while a simple exterior yard drain covering 50 feet might cost as little as $500 if you tackle it yourself. The price varies dramatically based on where you put it, how long it needs to be, and the soil conditions on your property.

If water is pooling in your yard after every rain, seeping into your basement, or drowning your foundation plantings, you are not alone. Drainage problems affect millions of American homes, and a French drain is one of the most reliable long-term solutions. Let's walk through exactly what you can expect to pay, and what drives those costs up or down.

---

## What Is a French Drain, Exactly?

A French drain is a trench filled with gravel and a perforated pipe that redirects groundwater away from a problem area. Named after Henry French, a Massachusetts farmer who wrote about the technique in 1859, the concept is simple: water follows the path of least resistance, and the gravel-filled trench creates that path, channeling water toward a drain outlet, dry well, or street.

There are two main types:

- **Exterior French drain:** Installed in your yard or around the perimeter of your foundation, outside the house. This is typically the less expensive option.
- **Interior French drain (basement system):** Installed beneath your basement floor along the perimeter walls. This requires breaking up concrete and is a much larger project, but it catches water that has already entered the foundation wall.

### By Linear Foot

Most contractors charge by the foot because labor and materials scale with length:

- **Simple exterior drain:** $15 to $35 per linear foot
- **Foundation exterior drain with membrane:** $25 to $50 per linear foot
- **Interior basement drain:** $50 to $100 per linear foot (includes concrete removal and replacement)

For a 100-foot exterior drain, expect to pay $1,500 to $3,500 total. A 200-foot system protecting a full foundation perimeter jumps to $3,000 to $7,000.

## What Affects the Final Price?

### 1. Depth of the Trench

Deeper trenches cost more to dig and require more gravel. A shallow yard drain might only need to go 18 inches deep, while a drain protecting a basement foundation may need to go 6 to 8 feet down to reach below the footing. Every extra foot of depth adds labor and disposal costs.

### 2. Soil Conditions

Rocky soil or heavy clay dramatically increases excavation time and equipment needs. If your contractor hits large rocks or thick clay, expect to pay 20 to 40 percent more than an initial estimate. Sandy or loose soil is easier to work with and cheaper to excavate.

### 3. Landscaping Disruption

A French drain installation will tear up your yard. Restoring sod, replanting shrubs, repairing walkways, or repouring a concrete section after an interior basement drain adds $500 to $3,000 to the total, depending on what gets damaged. Get this included in your quote upfront.

### 4. Outlet Location

Where does the water go when the drain collects it? The options are:
- **Street or storm drain:** Often the cheapest if accessible
- **Dry well:** A buried gravel-filled chamber, adds $500 to $2,000
- **Creek or natural outlet:** Can require permits and may add costs
- **Sump pump pit:** Ties into an existing pump system, usually straightforward

If your lot slopes naturally toward a street or woods, outlet costs are minimal. A flat lot in a subdivision may require a dry well or pump, which adds to the budget.

### 5. Permits

Many municipalities require permits for significant drainage work, especially if it connects to a storm sewer. Permits typically cost $50 to $400 and may require an inspection. Your contractor should handle this, but confirm it is included in the quote. Never skip the permit; unpermitted drainage work can cause problems when you sell your home.

### 6. Region and Labor Rates

Labor costs vary significantly by state. California, New York, and Washington contractors charge more than those in the South and Midwest. Here is a rough regional comparison for a typical 100-foot exterior drain:

## Interior Basement French Drain: A Bigger Investment

If water is actively entering your basement, an interior drainage system may be the right call. This is more invasive than an exterior drain but addresses the problem right at its source.

The process involves:
1. Jackhammering a strip of concrete along the perimeter walls (typically 12 to 18 inches wide)
2. Laying a perforated drain pipe in gravel
3. Routing water to a sump pit
4. Pouring new concrete over the system

A complete interior perimeter system for an average 1,200 square foot basement costs $8,000 to $15,000. Larger basements or homes with severe water intrusion problems can push costs to $18,000 or more.

Major interior drainage contractors like Basement Systems and WaterGuard offer warranties of 25 years or more. If your foundation is actively failing, this investment is often cheaper than foundation repair, which can run $20,000 to $100,000 for serious structural problems.

## DIY French Drain: Can You Do It Yourself?

A short, shallow exterior yard drain is within reach for a motivated DIYer. Materials for a 50-foot drain run $200 to $500:

- Perforated pipe (4-inch diameter): $0.50 to $1.50 per foot
- Drain rock or gravel: $30 to $60 per ton (you may need 2 to 4 tons)
- Landscape fabric: $30 to $80
- Drain outlet cap: $10 to $30

The hard part is the digging. A 100-foot, 2-foot-deep trench is roughly 17 cubic yards of soil to move. That is a serious weekend project, and renting a trencher costs $200 to $400 per day. You also need to determine where the water will go and make sure you are not accidentally directing it toward a neighbor's property or structure, which can create legal liability.

For anything deeper than 2 feet, close to your foundation, or connected to a city storm drain, hire a professional. The risk of a badly installed drain causing more water damage is real.

## Signs You Need a French Drain

You may want to call a drainage contractor if you notice:

- Standing water in your yard that does not drain within 24 to 48 hours after rain
- A basement that smells musty or shows efflorescence (white, chalky deposits on walls)
- Water seeping through basement walls or floor cracks
- Soil erosion on slopes in your yard
- Foundation plantings that die from "wet feet" repeatedly
- Cracks in your driveway or walkways caused by soil movement from poor drainage

Poor yard drainage left unchecked can lead to mold growth, foundation damage, and deteriorating landscaping, all of which cost far more to repair than a properly installed drain.

| Solution | Average Cost | Best For |
|----------|-------------|----------|
| French drain | $1,500 to $6,500 | Redirecting groundwater, foundation protection |

Often, the best solution is a combination. For example, extending your downspouts plus a short French drain near the foundation works better than either alone.

## How to Hire a Drainage Contractor

Not every landscaper or excavation company understands drainage engineering. Look for contractors who:

- Hold a state contractor's license (check your state licensing board's website)
- Have specific experience with drainage systems, not just general landscaping
- Provide a written plan showing where water will exit the system
- Pull permits when required
- Offer a warranty on their work (at least 2 to 5 years for exterior, longer for interior systems)

Get at least three written bids. Ask each contractor to show you examples of completed drainage projects and ask for references from customers who have been through a full rainy season with their system.

A red flag: any contractor who guarantees your yard will be completely dry after installation. No drainage system can control every weather event. A quality contractor will be honest about what results to realistically expect.

The National Association of Home Builders (NAHB) has resources for finding licensed contractors in your area. For interior basement waterproofing specifically, the Basement Health Association also maintains a list of member contractors.

## Tax and Insurance Considerations

In most cases, a French drain is a home improvement rather than a repair, so it does not qualify for an immediate tax deduction. However, it adds to your home's cost basis, which can reduce capital gains taxes when you sell. Keep all receipts and contractor invoices.

From a homeowners insurance perspective, standard policies generally do not cover damage from gradual water seepage or poor drainage. If your basement floods because of an ongoing drainage problem, your insurer may deny the claim. Installing a French drain before a problem worsens is a proactive step that protects your property and your insurance coverage.

Some states offer programs for homeowners dealing with basement flooding. FEMA's flood mitigation programs may also provide assistance if your property is in a designated flood zone. Check your state's emergency management agency website for current programs.

## Getting the Most Out of Your French Drain

Once your drain is in, a bit of maintenance keeps it working for decades:

- Flush the pipe with a garden hose annually to clear sediment
- Keep the outlet clear of debris and leaves
- Watch for sinkholes or settling above the trench, which can indicate a pipe failure
- If you plant over the drain, avoid trees with aggressive roots (like willows) that can infiltrate the pipe

A properly installed French drain with quality materials can last 30 to 40 years. Cheaper installations with lower-grade pipe or too little gravel may clog within 5 to 10 years.
