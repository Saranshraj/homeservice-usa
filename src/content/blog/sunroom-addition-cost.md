A sunroom addition costs **$15,000 to $75,000** for most US homeowners in 2026, with the national average sitting around **$30,000 to $45,000** for a standard three-season or four-season room. A basic screened porch can come in as low as **$5,000 to $15,000**, while a fully insulated, climate-controlled all-season sunroom with premium windows and a dedicated HVAC system can reach **$80,000 to $120,000** in high-cost markets. The number that matters most is the type of room you want, because a screen enclosure and a four-season glass room are almost completely different projects with completely different price tags, structural requirements, and long-term costs.

If you are also considering a full [home addition](/blog/home-addition-cost/), sunroom costs may be a starting point before you go bigger. Many homeowners pair a sunroom with [outdoor lighting installation](/blog/outdoor-lighting-installation-cost/) and a [new patio](/blog/patio-installation-cost/) to create a complete outdoor living space. Before signing any contract, read our [guide to finding and vetting a contractor](/blog/how-to-find-good-contractor/), since sunroom projects attract a lot of fly-by-night installers.

## Sunroom Cost by Type: The Most Important Variable

The type of sunroom you choose is by far the biggest cost driver. Here is how the four main types break down in 2026:

| Sunroom Type | Typical Cost Range | Best For |
|---|---|---|
| Screen porch / patio enclosure | $5,000 to $15,000 | Keeping bugs out, mild climates |
| Three-season room | $15,000 to $40,000 | Spring, summer, fall use |
| Four-season room (insulated) | $35,000 to $75,000 | Year-round use in most climates |
| All-season / custom build | $50,000 to $120,000+ | Full living space, extreme climates |
| Prefab/kit sunroom | $8,000 to $30,000 | Budget-friendly, faster install |

### Screen Porch / Patio Enclosure: $5,000 to $15,000

A screened porch is the most affordable option. You are essentially building a lightweight frame and adding fiberglass or aluminum screening. There is no insulation, no HVAC, and typically no heating. These rooms are usable roughly six to eight months a year in most US climates. If you live in Florida, Georgia, Texas, or another warm-weather state, a screened porch might be the only sunroom you ever need. Expect to pay **$15 to $25 per square foot** for a basic screen enclosure.

### Three-Season Room: $15,000 to $40,000

A three-season room typically uses single-pane or tempered glass panels, has basic weatherstripping, and may include a portable electric space heater in cooler months. It is not insulated to the standards of your home's living space and is not connected to your central HVAC. You can enjoy it comfortably from roughly April through October in most northern states. Costs run **$100 to $200 per square foot** depending on size, materials, and site conditions.

### Four-Season / All-Season Room: $35,000 to $75,000

This is the most popular upgrade for homeowners who want genuine year-round living space. A true four-season room is built to the same standards as the rest of your home: insulated walls, insulated or thermally broken framing, double-pane or triple-pane low-E windows, a connection to your home's existing HVAC or a dedicated mini-split system, and a proper roof. These rooms add real square footage to your home and appear on your property records. Expect to pay **$200 to $400 per square foot** fully finished.

A four-season sunroom also triggers a building permit in nearly every US jurisdiction, requires foundation work, and in some states must meet specific energy code requirements. The [U.S. Department of Energy](https://www.energy.gov) publishes window performance guidelines that your contractor should follow, particularly for homes in Climate Zones 4 through 7 (most of the northern half of the country).

## Sunroom Cost by Size

Size is the second biggest driver after type. Here are typical cost ranges for a four-season room at national average labor rates:

| Size (sq ft) | Three-Season Cost | Four-Season Cost |
|---|---|---|
| 100 sq ft (10x10) | $10,000 to $20,000 | $20,000 to $40,000 |
| 150 sq ft (10x15) | $15,000 to $30,000 | $30,000 to $60,000 |
| 200 sq ft (10x20) | $20,000 to $40,000 | $40,000 to $80,000 |
| 300 sq ft (15x20) | $30,000 to $60,000 | $60,000 to $120,000 |

Most homeowners build sunrooms in the 150 to 250 square foot range. That size hits a sweet spot: large enough to feel like a real room, small enough to keep costs manageable and to heat and cool efficiently.

## What Drives the Cost Up (and Down)

### Foundation Type

Your sunroom needs a solid base. Costs vary depending on what is already there:

- **Existing concrete patio slab (in good condition):** No foundation cost, saves $3,000 to $8,000
- **New concrete slab poured:** $3,000 to $7,000 for a typical sunroom size
- **Pier-and-beam foundation:** $4,000 to $10,000, often needed on sloped lots
- **Stem wall with footings:** $6,000 to $15,000, required in many northern climates where frost lines run deep

Northern states like Minnesota, Wisconsin, Michigan, and Maine require foundations that go below the frost line, often 42 to 60 inches deep. That adds thousands to the foundation alone compared to a similar project in Georgia or Arizona.

### Windows and Glazing

Windows typically represent 30 to 40 percent of a sunroom's total cost. Your choices have a massive impact on energy bills and comfort:

- **Single-pane glass:** Cheapest upfront, but terrible for insulation; fine for screen porches, not recommended for four-season use
- **Double-pane low-E glass:** The baseline for four-season rooms; reduces heat gain in summer and heat loss in winter; look for [ENERGY STAR certified windows](https://www.energystar.gov) for your climate zone
- **Triple-pane glass:** Adds 20 to 40 percent to window costs but reduces heat loss by another 20 to 30 percent; most valuable in Climate Zones 5 to 7 (northern US)
- **Tempered glass:** Required by building codes in many locations; adds safety without significantly affecting energy performance

Budget roughly **$200 to $600 per window** installed for double-pane low-E, or **$400 to $1,000 per window** for triple-pane.

### Roofing Options

Your sunroom roof dramatically affects the feel of the space:

- **Solid roof (matches house):** Most expensive but best insulation, blends with the home; adds $10,000 to $25,000 depending on size and materials
- **Polycarbonate panels:** Translucent, lets light through, budget-friendly ($5,000 to $12,000); can be noisy in rain, less energy-efficient
- **Tempered glass roof:** Premium look and maximum light; $15,000 to $35,000 for a full glass ceiling; requires UV-blocking coatings to control heat
- **Aluminum pan roof:** Often used on three-season rooms; moderate cost, decent durability

### HVAC and Heating

A four-season room needs its own heating and cooling solution:

- **Extend existing HVAC:** $1,500 to $4,000 if your current system has enough capacity; requires ductwork
- **Mini-split system (recommended):** $3,000 to $7,000 installed; see our [mini-split installation cost guide](/blog/mini-split-installation-cost/) for full details; highly efficient and does not stress your existing system
- **Electric baseboard heat:** $500 to $1,500 to install; cheap upfront but expensive to operate
- **Radiant floor heating:** $3,000 to $8,000; luxurious, even heat; works best with a concrete slab floor

## Sunroom Costs by State

Labor rates vary significantly across the US. Here are rough cost multipliers for a $35,000 national average four-season room:

| State/Region | Cost Multiplier | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| California (Bay Area, LA) | 1.3 to 1.5x | High labor and material costs |
| New York, New Jersey | 1.2 to 1.4x | Strict permit requirements |
| Florida | 0.9 to 1.0x | Lower labor; hurricane-rated materials required |
| Texas | 0.85 to 0.95x | Lower labor costs |
| Midwest (OH, IN, IL) | 0.9 to 1.0x | Near national average |
| Minnesota, Wisconsin | 1.0 to 1.15x | Deep frost foundations add cost |
| Southeast (GA, SC, NC) | 0.85 to 0.95x | Milder winters reduce HVAC needs |
| Pacific Northwest (WA, OR) | 1.1 to 1.25x | High labor, rain management required |
| Mountain West (CO, UT) | 1.0 to 1.1x | Near average; altitude affects some materials |

Florida homeowners face a special consideration: the Florida Building Code requires impact-resistant or hurricane-rated glazing in wind zones. This can add **$3,000 to $10,000** to window costs compared to an identical room in, say, Kansas. The [Florida Department of Business and Professional Regulation](https://www.myfloridalicense.com) publishes contractor licensing requirements you should verify before hiring anyone.

## Permits and HOA Approval

A sunroom addition almost always requires a building permit. Costs and timelines vary, but budget:

- **Building permit:** $300 to $1,500 depending on jurisdiction and project value
- **Structural drawings/engineering:** $500 to $2,000 if required by your local building department
- **HOA approval:** Free to apply, but can take 30 to 90 days; your HOA may have restrictions on materials, colors, and proximity to property lines

Never skip the permit. An unpermitted sunroom can create serious problems when you sell your home, and your homeowner's insurance may deny claims related to an unpermitted structure. The [National Association of Home Builders](https://www.nahb.org) strongly recommends confirming permit requirements with your local building department before any work starts.

## Does a Sunroom Add Home Value?

A sunroom typically recoups 50 to 70 percent of its cost at resale according to industry data, which is less than a kitchen remodel but more than a swimming pool. A four-season room that adds genuine square footage to your home performs better at resale than a three-season or screen porch, which buyers often view as a seasonal amenity rather than living space.

The value equation is stronger in:
- **Cold climates** where year-round use is a real selling point
- **Markets where outdoor living space commands a premium** (think western states)
- **Homes that are undersized** for their neighborhood where the extra square footage closes a gap

## Prefab vs. Custom Build: Which Is Right for You?

**Prefab/kit sunrooms** ($8,000 to $30,000) use factory-manufactured aluminum or vinyl framing systems with pre-cut glass panels. Companies like Patio Enclosures, Sunroom by Brady, and Florida Glass offer systems that can be installed in days. Pros: lower cost, faster installation, predictable appearance. Cons: less customization, may not match your home's architecture, some kits are not rated for four-season use.

**Custom-built sunrooms** ($25,000 to $120,000) are framed like a regular room addition by a general contractor. They match your home's existing roofline, materials, and style. They can be designed to any specification and built to any energy efficiency standard. Pros: looks like part of the original house, maximum durability, full customization. Cons: higher cost, longer timeline (six weeks to four months), requires a GC rather than a specialty installer.

## Red Flags When Getting Quotes

Sunroom installation is an industry with a lot of pressure-sales tactics and aggressive salespeople. Here is what to watch for:

- **"Today only" pricing or discounts that expire tonight:** A legitimate contractor does not price work that way.
- **No physical business address or local phone number:** Always verify a contractor has a real presence in your area.
- **No written contract with itemized scope:** Never agree to a verbal quote.
- **Asking for more than 30 percent down before any work starts:** The [Federal Trade Commission](https://www.ftc.gov) flags large upfront payments as a common contractor scam pattern.
- **No mention of permits:** If a contractor says your project does not need a permit, call your local building department and verify independently.

## Getting the Best Price: Practical Tips

1. **Get three written quotes** from licensed, insured contractors. For a $40,000 project, quotes can vary by $10,000 or more.
2. **Schedule in the off-season.** Contractors are often less busy from November through February in northern states, and you may get better availability and pricing.
3. **Leverage an existing patio.** If you have a concrete patio in good condition, it can serve as your sunroom floor slab, saving several thousand dollars on foundation costs.
4. **Choose a simple footprint.** A square or rectangular room is significantly cheaper than an angled or curved design.
5. **Ask about financing.** Many sunroom companies offer financing through third-party lenders. Compare their rates to a [home equity line of credit](/blog/how-to-finance-home-repairs/) before signing, since contractor-arranged financing often carries higher interest rates.
