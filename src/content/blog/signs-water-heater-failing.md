**The short answer:** The clearest signs your water heater is about to fail are rusty water coming from hot taps, pooling water around the base, rumbling or popping noises from the tank, water that never gets fully hot, and a unit that is more than 10 to 12 years old. If you notice two or more of these signs at once, start shopping for a replacement now rather than waiting for a full failure that could flood your basement or garage.

A water heater failure is one of those homeowner nightmares that feels sudden but almost never is. In most cases, the unit has been giving you warnings for weeks or months. The problem is that most people do not know what to look for until they wake up to a cold shower or a flooded utility room. This guide walks you through every warning sign, what each one means, and whether you should repair or replace.

If you are already past the warning stage and need to know what a new unit will cost, our [water heater replacement cost guide](/blog/water-heater-replacement-cost/) has the full breakdown.

## Sign 1: Your Water Heater Is Over 10 Years Old

This is the single most reliable predictor of failure. The average tank water heater lasts 8 to 12 years. If yours is approaching or past the 10-year mark, it is living on borrowed time regardless of whether it seems fine right now.

**How to find the age:** Look at the serial number on the manufacturer's label (usually on the upper portion of the tank). Most brands encode the manufacture date in the first few characters. For example, a serial number starting with "F12" typically means June 2012 (F = 6th month). If you cannot decode it, search "[your brand] serial number date code" online or call the manufacturer.

**What to do:** If the unit is over 10 years old and working fine, start budgeting for a replacement. You have time to shop around, compare quotes, and choose the right type. A planned replacement on your schedule costs 20 to 30 percent less than an emergency replacement when you have no hot water and need a plumber today.

## Sign 2: Rusty or Discolored Hot Water

When you turn on only the hot water tap and see rusty, brown, or yellowish water, that usually means the inside of your tank is corroding. The anode rod, a sacrificial metal rod inside the tank that protects the steel lining, has likely been fully consumed.

**Important distinction:** If you see discolored water from both hot AND cold taps, the problem might be your pipes rather than the water heater. Run only the hot water for 2 to 3 minutes. If the rust clears up, it is likely just sediment. If it persists and only comes from the hot side, the tank is corroding internally.

**Can you fix it?** If the anode rod has not been replaced in 3 to 5 years, a new anode rod ($100 to $200 installed) might solve the problem temporarily. But if the tank is already over 8 years old and showing rust, the corrosion has likely progressed beyond what a new rod can stop.

## Sign 3: Rumbling, Popping, or Banging Noises

A water heater that sounds like a popcorn machine or a bubbling pot is telling you something important. Those noises come from sediment buildup at the bottom of the tank. Over years of use, minerals in your water settle into a layer of hardened sediment. When the burner heats the water beneath this layer, it creates steam bubbles that pop through the sediment.

**Why this matters beyond just noise:** Sediment acts as an insulating layer between the burner and the water. This means the burner has to work harder and run longer, which overheats the tank bottom. Over time, this accelerates metal fatigue and increases the risk of a tank leak.

**Can you fix it?** Flushing the tank may help if the sediment has not hardened too much. A professional flush costs $100 to $200. If the noises started recently and the unit is under 8 years old, a flush is worth trying. If the unit is older and the noises have been worsening for months, flushing probably will not help, and the damage to the tank bottom may already be done.

## Sign 4: Water Pooling Around the Base

Any water around the base of your water heater demands immediate attention. This could indicate a slow leak from the tank itself, which means the inner lining has cracked or corroded through.

**Before you panic, check these first:**
- The temperature and pressure (T&P) relief valve on the side of the tank. If it is dripping, it may need replacement ($20 part, $150 to $250 installed) or may be responding to excessive pressure.
- The inlet and outlet fittings at the top of the tank. Loose connections can drip down the side and pool at the base.
- Condensation. In humid climates, condensation on a cold tank can create puddles, especially when a new tank is first installed.

**If the leak is from the tank body itself:** This cannot be repaired. A cracked or corroded tank will only get worse, and a full rupture can release 40 to 80 gallons of water into your home in minutes. If the tank is leaking, replace it as soon as possible.

## Sign 5: Inconsistent or Lukewarm Water Temperature

If your showers are not getting as hot as they used to, or the hot water runs out much faster than it should, several things could be happening:

**For gas water heaters:**
- Thermostat has drifted or failed (repairable, $150 to $300)
- Dip tube has broken, mixing cold incoming water with hot water at the top of the tank (repairable, $150 to $300)
- Sediment buildup reducing heating efficiency (flushable if caught early)
- Burner assembly issues (repairable if unit is newer)

**For electric water heaters:**
- One of two heating elements has failed, cutting your effective hot water capacity in half (repairable, $150 to $300 per element)
- Thermostat calibration is off (repairable, $100 to $200)
- Sediment covering the lower element (may be flushable)

**When to repair vs. replace:** If the unit is under 7 years old and the issue is a single component like a heating element, thermostat, or dip tube, repair makes sense. Total repair cost should be less than half the cost of a new unit. ## Sign 6: The T&P Relief Valve Keeps Opening

The temperature and pressure relief valve is a safety device on every water heater. It opens to release water if the temperature exceeds 210 degrees or pressure exceeds 150 psi. If this valve is frequently discharging water, something is wrong.

**Possible causes:**
- Thermal expansion in a closed plumbing system (fixable with an expansion tank, $50 to $150)
- Excessively high water pressure from the main (fixable with a pressure reducing valve, $200 to $400 installed)
- The T&P valve itself is faulty (replaceable for $100 to $200)
- The water heater thermostat is malfunctioning and overheating the water (potentially dangerous)

**Warning:** Never cap, plug, or remove a T&P valve. This valve prevents your water heater from becoming a pressure bomb. If it keeps opening and basic fixes do not solve the issue, call a licensed plumber immediately.

## Sign 7: Visible Corrosion on the Tank or Fittings

Inspect the exterior of your tank, especially around the inlet and outlet connections at the top and the area around the T&P valve. Visible rust, flaking metal, or green corrosion on fittings are warning signs.

**Fitting corrosion** can usually be addressed by replacing the corroded fittings and connections ($100 to $300). This is normal wear.

**Tank body corrosion** is a different story. If you see rust or corrosion on the tank itself, especially on the bottom, the protective lining is failing and the tank will eventually leak. This is not repairable.

## Sign 8: Your Energy Bills Have Spiked

A water heater that is losing efficiency will run longer and more frequently to keep water at temperature. If your gas or electric bill has increased noticeably and you have not changed your usage habits, your aging water heater could be the cause.

According to the [U.S. Department of Energy](https://www.energy.gov/energysaver/water-heating), water heating accounts for about 18 percent of a typical home's energy costs. An inefficient unit nearing the end of its life can increase that percentage significantly.

Upgrading to a heat pump water heater can cut water heating costs by 50 to 70 percent. With the current $600 federal tax credit from the Inflation Reduction Act, the payback period on a heat pump unit is often 3 to 4 years. ## Emergency Steps If Your Water Heater Fails

If your water heater is actively leaking or has stopped working entirely:

1. **Turn off the power.** For gas units, turn the gas valve to "off." For electric units, flip the breaker at your electrical panel.
2. **Turn off the water supply.** There should be a shutoff valve on the cold water inlet pipe above the tank. Turn it clockwise to close.
3. **Open a hot water faucet.** This relieves pressure in the tank and helps prevent additional leaking.
4. **Drain the tank if needed.** Attach a garden hose to the drain valve at the bottom and route it outside or to a floor drain.
5. **Call a licensed plumber.** Get at least two quotes if the situation allows. Even in an emergency, a 30-minute phone call can save you hundreds of dollars.
6. **Check your [homeowners insurance](/blog/homeowners-insurance-claims/).** Water heater failures that cause water damage are often covered under standard homeowner policies. Document the damage with photos before cleanup.

## The Repair vs. Replace Decision

Here is a simple framework:

| Factor | Lean Toward Repair | Lean Toward Replace |
|---|---|---|
| Age | Under 7 years | Over 10 years |
| Repair cost | Under $300 | Over $500 |
| Previous repairs | None or one | Two or more in past 2 years |
| Type of problem | Single component (element, thermostat) | Tank leak, widespread corrosion |
| Energy costs | Normal | Noticeably increasing |
| Hot water needs | Staying the same | Growing (more people, home addition) |

**The 50 percent rule:** If the repair costs more than 50 percent of a new unit, replace it. A $400 repair on a $1,200 unit that is 9 years old is throwing good money after bad.

## How to Make Your Water Heater Last Longer

If your unit is still healthy, these maintenance steps can add years to its life:

- **Flush the tank annually** to remove sediment. This is a 30-minute DIY task. The [Department of Energy recommends it](https://www.energy.gov/energysaver/maintaining-your-water-heater) for all tank water heaters.
- **Replace the anode rod every 3 to 5 years.** This $100 to $200 service is the single most impactful maintenance you can do.
- **Check the T&P valve annually.** Lift the lever briefly to make sure it operates freely, then let it snap back. If it drips afterward, it may need replacement.
- **Keep the area around the water heater clear.** At least 12 inches of clearance on all sides for ventilation and access.
- **Set the thermostat to 120 degrees.** This is the [DOE's recommended setting](https://www.energy.gov/energysaver/water-heating) for both safety and efficiency.
