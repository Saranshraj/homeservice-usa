Professional landscaping costs **$1,200 to $5,500** for a typical US homeowner tackling a standard front or back yard. If you are planning a full landscape design with hardscaping, planting, and irrigation, expect to spend anywhere from $8,000 to $15,000 or more. For ongoing lawn care and maintenance, most homeowners pay **$100 to $250 per month**. These numbers vary a lot depending on your yard size, your region, the materials you choose, and whether you hire a landscape architect or a general lawn crew.

You are not alone if sticker shock hit you the moment you started getting quotes. Landscaping is one of the most underestimated home projects, and prices have climbed 18 to 25 percent over the past few years due to labor shortages and material costs. This guide breaks down every major category so you know exactly what you are paying for before you sign anything.

## Average Landscaping Costs by Project Type

Different landscaping jobs live in very different price categories. Here is a breakdown of the most common projects:

| Project | Low Cost | High Cost | Average |
|---|---|---|---|
| Full landscape design (front + back) | $5,000 | $25,000 | $12,000 |
| Front yard only | $1,500 | $6,000 | $3,200 |
| Back yard only | $2,500 | $10,000 | $5,000 |
| Lawn seeding or sodding | $500 | $4,000 | $1,800 |
| Mulch installation | $150 | $600 | $300 |
| Tree and shrub planting | $200 | $3,000 | $800 |
| Garden bed creation | $300 | $1,500 | $700 |
| Irrigation system | $1,800 | $5,000 | $3,200 |
| Ongoing maintenance (monthly) | $80 | $350 | $175 |
| Seasonal cleanup | $150 | $600 | $300 |

Prices above reflect a quarter-acre lot in a mid-cost US city. If you are in a high-cost area like the Bay Area, Seattle, or the Northeast, add 25 to 50 percent. If you are in a lower-cost region like the Midwest or rural South, subtract 15 to 20 percent.

## Breaking Down What You Actually Pay For

### Labor Costs

Labor is the single biggest line item in landscaping, typically making up **50 to 70 percent** of the total bill. A solo landscaper charges $45 to $85 per hour. A full crew from an established landscaping company runs $100 to $200 per hour.

Landscape architects, who design complex plans and supervise installation, charge $50 to $150 per hour or sometimes charge a flat fee of 10 to 20 percent of total project cost.

If your project requires design permits, expect to add $500 to $2,000 depending on your municipality.

### Plants and Materials

Plants are expensive and prices have risen sharply. Here is what you can expect to pay at a nursery or through a landscaper:

- **Small shrubs (1-gallon container):** $10 to $30 each
- **Medium shrubs (5-gallon container):** $30 to $80 each
- **Large specimen plants (15+ gallon):** $150 to $500 each
- **Ornamental trees:** $200 to $1,500 depending on size and species
- **Perennial flowers (1 flat):** $40 to $80 per flat of 18 plants
- **Mulch:** $35 to $75 per cubic yard delivered
- **Topsoil:** $20 to $50 per cubic yard

Landscapers typically mark up materials 20 to 50 percent above wholesale. If you want to save money, some contractors will agree to an owner-supplied materials arrangement, but not all will.

### Hardscaping vs. Softscaping

Your quote may separate "softscaping" (plants, lawn, beds) from "hardscaping" (patios, walkways, walls, edging). Hardscaping almost always costs more per square foot because it is more labor-intensive and uses permanent materials.

- **Concrete walkway:** $8 to $20 per square foot
- **Paver patio:** $15 to $35 per square foot
- **Natural stone patio:** $25 to $80 per square foot
- **Retaining wall:** $20 to $100 per square foot depending on material
- **Decorative edging (steel, aluminum):** $3 to $10 per linear foot

If you are planning a major patio build, check out our article on [patio installation costs](/blog/patio-installation-cost) for a deeper breakdown.

## How Yard Size Affects the Total Price

Landscapers often price by the square foot for installation or by the hour for maintenance. Here is a rough estimate based on yard size:

| Lot Size | Basic Landscaping | Full Landscape Renovation |
|---|---|---|
| Small (under 2,000 sq ft) | $1,200 to $3,500 | $4,000 to $8,000 |
| Medium (2,000 to 5,000 sq ft) | $3,000 to $7,000 | $8,000 to $18,000 |
| Large (5,000 to 10,000 sq ft) | $6,000 to $14,000 | $15,000 to $30,000 |
| Very large (over 10,000 sq ft) | $12,000+ | $25,000+ |

These are ballpark ranges. Your actual cost depends heavily on the condition of your current yard (is it flat or sloped?), existing tree coverage, soil quality, and local water access.

## State-by-State Pricing Differences

Where you live makes a big difference. Here are approximate cost multipliers compared to the national average:

**Above average cost states:**
- California: 30 to 50% above average
- New York and New Jersey: 25 to 40% above average
- Washington state: 20 to 35% above average
- Massachusetts and Connecticut: 20 to 30% above average

**Near average:**
- Texas, Florida, Georgia: roughly average to 10% above
- Illinois, Ohio, Michigan: roughly average
- Colorado: 10 to 20% above average

**Below average cost states:**
- Mississippi, Arkansas, Alabama: 15 to 25% below average
- Kansas, Nebraska, Iowa: 10 to 20% below average
- West Virginia, Kentucky: 15 to 25% below average

Regional water costs also affect long-term landscaping decisions. In drought-prone states like Arizona, Nevada, New Mexico, and California, native and drought-resistant plantings are not just trendy, they can save you $600 to $1,500 per year in water bills. Some states and water districts offer rebates for removing grass and replacing it with drought-tolerant landscapes. Check your local water utility or visit the [EPA WaterSense program](https://www.epa.gov/watersense) for rebate information in your area.

## Lawn Care and Maintenance Costs

If you already have a yard and just need ongoing upkeep, the pricing looks very different from a full renovation.

**Basic lawn care services:**
- **Weekly mowing:** $35 to $80 per visit (most companies charge per visit or monthly)
- **Monthly maintenance package:** $120 to $350 (mowing, edging, blowing)
- **Fertilization (per treatment):** $50 to $150 depending on yard size
- **Aeration:** $75 to $250
- **Overseeding:** $100 to $400
- **Leaf removal (seasonal):** $150 to $500 per session
- **Spring cleanup:** $175 to $600

For year-round lawn care on a half-acre lot, expect to pay $1,500 to $4,000 annually if you hire a full-service company.

## DIY Landscaping: What You Can Do Yourself

Many homeowners do a hybrid approach: hire pros for the heavy work and handle the simpler tasks themselves. Here is an honest look at what is DIY-friendly versus what benefits from professional help.

**Good DIY projects:**
- Planting small shrubs, perennials, and annuals
- Spreading mulch and compost
- Seeding bare patches in your lawn
- Installing garden bed edging
- Basic pruning and shrub trimming
- Weeding and general bed maintenance

**Leave it to professionals:**
- Grading and drainage work (incorrect slope can cause water damage to your foundation)
- Large tree planting and removal
- Irrigation system design and installation
- Retaining walls over 2 feet tall (many states require permits for taller walls)
- Sod installation on large areas
- Any work near buried utilities (call 811 before you dig)

The [National Association of Home Builders (NAHB)](https://www.nahb.org) estimates that professional landscaping adds 5 to 12 percent to home resale value, making it one of the better-returning home improvement investments. That said, the return depends heavily on staying within neighborhood norms. A $30,000 landscape on a block where homes sell for $250,000 is unlikely to return that investment at sale.

## How to Hire a Landscaper Without Getting Burned

The landscaping industry has a high number of unlicensed operators and seasonal workers who disappear after taking your deposit. Here are the steps to protect yourself.

**Before hiring:**
1. Get at least three written quotes for any project over $500.
2. Verify the contractor holds a valid contractor's license in your state. Most states require landscapers doing grading, drainage, or irrigation work to be licensed. Check your state licensing board or visit the [NAHB contractor licensing resources](https://www.nahb.org).
3. Ask for proof of general liability insurance (minimum $1 million) and workers' compensation if they have employees.
4. Check reviews on Google, Houzz, and the Better Business Bureau.
5. Ask for references from projects completed in the last 12 months and call them.

**Red flags to watch for:**
- Asking for more than 30 to 40 percent upfront before work starts
- Providing only a verbal quote with no written contract
- Pressure to sign today to get a special price
- No physical business address (just a phone number)
- Offering to skip permits to save money

**What your contract should include:**
- Detailed scope of work with plant species, sizes, and quantities
- Payment schedule tied to project milestones, not arbitrary dates
- Start and completion dates
- Who is responsible for plant replacement if something dies within 90 days
- Cleanup and debris removal terms

## Financing Your Landscaping Project

Landscaping is not typically covered by home equity loans or renovation loans the way structural work is. But you have options.

**Common financing paths:**
- **Home equity line of credit (HELOC):** Good if you have equity. Rates in 2026 run 7 to 11 percent. Interest may be tax-deductible if improvements qualify; check with a tax professional or review [IRS Publication 936](https://www.irs.gov/pub/irs-pdf/p936.pdf).
- **Personal loan:** Easier to get than HELOC, no home equity required, but rates are higher at 9 to 24 percent.
- **Credit card with 0% intro APR:** Works for smaller projects under $3,000 if you can pay off before the intro period ends.
- **Contractor financing:** Many larger landscaping companies offer payment plans, sometimes with low or no interest for a promotional period.

Some states and municipalities offer low-interest loans or grants for specific landscaping improvements that reduce stormwater runoff or improve energy efficiency through shade tree planting. The [EPA's Green Infrastructure resources](https://www.epa.gov/green-infrastructure) can point you toward programs in your area.

## Seasonal Timing: When to Save the Most Money

The timing of your landscaping project affects the price. Most landscapers are busiest from April through June and again in September. You can often negotiate discounts of 10 to 20 percent if you schedule work in:

- **Late July and August:** Many crews have open slots during the summer heat slowdown.
- **October and November:** Fall is a great time for planting trees and shrubs, and crews are often hungry for work after the spring rush.
- **Late winter (February to March):** For projects that can be planned ahead, signing a contract in winter often gets you priority scheduling and a lower price.

## Signs Your Landscaping Needs Professional Help

You can do a lot of maintenance yourself, but certain yard problems really do need a pro.

- **Persistent soggy areas or pooling water:** Poor drainage can damage your foundation over time. You may need grading, French drains, or a dry creek bed.
- **Slope erosion:** Bare slopes losing soil need erosion-control plants, terracing, or retaining walls before they cause bigger problems.
- **Trees showing disease or structural failure risks:** An arborist certified by the [International Society of Arboriculture (ISA)](https://www.isa-arbor.com) can assess safety and treatment options.
- **Irrigation system failures:** Broken heads, soggy spots despite no rain, or a spike in your water bill all signal irrigation problems that need licensed diagnosis.
