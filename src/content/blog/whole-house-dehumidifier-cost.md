A whole-house dehumidifier costs **$1,200 to $3,800 installed** for most US homeowners in 2026, with the national average landing around **$1,900**. The unit itself runs $700 to $2,500 depending on capacity and brand, and professional installation adds $300 to $700 on top. If you live in a humid climate like the Southeast, Gulf Coast, or coastal Northeast, this investment pays for itself in lower cooling bills, reduced mold risk, and a home that simply feels more comfortable. If you are in a drier climate like the Mountain West or parts of the Midwest, you may never need one at all.

This guide breaks down exactly what drives the cost, which type is right for your situation, and how to avoid overpaying.

---

## Why Humidity Is Such a Big Deal for Homeowners

You can feel high humidity before you see any damage. That sticky, heavy feeling in a basement, the musty smell in a closet, the condensation on basement walls in summer. These are early warnings. Left unaddressed, indoor relative humidity above 60 percent creates the conditions that wood rot, mold, dust mites, and structural damage need to thrive.

The [Environmental Protection Agency recommends](https://www.epa.gov/mold/brief-guide-mold-moisture-and-your-home) keeping indoor humidity between 30 and 50 percent. During summer in a humid climate, outdoor humidity can hit 80 to 90 percent, and without active dehumidification, your home cannot come close to that target. The [American Lung Association](https://www.lung.org/clean-air/at-home/indoor-air-pollutants/dampness-and-mold) links chronic exposure to damp, moldy environments to respiratory illness, particularly in children, the elderly, and anyone with asthma.

If you are already dealing with water intrusion, make sure to address the root cause first. No dehumidifier can compensate for a cracked foundation or a failed sump pump. See our guides on [basement waterproofing cost](/blog/basement-waterproofing-cost/) and [sump pump installation cost](/blog/sump-pump-installation-cost/) before investing in dehumidification.

## The Three Main Types Explained

### 1. Portable and Room Dehumidifiers ($150 to $500)

These plug into a standard outlet and treat a single room or area. They work fine for a dry summer or occasional basement moisture issues, but they have real limitations. You have to empty the water reservoir manually every day or two (or run a drain hose), they are noisy, and they only treat the space they are sitting in.

For whole-home coverage, you would need multiple units, and the ongoing inconvenience adds up. These make sense as a temporary fix or for mild, localized humidity problems in a single room.

### 2. Freestanding Basement Dehumidifiers ($900 to $1,800 Installed)

This is the sweet spot for most homeowners with a damp basement. These units sit directly in the basement, draw out moisture continuously, and drain automatically via gravity or a built-in pump. Brands like Santa Fe and Aprilaire make units designed specifically for the cooler temperatures and high moisture loads typical of basements.

Unlike consumer-grade portable units, these are built to run 24 hours a day for years. They also operate at temperatures as low as 33 degrees Fahrenheit, which matters if your basement gets cold in winter. Installation is usually straightforward: plug in, connect a drain line, and set the target humidity level. Most HVAC contractors or basement waterproofing companies can handle this in a few hours.

### 3. Ducted Whole-House Dehumidifiers ($1,500 to $3,200 Installed)

A ducted unit integrates directly into your home's HVAC ductwork, treating conditioned air throughout the entire house from one central location. This is the gold standard for whole-home humidity control. It runs quietly (you barely know it is there), requires almost no maintenance, and keeps every room at a consistent humidity level.

The tradeoff is cost and installation complexity. You need an HVAC professional who can splice into existing ductwork and configure the unit to work with your existing thermostat or add a separate humidistat. If you are upgrading or replacing your HVAC system, adding a ducted dehumidifier at the same time saves significantly on labor. See our [HVAC maintenance cost guide](/blog/hvac-maintenance-cost/) for context on broader system costs.

### 4. Crawl Space Dehumidifiers ($1,250 to $2,400 Installed)

Crawl spaces are notoriously humid. Soil moisture wicks up through exposed dirt, and because crawl spaces are poorly ventilated, that moisture goes straight into the floor structure above. A dedicated crawl space dehumidifier combined with a vapor barrier is a common recommendation in the Southeast and coastal regions.

These units are designed to handle extreme humidity and are usually paired with [crawl space encapsulation](/blog/crawl-space-encapsulation-cost/), which costs an additional $1,500 to $5,000. Together, they dramatically reduce wood rot risk and improve air quality in the living space above.

## What Drives the Cost Up or Down

### Unit Capacity (Pints Per Day)

Dehumidifiers are rated by how many pints of water they remove per day. A small basement unit might handle 70 pints per day. Commercial-grade whole-house units reach 130 to 200 pints per day. More capacity costs more upfront but may let you run the unit less aggressively, saving on electricity over time.

A rough guide:
- Basement up to 1,500 sq ft, moderately humid: 70 to 90 pints per day
- Whole house under 2,000 sq ft, humid climate: 90 to 130 pints per day
- Whole house over 2,500 sq ft or very humid climate: 130 pints or more per day

### Brand and Quality Tier

The dehumidifier market splits into consumer-grade (brands like hOmeLabs, Frigidaire, and GE sold at big-box stores) and professional-grade (Aprilaire, Santa Fe, Therma-Stor). Professional units cost two to four times more upfront but are built for continuous duty, come with longer warranties, and perform reliably in the temperature ranges and humidity levels that challenge consumer units.

For a whole-house or basement application where you need the unit running year-round, the professional tier is worth the premium. For an occasional-use situation, a consumer unit is fine.

### Labor and Installation Complexity

Installing a simple freestanding basement unit takes one to two hours for a contractor. A ducted whole-house installation is significantly more involved: typically four to eight hours depending on ductwork access, adding a drain line, wiring a humidistat, and testing the system. If the contractor has to work in a tight attic or crawl space, labor costs go up.

Expect to pay:
- Freestanding unit installation: $200 to $400
- Ducted whole-house installation: $400 to $700
- Crawl space installation with vapor barrier prep: $400 to $700

### Your Location

Contractors in high-cost metro areas (New York, Boston, Los Angeles, San Francisco) charge 25 to 40 percent more for the same installation than contractors in lower-cost markets. That said, the need for a dehumidifier in a dry climate like Phoenix or Denver is minimal, so this cost variable mainly applies where dehumidifiers matter most: the South, Southeast, Mid-Atlantic, Great Lakes region, and Pacific Northwest.

## How Much Does It Cost to Run a Whole-House Dehumidifier?

This is an important question homeowners often overlook. A 70-pint basement unit running eight hours per day at the US average electricity rate of about $0.16 per kWh costs roughly **$10 to $20 per month**. A larger ducted whole-house unit running more aggressively in a humid summer can cost **$30 to $60 per month** in electricity.

The good news: many homeowners find they can raise their thermostat by one or two degrees when humidity is controlled, since lower humidity makes the same temperature feel cooler. That can partially offset the dehumidifier's electrical cost.

ENERGY STAR-certified dehumidifiers use 15 percent less energy on average than standard models. Look for the [ENERGY STAR label](https://www.energystar.gov/products/dehumidifiers) when shopping; it is worth checking, as not all units qualify.

## Signs You Actually Need a Whole-House Dehumidifier

Not every home needs one. Here are the clearest signs yours does:

**You notice these in your basement or crawl space:**
- Condensation on walls or pipes in summer
- Musty or earthy smell that does not go away
- Water stains or efflorescence (white chalky mineral deposits) on walls
- Visible mold or mildew growth
- Rusting metal components

**You notice these in your living space:**
- Hardwood floors cupping or warping in summer
- Doors and windows sticking in humid months
- Allergy and asthma symptoms that are worse at home
- Persistent condensation on windows

**Your hygrometer reads above 60 percent** for sustained periods. You can buy a simple digital hygrometer for $10 to $20 at any hardware store to check. If you consistently read above 55 to 60 percent inside, a dehumidifier will make a real difference.

## DIY vs. Hiring a Pro

A freestanding basement dehumidifier is genuinely a DIY installation for most homeowners: plug it in, run the drain hose to a floor drain or use the built-in pump, and set the target humidity. If you are comfortable with a drill and basic home improvements, you can handle this in an afternoon.

A ducted whole-house unit is different. Splicing into ductwork, running electrical, connecting a drain, and integrating with existing HVAC controls requires HVAC knowledge. Getting it wrong can reduce system efficiency or cause drainage problems. This is a job for a licensed HVAC contractor.

For the crawl space, the installation itself is manageable if you are comfortable working in a confined space, but combining it with vapor barrier installation (which you should do at the same time) is best left to a professional.

## Finding and Vetting Contractors

For a ducted or crawl space installation, you want a licensed HVAC contractor or a basement waterproofing company that specializes in moisture control. Get at least three quotes and ask specifically:

- Do they carry the brand they are recommending? (Some contractors push certain brands because of rebates.)
- What is included in the warranty, and who handles service calls?
- Will the drain line run to a proper drain or sump pit, not just to the floor?
- What capacity unit do they recommend, and why?

Always verify contractor licensing through your state's licensing board. HVAC contractor licensing requirements vary by state; the [National Association of Home Builders](https://www.nahb.org) maintains resources on contractor standards and vetting. Our guide on [how to find a good contractor](/blog/how-to-find-good-contractor/) walks through the full vetting process.

## Dehumidifier vs. Air Conditioner: Do You Need Both?

A common question: does running your air conditioner handle humidity control? The answer is: sort of, but usually not enough in a humid climate. Air conditioners do remove some moisture as a byproduct of cooling, but they are designed to control temperature, not humidity. When temperatures drop in fall but humidity remains high, your AC will not run enough to keep humidity in check. And in an unconditioned basement, your AC does nothing at all.

A whole-house dehumidifier works independently of your AC and handles humidity even when the AC is not running. In practice, most homeowners in humid climates benefit from having both. If your AC is due for replacement, ask about systems with built-in dehumidification features; some newer units offer enhanced humidity control at a modest premium over standard models. ## Rebates and Tax Credits

As of 2026, dehumidifiers do not qualify for federal tax credits under the Inflation Reduction Act, which is focused on heating and cooling upgrades like heat pumps, insulation, and windows. However, some utility companies offer rebates for ENERGY STAR-certified dehumidifiers, particularly in humid regions where high humidity increases overall grid load.

Check your utility's website or the [ENERGY STAR rebate finder](https://www.energystar.gov/rebate-finder) to see what is available in your area. Rebates typically run $25 to $75 per unit.
