Snow removal services typically cost **$25 to $100 per visit** for a standard residential driveway, or **$200 to $600 for a seasonal contract** covering all snowfalls. If you live in a heavy-snow region like the Upper Midwest or New England, full-service seasonal packages can run $500 to $1,200. Single-event rates after a major storm often jump to $150 or more. Knowing what drives these numbers helps you negotiate a fair deal before the first flakes fall.

## What You'll Pay: Quick Reference

| Service Type | Typical Cost |
|---|---|
| Per-visit driveway plowing | $25 to $100 |
| Per-visit walkway/steps shoveling | $25 to $60 |
| Seasonal contract (average home) | $200 to $600 |
| Seasonal contract (heavy-snow region) | $500 to $1,200 |
| De-icing / salt application (per visit) | $15 to $40 added on |

| Emergency or post-storm call-out | $100 to $250 |

## The Biggest Factor: Where You Live

Snow removal pricing varies dramatically by region because labor markets, snowfall totals, and competition all differ. Here is what homeowners typically report paying in their area:

**Northeast (Maine, Vermont, New Hampshire, Upstate New York)**
Seasonal rates of $500 to $1,200 are common because snowfall is heavy and unpredictable. Per-visit rates run $45 to $100.

**Midwest (Minnesota, Wisconsin, Michigan, Illinois, Ohio)**
One of the most competitive markets. Seasonal contracts often land at $300 to $700. Per-visit rates: $30 to $75.

**Mid-Atlantic (Pennsylvania, New Jersey, Maryland, Virginia)**
Variable snowfall makes per-visit pricing popular. Expect $35 to $85 per visit. Seasonal contracts for a good-sized driveway run $200 to $500.

**Mountain West (Colorado, Utah, Wyoming)**
Heavy mountain snowfall pushes costs up. Per-visit rates: $50 to $125. Seasonal packages: $500 to $1,000.

**Pacific Northwest (Washington, Oregon)**
Lower snowfall areas often have fewer providers; on-call rates: $60 to $150 per visit.

**Southern States**
Snow removal contractors are rare and charge premium rates because equipment is not widely available. If you can find someone, expect $75 to $200 per visit during rare snow events.

## Per-Visit vs. Seasonal Contract: Which Is Better for You?

This is one of the most important decisions you will make, and the answer comes down to your local snowfall patterns.

**Choose per-visit pricing if:**
- Your area gets fewer than five significant snowfalls per year
- You want flexibility to skip service for small accumulations you can handle yourself
- You are in a mid-Atlantic or border-state climate

**Choose a seasonal contract if:**
- Your area reliably gets 10 or more snowfalls above 2 inches
- You want predictable costs and guaranteed priority service
- You have mobility limitations or a long driveway

A good rule of thumb: if you would call a contractor more than six times in a typical winter, the seasonal contract usually saves money. Run the math. If per-visit rates are $60 and you anticipate 10 events, that is $600. A seasonal contract for $450 is the better value, plus you get priority over per-visit customers during big storms when everyone is calling at once.

## What Affects Your Specific Quote

**1. Driveway length and width**
Most contractors price driveway plowing on a per-linear-foot or tiered basis. A single-car driveway (20 to 40 feet) costs less than a two-car or long circular driveway. Some charge extra for turnarounds, circular sections, or plowing behind parked cars.

**2. Trigger depth**
Most contracts specify a minimum snow depth (the "trigger") before the contractor is obligated to plow, typically 2 to 3 inches. If you want service at 1 inch, expect to pay more. Many homeowners find the 2-inch trigger works well for most storms.

**3. Time of day / priority**
Early-morning service (before 7 a.m.) so you can leave for work on time often costs $10 to $25 extra per visit. Priority scheduling during major storms can be built into premium contracts.

**4. Stairs, walkways, and porches**
Plowing the driveway and clearing the front walk are usually separate line items. Full-service packages that include walkway shoveling and porch clearing add $25 to $75 per visit to base rates.

**5. De-icing and salt**
Salt and calcium chloride applications protect against ice re-formation after plowing. Contractors often charge $15 to $40 per application, or include a set number of applications in seasonal contracts. If you have a stamped concrete or brick driveway, ask about concrete-safe alternatives; rock salt can damage decorative surfaces.

**6. Equipment used**
A contractor with a truck-mounted plow and a skid steer can clear a large property quickly. Smaller contractors using ATVs or single-stage snowblowers charge less but may take longer on big storms. For large or complex properties, make sure the contractor has the right equipment.

## Hidden Costs to Watch For

**Overage fees**: Some seasonal contracts cap the number of plowing events included. If you go over that cap (say, more than 15 events), additional visits are billed at a per-event rate. Read your contract carefully.

**Salt and material charges**: Some contractors advertise base rates and then charge separately for every bag of salt or ice melt used. Ask upfront whether de-icing materials are included.

**Storm surcharges**: A major blizzard (over 12 inches) often triggers surcharges of $25 to $75 per visit even on seasonal contracts, because it requires multiple passes and much more time.

**Cancellation penalties**: Seasonal contracts often require a deposit and have cancellation clauses. If you sell your house mid-winter, you may owe the balance.

**Mailbox and landscaping damage**: Ask whether the contractor is insured for property damage. Contractors can nick mailboxes, decorative edging, or sprinkler heads buried under snow. A reputable contractor carries general liability insurance.

## How to Vet a Snow Removal Contractor

Hiring the wrong crew can leave you stranded during a storm or stuck with a damaged driveway. Here is what to verify before signing anything:

**Check licensing and insurance**: Requirements vary by state, but you should always ask for proof of general liability insurance and, if they employ workers, workers' compensation coverage. The [National Association of Landscape Professionals (NALP)](https://www.landscapeprofessionals.org) maintains a contractor directory and publishes industry safety standards.

**Get at least three quotes**: Prices vary widely. Getting multiple bids takes 30 minutes but can save you $150 to $300 on a seasonal contract.

**Read the trigger clause**: Make sure the trigger depth (the minimum snowfall before service kicks in) matches your needs. Some contractors default to a 3-inch trigger; ask to lower it if you need a clearer driveway for a steep incline.

**Ask about their equipment backup**: If a contractor's plow breaks down during a major storm, do they have a backup plan? A good operator has a second vehicle or a relationship with a subcontractor.

**Check online reviews and ask neighbors**: Word-of-mouth in your neighborhood is invaluable. Someone down the street who has used the same contractor for three winters is a better reference than any online listing.

**Get the contract in writing**: The contract should spell out trigger depth, service area (driveway only vs. walkway and porch), response time, what happens during large storms, and cancellation terms.

## DIY Snow Removal: When It Makes Sense

For some homeowners, handling snow removal yourself is entirely reasonable. Here is when it works and when it does not:

**DIY works well if:**
- You have a short driveway (under 50 feet)
- You are in good health and physically capable of shoveling
- Snowfall is light and infrequent (under five events per year)
- You enjoy the workout and the fresh air

A good two-stage electric snowblower runs $500 to $900 and handles most residential driveways efficiently. After two or three seasons, it pays for itself compared to hiring out. The [Consumer Product Safety Commission (CPSC)](https://www.cpsc.gov) publishes safety guidelines for snowblower use, including carbon monoxide risks when using gas-powered equipment in enclosed spaces.

**Hire a pro if:**
- You have a heart condition or other health issues. Shoveling is a leading cause of winter cardiac events, according to research published by the American Journal of Emergency Medicine.
- You have a long, steep, or wide driveway
- You travel for work and need reliable service in your absence
- You are over 65 or have joint problems
- Snowfall in your area is heavy and frequent

## What About Snow Removal Apps?

Several on-demand snow removal apps have launched in the past few years, similar to rideshare apps but for plowing. These platforms connect homeowners with local contractors for per-event service. Pricing is often transparent upfront. The tradeoff is that during major storms, demand surges and prices go up, similar to ride-share surge pricing. For occasional light snowfalls, these apps can be cost-effective. For reliable service during blizzards, a seasonal contract with a local operator is more dependable.

## Tax Deductions and Business Use

If you work from home and your driveway serves your business, a portion of snow removal costs may be deductible. Consult a tax professional about your specific situation. The [IRS Publication 587](https://www.irs.gov/publications/p587) covers home office deductions. Rental property owners can typically deduct snow removal as an ordinary maintenance expense.

## Preparing Your Property for Snow Removal Service

A few simple steps make the contractor's job easier and protect your property:

- Mark the edges of your driveway and any obstacles (sprinkler heads, decorative edging, low landscaping) with driveway markers. These bright-colored stakes cost $10 to $20 for a pack of 25 at any home improvement store.
- Clear vehicles from the driveway if possible. Contractors cannot plow around parked cars without leaving strips of snow.
- Leave your porch light on and house number visible so contractors can find your property in the dark.
- Agree on where the contractor should push snow. Avoid having it pushed in front of a neighbor's property or blocking storm drains. Many municipalities prohibit pushing snow into the street; check your local ordinances.
