Summer is the most expensive season to skip home maintenance. A neglected air conditioner can fail during a heat wave and cost you $3,000 to $7,000 to replace. A small roof leak ignored in spring becomes a $5,000 to $15,000 mold and water damage problem by August. The good news: completing this 25-task checklist takes about one weekend and costs between $200 and $600 total for materials and minor service calls, compared to thousands in emergency repairs.

This guide covers everything US homeowners need to check before and during summer, with specific costs, red flags to watch for, and links to authoritative resources.

---

## Why Summer Maintenance Matters More Than You Think

Summer brings heat stress, thunderstorms, humidity, and increased water usage, all at once. Your home's systems work harder from June through September than any other time of year.

According to the [Federal Emergency Management Agency (FEMA)](https://www.fema.gov), severe summer storms cause billions in home damage every year, much of it preventable with basic prep. The [Department of Energy](https://www.energy.gov) estimates that homeowners waste 20 to 30 percent of their cooling energy through air leaks and poor insulation alone.

## HVAC and Cooling: Your Top Priority

### 1. Schedule an AC Tune-Up

If you haven't had your central air conditioner serviced this year, do it now. A professional tune-up costs **$75 to $200** and typically includes cleaning coils, checking refrigerant levels, lubricating moving parts, and testing thermostat accuracy.

Signs your AC needs attention beyond a tune-up:
- It takes more than 30 minutes to cool your home by 5 degrees on a mild day
- You hear grinding, squealing, or banging from the unit
- Warm air blowing from vents (see our guide on [AC blowing warm air](/blog/ac-blowing-warm-air))
- Ice forming on refrigerant lines

ENERGY STAR estimates that a well-maintained AC uses up to 15 percent less energy than a neglected one. That can translate to $100 to $300 in annual savings depending on your climate and energy rates. Learn more at [energystar.gov](https://www.energystar.gov).

### 2. Replace or Clean Air Filters

This is the single easiest HVAC task you can do yourself. A clogged filter restricts airflow, makes your system work harder, and can cause the evaporator coil to freeze.

- Standard 1-inch filters: replace every 30 to 90 days ($5 to $15 each)
- Thicker media filters (4-5 inch): replace every 6 to 12 months ($20 to $45 each)
- Reusable filters: clean with water every 30 days (free after purchase)

In summer, with pollen, dust, and more foot traffic, replace or clean filters on the shorter end of those intervals.

### 3. Clear Around Your Outdoor AC Unit

Your condenser unit needs at least 2 feet of clear space on all sides and 5 feet above it. Overgrown shrubs, stacked firewood, and mulch piled against the unit reduce airflow and can void your warranty.

Trim vegetation back and rinse the fins gently with a garden hose. Never use a pressure washer. If the fins are bent, a fin comb from a hardware store ($10 to $15) can straighten them.

### 4. Test and Program Your Smart Thermostat

If you have a programmable or smart thermostat, review your summer schedule settings. Set it to 78 degrees when home and 85 degrees when away. The [Department of Energy](https://www.energy.gov/energysaver/thermostats) says this simple practice saves about 10 percent on cooling costs annually.

Don't have a smart thermostat yet? Installation costs $150 to $350 and typically pays for itself within two cooling seasons.

## Roof and Exterior: Stop Leaks Before They Start

### 5. Inspect Your Roof After Spring Storms

You don't need to get on the roof to do a basic visual check. Use binoculars from the ground and look for:
- Missing, cracked, or curling shingles
- Dark staining or algae growth
- Sagging areas
- Flashing that looks bent, lifted, or rusted around chimneys, vents, and skylights

Summer thunderstorms bring hail, high winds, and heavy rain. If your roof has pre-existing damage going in, the first big storm can turn a $400 flashing repair into a $12,000 roof replacement plus water damage restoration.

A professional [roof inspection](/blog/roof-inspection-cost-what-to-expect) costs $150 to $400 and is often free if you're already getting repair work done.

### 6. Clean Your Gutters and Downspouts

If you have trees nearby, you likely have debris in your gutters even after a spring cleaning. Clogged gutters in summer rainstorms can overflow and direct water toward your foundation.

DIY gutter cleaning costs nothing beyond a few hours and a ladder. Professional cleaning runs **$100 to $225** for a single-story home. If gutters are pulling away from the fascia or you see rust spots, address them before heavy summer storms.

### 7. Check Window and Door Caulking and Weatherstripping

Heat and UV rays degrade exterior caulk faster than any other season. Walk around your home and press on caulk lines around windows, doors, and where siding meets trim. If it cracks, crumbles, or pulls away easily, it needs replacing.

A tube of exterior caulk costs $6 to $12 and takes about 20 minutes per window to apply. This one task can reduce your cooling bills by 5 to 15 percent, according to [Energy.gov](https://www.energy.gov/energysaver/air-sealing-your-home).

### 8. Inspect Your Deck or Patio

Summer is deck season, which means it's also the time to catch problems before someone gets hurt. Check for:
- Soft, spongy, or discolored wood (signs of rot)
- Loose railings or balusters (grab each one and push; it shouldn't move)
- Popped nails or screws
- Gaps between boards that have widened

[Wood rot repair](/blog/wood-rot-repair-cost) costs $100 to $1,000 depending on how much is affected. Catching it early saves money and keeps your family safe.

### 9. Seal Your Driveway

Asphalt driveways need resealing every 2 to 5 years. UV radiation and heat accelerate cracking. A fresh seal coat protects the surface and prevents water from penetrating and widening cracks over winter.

DIY driveway sealing: $50 to $100 in materials for a standard two-car driveway. Professional sealing: $100 to $300. Crack filler is $5 to $15 per tube.

## Plumbing: Heat, Water, and Outdoor Use

### 10. Inspect Outdoor Faucets and Hose Bibs

After a winter of freeze risk, check outdoor faucets for drips, loose handles, or leaking from the wall pipe connection. A faucet that drips at the base when in use often has a worn packing nut or washer, an easy fix costing $10 to $20 in parts.

Also check the washers on your garden hoses and replace cracked or stiff hoses before they split under pressure.

### 11. Test Your Sump Pump

Severe summer storms dump water fast. If your basement or crawl space has a sump pump, test it now before the rainy season peaks.

Pour 5 gallons of water into the pit and watch that the pump activates within seconds and drains the water completely. If it doesn't respond, runs continuously, or makes a grinding sound, call a plumber. Sump pump replacement costs **$300 to $700** including installation.

[The American Society of Home Inspectors](https://www.ashi.org) recommends testing sump pumps at least twice a year.

### 12. Check Your Water Heater

Summer is a good time to flush sediment from your water heater tank, check the temperature setting, and test the pressure relief valve. The recommended setting is 120 degrees Fahrenheit, which prevents scalding and reduces energy use by up to 10 percent.

Flushing a water heater yourself takes about 30 minutes and requires just a garden hose. If you see signs your water heater might be failing (see our guide on [signs your water heater is failing](/blog/signs-water-heater-failing)), address them now before the cooler months when demand increases.

### 13. Run All Bathroom and Kitchen Fans

Humidity from summer showers and cooking can build up quickly if exhaust fans aren't venting properly. Hold a tissue in front of each fan: it should be pulled toward the fan. If not, the fan motor may be failing or the duct may be blocked.

A bathroom exhaust fan replacement costs **$150 to $400** installed, and [the EPA](https://www.epa.gov/indoor-air-quality-iaq/improving-indoor-air-quality) recommends running exhaust fans during and 15 minutes after showers to reduce indoor humidity and mold risk.

## Pest Prevention: Get Ahead of Summer Infestations

### 14. Inspect and Seal Entry Points

Mice, ants, wasps, and roaches all move aggressively in summer heat looking for food, water, and shelter. A thorough inspection now saves you a $200 to $900 pest control bill later.

Walk the exterior perimeter and seal:
- Gaps where pipes enter the home (use steel wool plus caulk)
- Cracks in the foundation larger than 1/4 inch
- Gaps under doors (replace weatherstripping)
- Torn or missing window screens

[The CDC](https://www.cdc.gov/niosh/topics/indoorenv/pests.html) recommends removing standing water sources and sealing food to prevent summer pest invasions.

### 15. Check for Wasp and Bee Nests

Early summer is when new nests start forming. Check overhangs, soffits, shutters, and woodpiles. A small paper wasp nest in May ($0 to $15 to treat yourself) becomes a basketball-sized hive by August that requires a professional ($100 to $400).

If you discover honeybees, contact a local beekeeper before exterminating. Honeybee relocation is often free, and the [USDA](https://www.usda.gov) encourages protecting pollinators where possible.

### 16. Treat Your Yard for Fleas and Ticks

If you have pets or children using the yard, consider a perimeter treatment for fleas and ticks in late spring or early summer. The [CDC](https://www.cdc.gov/ticks/prevention) recommends treating the edges of your lawn where it meets woods or brush, since that's where ticks congregate.

DIY yard treatment products: $30 to $80. Professional lawn spray: $75 to $200 per treatment.

## Safety Checks: These Can Save Lives

### 17. Test Smoke and Carbon Monoxide Detectors

The [U.S. Fire Administration](https://www.usfa.fema.gov/prevention/home-fires/smoke-alarms/) recommends testing detectors monthly. Push and hold the test button for 5 seconds. If the alarm doesn't sound clearly, replace the batteries or the unit.

Summer means more grilling, bonfires, and window-open cooking. These activities increase CO and fire risk. The [CPSC](https://www.cpsc.gov) recommends replacing CO detectors every 5 to 7 years and smoke detectors every 10 years.

### 18. Check Your Fire Extinguisher

Look at the pressure gauge on your kitchen fire extinguisher. The needle should be in the green zone. Check that the safety pin is intact and the nozzle is free of blockage. If it's expired (check the label) or was partially discharged, replace it.

A standard 2.5-lb kitchen extinguisher costs $20 to $45 at any hardware store.

### 19. Inspect Electrical Outlets Near Water

Kitchens, bathrooms, garages, and outdoor areas require GFCI (ground fault circuit interrupter) outlets. Push the test and reset buttons on each GFCI outlet to verify they trip and reset correctly.

If any outdoor outlets have damaged covers or water infiltration, have an electrician replace them. An outdoor outlet replacement costs **$75 to $200** and significantly reduces shock and fire risk. The [CPSC](https://www.cpsc.gov/Safety-Education/Safety-Education-Centers/Electrical-Safety-Information-Center) has detailed guidance on GFCI requirements.

### 20. Inspect and Clean Your Dryer Vent

Dryer vent lint buildup is a leading cause of house fires. Disconnect the dryer from the wall and check the flexible duct for kinks or accumulated lint. For long duct runs, a dryer vent cleaning kit ($20 to $35) or professional cleaning ($80 to $200) clears blockages a vacuum can't reach.

The [CPSC](https://www.cpsc.gov) reports that dryers cause about 2,900 house fires annually, nearly all preventable.

## Energy Efficiency: Reduce Your Summer Bills

### 21. Add Window Coverings on South and West Faces

Up to 30 percent of your cooling load comes from solar heat gain through windows, according to [Energy.gov](https://www.energy.gov/energysaver/window-treatments-and-coverings). Blackout curtains or cellular shades on south and west-facing windows can cut cooling costs by $100 to $300 per summer.

Exterior solutions like awnings or solar screens reduce heat gain even more because they block sunlight before it enters the glass.

### 22. Check Attic Ventilation

A poorly ventilated attic can reach 150 degrees or higher in summer. That heat radiates down into living spaces, making your AC work harder and shortening its lifespan.

Signs of poor attic ventilation:
- Unusually hot second floor even when AC is running
- Ice dams forming in winter (a sign of uneven attic temps)
- High energy bills in summer despite AC maintenance

Ridge vents, soffit vents, and attic fans work together to flush hot air out. Have an HVAC professional or insulation contractor assess your attic if you suspect poor airflow.

### 23. Inspect Attic Insulation

The [Department of Energy](https://www.energy.gov/energysaver/insulation) recommends R-38 to R-60 insulation for attics in most US climate zones. If your insulation is thin, compressed, or missing in spots, adding more can reduce cooling costs by 15 to 20 percent annually.

Blown-in attic insulation costs **$1,500 to $3,500** for most homes, but tax credits are available. See our guide on [home improvement tax credits 2026](/blog/home-improvement-tax-credits-2026) for details.

## Outdoor and Landscaping: Keep Your Yard Working for You

### 24. Trim Trees and Shrubs Away from the House

Dead or overhanging limbs become projectiles in summer storms. The [Insurance Information Institute](https://www.iii.org) recommends trimming branches that hang over your roof or within 10 feet of your home to reduce storm damage risk.

Professional tree trimming runs $200 to $1,000 depending on the size and number of trees. If a branch is large and close to power lines, that's a job for a certified arborist, not a weekend DIY project.

### 25. Service Your Lawn Irrigation System

If you have an in-ground sprinkler system, now is the time to check for broken heads, misaligned sprinklers spraying your house or driveway, and leaks at valve boxes. Each broken sprinkler head wastes 20 to 25 gallons per minute it runs.

Walk the yard while the system cycles through each zone. A replacement sprinkler head costs $5 to $15 and takes about 15 minutes to swap out. If a zone doesn't activate, the valve solenoid may need replacing ($10 to $25 in parts).

The [EPA's WaterSense program](https://www.epa.gov/watersense) estimates that smart irrigation management can save the average home 8,800 gallons per year.

## Regional Considerations

Summer maintenance needs vary significantly by US region:

**Southeast and Gulf Coast (FL, TX, LA, MS, AL, GA):** Prioritize mold prevention, hurricane prep, and pest control. High humidity accelerates rot and mold faster than any other region. Check seals around windows and doors monthly during rainy season.

**Southwest (AZ, NV, NM, UT):** Extreme heat (110+ degrees) is the main threat. Shade your AC condenser if possible, and inspect roof coatings and sealants before monsoon season. Evaporative coolers need pad replacement and cleaning in spring.

**Midwest and Plains (IL, OH, MO, KS, NE):** Tornado and hail season peaks in June and July. Know where your shutoff valves are and check your basement sump pump before major storm systems move through.

**Pacific Northwest (WA, OR):** Summer wildfire smoke degrades outdoor air quality. Use MERV 11 or higher filters during smoke events. Check deck coatings, as dry summers after wet winters can cause wood to crack.

**Northeast (NY, MA, CT, PA):** High humidity plus summer heat accelerates paint peeling on wood siding and trim. Inspect and touch up exterior paint before it fails completely.

## When to Call a Pro vs. DIY

Most of these tasks are straightforward for a motivated homeowner. But call a professional for:

- Any electrical work beyond replacing a switch or outlet cover
- Roof repairs involving flashing, valleys, or structural damage
- AC repairs that involve refrigerant (requires EPA certification)
- Sewer line inspections if you notice slow drains throughout the house
- Tree removal near power lines or structures
