Repairing a frozen pipe costs **$200 to $1,500** for most US homeowners when a licensed plumber thaws or patches a single pipe. If the pipe has already burst and water has soaked into walls, flooring, or insulation, total costs including water damage restoration jump to **$3,000 to $15,000 or more**. Emergency after-hours calls add a 50 to 100 percent premium on top of those numbers. The good news: catching a frozen pipe before it bursts keeps the repair bill under $500 in the majority of cases.

Every year, about 250,000 American households deal with pipes that freeze, according to the [Insurance Information Institute](https://www.iii.org/). The average insurance claim for a burst frozen pipe runs just under **$18,000**, and that number has climbed sharply since the 2021 Texas freeze, which caused more than $20 billion in residential damage alone. Knowing what repairs cost, what your homeowners insurance actually covers, and what you can do right now to prevent this from happening are the three most important things you can take away from this guide.

## What Drives the Cost of Frozen Pipe Repair

Several factors swing the final bill significantly. Understanding them helps you get accurate quotes and avoid being overcharged.

### 1. Whether the Pipe Has Burst or Just Frozen

This is the single biggest cost factor. A frozen pipe that has not yet burst is a contained emergency. A plumber can often thaw it with a heat gun or hot water and be done in an hour or two. Total bill: **$150 to $500** depending on your area and the accessibility of the pipe.

A burst pipe is a different story. Water has already escaped. You now need the pipe repaired or replaced, and potentially also need a water damage restoration company to dry out walls, flooring, or structural framing. That combination can easily push total costs past **$5,000**.

### 2. Pipe Location and Accessibility

Pipes in finished walls or ceilings cost the most to reach because the plumber has to open drywall, make the repair, and then leave you with a patching job. Pipes under kitchen or bathroom sinks, in unfinished basements, or in crawl spaces are much easier to access.

| Pipe Location | Repair Cost Range |
|---|---|
| Under sink (accessible) | $150 to $400 |
| Basement or crawl space | $250 to $700 |
| Attic (common in South and Southwest) | $400 to $1,200 |
| Inside finished wall or ceiling | $500 to $1,500+ |

### 3. Pipe Material

The material your pipes are made of affects both how badly they burst and how complex the repair is.

**Copper pipes** are the oldest and most common material in homes built before the 1990s. When copper freezes and bursts, it splits cleanly. A plumber cuts out the damaged section and solders in a new piece. Cost for a single repair: **$300 to $900**.

**PEX (cross-linked polyethylene)** pipes, the flexible plastic tubing common in homes built after 2000, are more forgiving because PEX can expand slightly when water freezes. When PEX does burst, the repair is often simpler: **$200 to $600** for a single section.

**CPVC and galvanized steel** pipes crack more severely and are more expensive to repair, especially if the pipe is older and corroded: **$400 to $1,200** per section.

### 4. Labor Rates by Region

Plumber rates vary a lot by state. Here are median hourly rates for licensed plumbers in different regions. Emergency frozen-pipe calls are typically billed at time-and-a-half or double time.

| Region | Standard Hourly Rate | Emergency Rate |
|---|---|---|
| Northeast (MA, CT, NY, NJ) | $90 to $150/hr | $150 to $250/hr |
| Midwest (IL, OH, MI, MN) | $70 to $120/hr | $110 to $200/hr |
| South (TX, GA, FL, LA) | $65 to $110/hr | $100 to $190/hr |
| West (CA, OR, WA) | $90 to $160/hr | $150 to $270/hr |
| Mountain/Plains (CO, UT, KS) | $70 to $130/hr | $110 to $210/hr |

Most frozen pipe calls take 1 to 3 hours for the plumbing work itself.

### 5. Northern vs. Southern States

Here is a fact that surprises many homeowners: frozen pipe damage is often **worse and more expensive in southern states** than in the North.

Why? In states like Minnesota, Wisconsin, and Maine, building codes require pipes to be well inside insulated walls and well below grade. Contractors and homeowners are also experienced at winterizing. Freezes happen, but the infrastructure is built for it.

In Texas, Georgia, Florida, Louisiana, and the Carolinas, pipes are often run in exterior walls, under mobile homes with minimal skirting, or in attics with little insulation. When temperatures drop below freezing, which happens less often but still happens, those pipes are extremely vulnerable. The 2021 Texas freeze was so devastating partly because so many pipes were in locations that would never be acceptable in a northern state.

If you live in the South and have not had your pipe locations assessed, consider a plumber walkthrough (typically **$100 to $200**) before winter hits.

## Signs You Have a Frozen Pipe Right Now

If you are reading this because your water is not working, act fast. The window between a frozen pipe and a burst pipe can be measured in hours.

**Signs of a frozen pipe:**
- Little or no water coming from a specific faucet while others work normally
- A bulge or frost on an exposed section of pipe
- Strange banging or cracking sounds in the walls when the heat is running
- An unusually cold section of wall or floor near a pipe run

**What to do immediately:**
1. Turn off the main water shutoff valve. If the pipe bursts while frozen, this limits water damage.
2. Open the affected faucet slightly so water can trickle through as the pipe thaws.
3. If you can see the pipe, apply gentle heat with a hair dryer, a heating pad, or a portable space heater. Never use an open flame.
4. Call a licensed plumber if you cannot locate or access the frozen section, or if you hear a pop or see a wet spot on the wall.

The [American Red Cross](https://www.redcross.org/) publishes a frozen pipe thawing guide that is worth bookmarking before temperatures drop.

## What Does Homeowners Insurance Actually Cover?

This question causes enormous frustration because the answer is "it depends" in ways that genuinely matter.

Most standard homeowners insurance policies (HO-3 forms) cover **sudden and accidental water damage** from a burst pipe. That means the drywall, flooring, insulation, and personal property soaked by a burst frozen pipe are typically covered after your deductible. The average deductible is $1,000 to $2,500, so a claim only makes financial sense for larger losses.

What most policies do **not** cover:
- The cost to repair or replace the pipe itself (that is considered maintenance)
- Damage caused by neglect, such as leaving your heat off in a vacant home
- Gradual leaks that you knew about and did not fix

**The key phrase to understand: "sudden and accidental."** If your insurer can argue that you failed to maintain adequate heat, left the house without winterizing, or knew about a risk and ignored it, they may deny the claim. Always document your thermostat settings during cold snaps, and if you leave for more than a few days in winter, have someone check the house.

For the full story on how to file a claim and what to expect, see our guide on [homeowners insurance claims](/blog/homeowners-insurance-claims/).

The [National Association of Insurance Commissioners](https://content.naic.org/) also has a state-by-state breakdown of what different policy types typically include.

## The Real Cost You Are Trying to Avoid: Water Damage

The repair to the pipe itself is rarely the most expensive part of a frozen pipe event. The water damage is.

When a burst pipe goes unnoticed for even a few hours, say, overnight or while you are traveling, water soaks into:
- Drywall (which must be removed and replaced once it absorbs water)
- Hardwood flooring (which warps and buckles)
- Subfloor and structural framing (which can develop mold within 24 to 48 hours)
- Insulation (which must be completely replaced when wet)
- Personal belongings, furniture, and electronics

A water damage restoration company will bring in industrial dehumidifiers, air movers, and moisture meters to dry the structure out. That process alone typically costs **$3,000 to $10,000** for a moderately affected room, and much more for a finished basement or multi-room event.

## How to Prevent Frozen Pipes: Costs That Are Worth It

Prevention is dramatically cheaper than repair. Here are the most effective options ranked by cost.

### Free and Low-Cost Prevention

**Let faucets drip during extreme cold.** Keeping a slow trickle running through pipes exposed to cold zones prevents the static pressure buildup that causes bursts. Cost: essentially free, though it adds a few cents to your water bill.

**Keep your heat set no lower than 55 degrees Fahrenheit**, even when you are away from home. Many freeze events happen in vacant or lightly occupied homes. Your heating bill for a cold week is far less than a burst pipe repair.

**Open cabinet doors** under sinks on exterior walls so warm air circulates around the pipes.

### Low-Cost DIY Options ($20 to $200)

**Foam pipe insulation sleeves** are available at any hardware store for **$0.50 to $3 per linear foot**. They slip over pipes in unheated spaces like crawl spaces, basements, and garages. This is the single best dollar-per-dollar investment you can make.

**Heat tape or heat cable** wraps around vulnerable pipes and plugs into an outlet. A 6-foot length costs **$20 to $40**. A 30-foot length runs **$50 to $75**. Follow the manufacturer's instructions carefully: improperly installed heat tape is a fire risk. The [Consumer Product Safety Commission (CPSC)](https://www.cpsc.gov/) has specific guidelines for safe installation.

**Pipe insulation wrap (fiberglass)** is better than foam for very cold areas and costs roughly **$1 to $4 per linear foot**.

### Professional Winterization ($100 to $400)

A licensed plumber can inspect and insulate your most vulnerable pipes, check for poorly located pipe runs, and apply heat tape professionally. This service typically costs **$100 to $300** in a standard home and **$200 to $400** for a larger home or one with many exposed pipe runs. If you have already had a freeze event or recently moved into an older home, this is worth scheduling every fall.

| Task | DIY Possible? | Notes |

## Getting Quotes: What to Ask Before You Hire

If you need a plumber for frozen pipe work, ask these questions before agreeing to anything:

1. **Are you licensed and insured in my state?** Licensing requirements vary. Check your state's licensing board. For a national directory, the [National Association of Home Builders (NAHB)](https://www.nahb.org/) offers a contractor search tool.
2. **Is this quote for time-and-materials or a flat fee?** Emergency calls billed by the hour can grow quickly if the pipe is hard to reach.
3. **Do you charge a trip fee?** Many plumbers charge $50 to $150 just to show up. Ask upfront.
4. **What is your policy if additional damage is found once you open the wall?** Make sure you get a clear answer before drywall is cut.
5. **Do you handle coordination with water damage restoration, or do I need to hire that separately?** Some plumbing companies have restoration partners; others do not.

Always get at least two quotes for repairs over $500. The price variation on pipe work can be 30 to 40 percent between contractors in the same city.
