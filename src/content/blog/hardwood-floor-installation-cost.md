Installing hardwood floors costs **$6 to $23 per square foot installed**, with most US homeowners paying **$8 to $15 per square foot** for solid hardwood including labor. For a 500 square foot living room, expect to pay **$4,000 to $10,000**. A whole-home installation covering 1,500 square feet typically runs **$12,000 to $25,000**, with the national average landing around **$4,500 to $7,500** for a single room project. The price range is wide because it depends on wood species, installation method, subfloor condition, and where you live. The good news: hardwood floors are one of the few renovations that consistently return 70 to 80 cents on the dollar at resale, making them a smart long-term investment even at today's prices.

Hardwood is a popular pairing with a [kitchen remodel](/blog/kitchen-remodel-cost/), and many homeowners install it during the same renovation window to save on labor scheduling. If your existing subfloor has moisture issues, get that resolved first, our [mold remediation guide](/blog/mold-remediation-cost/) explains what to look for before new flooring goes down. Always vet your flooring contractor using our [contractor vetting guide](/blog/how-to-find-good-contractor/).

## What You Are Actually Paying For

Before calling for quotes, it helps to understand how contractors break down the bill. Hardwood flooring costs have two main components: **materials** and **labor**.

Materials cover the wood itself, underlayment, fasteners, adhesive, and trim pieces. Labor covers subfloor preparation, acclimation time for the wood, actual installation, and finishing if you choose site-finished floors over prefinished.

Here is what you can expect for each line item:

| Component | Cost Range |
|-----------|------------|
| Solid hardwood materials | $3 to $15 per sq ft |
| Engineered hardwood materials | $2 to $10 per sq ft |
| Labor (installation only) | $3 to $8 per sq ft |
| Subfloor preparation | $1 to $3 per sq ft |
| Old flooring removal | $1 to $3 per sq ft |
| Underlayment | $0.25 to $0.75 per sq ft |
| Furniture moving | $50 to $150 per room |
| Site finishing (sand, stain, seal) | $3 to $5 per sq ft additional |

Prefinished hardwood skips the site finishing cost and gets your room back to normal faster, which is why many homeowners choose it even though prefinished floors show slight seams at board edges more than site-finished floors do.

## Solid vs. Engineered Hardwood: What Is the Price Difference?

This is the first choice most homeowners face, and it matters more than the price tag alone.

**Solid hardwood** is a single piece of real wood, typically 3/4 inch thick. It can be sanded and refinished many times over a 50 to 100 year lifespan. The catch: it expands and contracts with humidity, so it cannot go below grade (in basements) and does not hold up well in bathrooms or high-moisture spaces — if you're planning a [bathroom remodel](/blog/bathroom-remodel-cost/), tile or luxury vinyl are typically better flooring choices for those rooms.

**Engineered hardwood** has a real wood veneer bonded to layers of plywood underneath. It handles humidity better, can go in basements, and works over radiant heating systems. The tradeoff: the veneer is thinner (typically 2 to 6 millimeters), so it can only be refinished once or twice before you hit the core.

| Type | Material Cost | Total Installed Cost | Best For |
|------|--------------|---------------------|----------|
| Solid hardwood | $3 to $15 per sq ft | $6 to $23 per sq ft | Above grade, dry climates |
| Engineered hardwood | $2 to $10 per sq ft | $5 to $15 per sq ft | Basements, humid climates, radiant heat |
| Bamboo (technically grass) | $2 to $8 per sq ft | $5 to $12 per sq ft | Eco-conscious buyers |

## Hardwood Floor Costs by Wood Species

The species you choose is the single biggest driver of material cost. Oak is the most popular choice because it is abundant, affordable, durable, and takes stain beautifully. Exotic species like Brazilian cherry or walnut look stunning but cost two to three times as much.

| Wood Species | Material Cost per Sq Ft | Janka Hardness | Notes |
|-------------|------------------------|----------------|-------|
| Red or white oak | $4 to $9 | 1,290 to 1,360 | Most popular; takes stain well |
| Maple | $5 to $11 | 1,450 | Very hard; lighter color; hides scratches |
| Hickory | $5 to $12 | 1,820 | Rustic look; extremely durable |
| Cherry | $6 to $13 | 950 | Darkens beautifully with age; softer wood |
| Walnut | $9 to $18 | 1,010 | Rich dark color; premium look |
| Brazilian cherry | $8 to $15 | 2,350 | Very hard; exotic; deep red tones |
| White ash | $5 to $10 | 1,320 | Light and airy feel |
| Bamboo (strand-woven) | $2 to $8 | 3,000+ | Highly sustainable |

**A note on hardness:** The Janka rating measures how resistant wood is to denting. If you have dogs, kids, or high foot traffic, prioritize species with ratings above 1,200. Cherry and walnut are gorgeous but will show wear faster in busy households.

## What Drives the Price Up or Down

### Subfloor Condition

This is the hidden cost that surprises many homeowners. Your installer will pull up the old flooring and discover what is underneath. If the subfloor is uneven, soft, squeaky, or damaged by moisture, it needs repair before new flooring goes down. Subfloor leveling costs **$1 to $3 per square foot**, and replacing damaged sections typically adds **$300 to $700**.

### Installation Method

There are three main methods, and they affect both labor cost and which products you can use:

**Nail-down:** The traditional approach where fasteners go through the tongue of each plank into a wood subfloor. Fast, durable, and produces the most solid feel underfoot.

**Glue-down:** Adhesive spreads across the subfloor and planks press into it. Works on concrete slabs. Used almost exclusively for engineered hardwood. Adds **$1 to $2 per square foot** in labor.

**Floating:** Planks click or glue to each other at the edges but are not attached to the subfloor. The fastest installation and the most DIY-friendly method, but floors can feel slightly hollow underfoot.

### Prefinished vs. Site-Finished Floors

**Prefinished hardwood** arrives with the stain and finish already applied. Install and move on. You are back in the room within a day or two. The downside: the finish sits at each board edge, so seams are slightly visible, and your stain options are limited to what the manufacturer offers.

**Site-finished hardwood** arrives raw. After installation, the crew sands, stains, and seals the floor with polyurethane (usually three coats). The result is a seamless, smooth surface with any stain color you want. The tradeoff: the project takes three to five days total, and fumes from the finish require ventilating the space for at least 24 to 48 hours. Add **$3 to $5 per square foot** for site finishing.

### Plank Width

Narrow strip flooring (2.25 to 3.25 inches wide) requires more pieces and more time to install than wide plank (5 inches and wider). Wide plank flooring often costs **$1 to $3 more per square foot** in materials but can actually reduce labor cost in large open rooms because fewer planks are needed.

## DIY vs. Hiring a Pro: The Honest Breakdown

Can you install hardwood floors yourself? Yes, especially if you choose an engineered floating floor. Should you? That depends on your skills and patience.

**Consider DIY if:**
- You are installing floating engineered hardwood (the most forgiving method)
- You have basic carpentry skills and access to a miter saw
- The subfloor is flat and in good condition
- You can dedicate a full weekend or more to the project
- The room is a simple rectangle with few doorways or angled cuts

**Hire a pro if:**
- You are doing nail-down solid hardwood (requires special floor nailers)
- Your subfloor needs leveling or repair
- The space has complex angles, stairs, or many room transitions
- You want site-finished floors for a seamless look
- You cannot afford to waste material from learning-curve mistakes

**The savings from DIY:** Labor accounts for 40 to 50 percent of the total project cost. On a 500 square foot room, skipping professional installation can save **$1,500 to $4,000**. But improper acclimation (wood must sit in your home for three to seven days before installation to adjust to your humidity level) or boards that buckle can cost nearly as much to fix as the original savings.

The [National Wood Flooring Association (NWFA)](https://www.woodfloors.org) provides installation standards, care guidelines, and a certified contractor locator on its website.

## How to Save Money on Hardwood Floors

**1. Buy direct from a flooring distributor.** Big box stores mark up flooring 30 to 60 percent over cost. Local flooring distributors often sell at or near wholesale to homeowners who ask.

**2. Time your purchase.** Flooring goes on sale in January and February when contractor activity slows down. Buying materials in the off-season can save 20 to 30 percent.

**3. Choose a narrower plank.** Three-inch strip oak is less expensive per square foot than wide-plank oak, and it can look more traditional in older homes.

**4. Look at engineered hardwood.** You get the look of real wood with the durability to handle humidity, often at 20 to 30 percent less than comparable solid hardwood.

**5. Do your own demo.** Pulling up old carpet or laminate before the crew arrives can save **$1 to $3 per square foot** in demolition fees. Most homeowners can handle this with a utility knife, pry bar, and a free afternoon.

**6. Get three written quotes.** Labor prices for the exact same job can vary 40 to 50 percent between contractors. Multiple bids protect you from overpaying and give you leverage.

## What to Ask Before Signing

Before any contractor starts work, get clear answers to these questions:

- Is this quote for prefinished or site-finished floors?
- Does the price include subfloor prep, or is that extra?
- Does it include old flooring removal and disposal?
- How many years of hardwood installation experience do you have?
- Are you licensed and insured in [your state]?
- What warranty do you offer on labor?
- How long will the project take, including acclimation time?

Always verify your contractor's license through your state contractor licensing board before signing anything — the same vetting process that applies [when hiring any home contractor](/blog/how-to-find-good-contractor/) is just as important here. In most states, flooring installers need a general contractor's license or a specialty flooring license to operate legally.

## How Long Does Installation Take?

| Project Scope | Timeline |
|--------------|----------|
| Single bedroom, prefinished | 1 day |
| Living room, prefinished | 1 to 2 days |
| Whole home, prefinished | 2 to 4 days |
| Whole home, site finished | 5 to 7 days |
| Acclimation (always required before install) | 3 to 7 days |

Plan the acclimation window into your schedule before scheduling the installation crew. Skipping acclimation is the most common mistake homeowners make, and it leads to gaps or cupping in the finished floor.

If you are weighing hardwood against alternatives, here is a practical comparison:

| Flooring Type | Installed Cost | Lifespan | Can Refinish? |
|--------------|---------------|---------|--------------|
| Solid hardwood | $8 to $23 per sq ft | 50 to 100 years | Yes, many times |
| Engineered hardwood | $5 to $15 per sq ft | 25 to 50 years | Once or twice |
| Luxury vinyl plank (LVP) | $3 to $9 per sq ft | 20 to 30 years | No |

Hardwood costs more upfront, but its longevity and refinishability make it more cost-effective over the life of a home than [carpet](/blog/carpet-installation-cost/) or laminate, which need replacing every 10 to 20 years.
