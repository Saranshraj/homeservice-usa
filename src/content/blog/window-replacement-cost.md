Window replacement costs between **$300 and $1,500 per window installed**, with most US homeowners paying **$700 to $900 per window** for a standard double-hung vinyl window including labor. A full home with 10 to 15 windows typically runs **$8,000 to $24,000** depending on window type, frame material, and your location. If you are upgrading to ENERGY STAR certified windows, you qualify for a **30% federal tax credit up to $600** per year under the Inflation Reduction Act. That credit alone can meaningfully offset the cost of replacing a few windows per year.

If your windows are fogging up between the panes, letting in cold air drafts, or fighting you every time you try to open them, you are likely dealing with seal failures or aging frames that no longer perform the way they should. Replacing windows is one of the few home improvement projects that simultaneously improves comfort, lowers energy bills, and adds curb appeal.

Drafty windows and poor [attic insulation](/blog/attic-insulation-cost/) often show up together, so if your energy bills feel high, check both. New windows are also a natural pairing with an [exterior paint job](/blog/exterior-house-painting-cost/) since both involve working around the same trim and openings.

## Window Replacement Cost at a Glance

| Window Type | Average Cost per Window (Installed) |
|---|---|
| Standard double-hung (vinyl) | $400 to $900 |
| Standard double-hung (wood) | $700 to $1,600 |
| Standard double-hung (fiberglass) | $600 to $1,400 |
| Casement window | $500 to $1,200 |
| Sliding window | $350 to $800 |
| Picture window | $300 to $900 |
| Bay or bow window | $2,500 to $10,000 |
| Skylight | $900 to $2,500 |
| Garden window | $1,000 to $3,000 |
| Egress window (basement) | $2,500 to $5,000 |

Bay and bow windows are significantly more expensive because they extend beyond the wall line and require structural support. Egress windows require cutting through foundation walls, which explains the high cost.

## The Biggest Cost Factors

### 1. Frame Material

The frame material is usually the first choice you will make, and it has a big impact on both upfront cost and long-term performance.

**Vinyl:** Vinyl is by far the most popular window frame material in the US today for good reason. It costs less, requires virtually no maintenance, insulates well, and does not rot or corrode. A standard vinyl double-hung window costs **$200 to $600** for the unit, plus **$100 to $300** for installation. The main downside is aesthetics: vinyl frames are limited in color options and cannot be painted. Some homeowners feel vinyl looks less premium than wood or fiberglass.

**Wood:** Wood frames are the traditional choice and look beautiful, especially in older or historic homes. They are more expensive at **$400 to $1,200** per unit and require painting or staining every several years to prevent rot and moisture damage. Many "wood" windows sold today are actually wood-clad: a wood interior for the look and warmth, wrapped in aluminum or vinyl on the exterior for low maintenance. These hybrid options run **$600 to $1,500** per window.

**Fiberglass:** Fiberglass is the premium option in most respects. It is extremely durable, dimensionally stable (it expands and contracts less than vinyl with temperature changes, which matters for seal longevity), and can be painted. Fiberglass windows typically cost **$500 to $1,200** per unit. They are especially worth considering in climates with large temperature swings.

**Aluminum:** Aluminum frames are strong and slim, which is why they are popular in commercial buildings and in mid-century modern homes where the thin profile is part of the look. They are poor insulators, though, and can cause condensation in colder climates. Residential aluminum windows cost **$150 to $400** per unit but are not recommended for cold climates without a thermal break.

### 2. Glass Type and Glazing

The glass itself often matters more than the frame for energy performance.

**Single-pane glass** is found mostly in older homes and is essentially no longer sold for new installations in most of the country. A single-pane window has no insulating air or gas between layers. If you still have single-pane windows, every window you replace with a modern double-pane unit will noticeably reduce drafts and heating and cooling costs.

**Double-pane (insulated glass unit):** The standard today. Two panes of glass with a sealed argon or krypton gas fill between them. Argon is the common choice and is included in virtually all mid-grade and above windows. Krypton offers slightly better performance and is more expensive.

**Triple-pane glass:** An additional pane and gas layer for maximum insulation. Triple-pane windows cost **20 to 30 percent more** than comparable double-pane units. They make the most financial sense in very cold climates (Minnesota, Wisconsin, Montana, Alaska) where heating costs are high and windows are a significant source of heat loss. In mild climates, the added cost is rarely justified.

**Low-E coating:** Low-emissivity coatings on the glass reflect infrared heat while allowing visible light through. In cold climates, low-E coatings help keep heat inside in winter. In hot climates, they block solar heat gain. Almost all ENERGY STAR certified windows include low-E glass. This is not an upgrade to skip.

### 3. Installation Type: Full-Frame vs. Insert

**Insert replacement (pocket replacement):** The old sash and glass are removed but the existing frame stays in place. This is faster and cheaper, typically costing **$100 to $200 less per window** than full-frame replacement. Insert replacement works well when the existing frame is still in good structural condition.

**Full-frame replacement:** The entire window, including the frame, sill, and trim, is removed and a new window is installed. This costs more but is necessary if the frame has rot, water damage, or is not square anymore. If you have older windows and suspect hidden moisture damage, full-frame replacement is the right call even if it costs more.

Most contractors will prefer insert replacements because they are faster and more profitable per hour. Push for a full-frame assessment if your home is more than 30 years old or if you have noticed any soft spots, staining, or visible rot around your window frames.

### 4. Labor Costs

Window installation labor runs **$100 to $300 per window** for straightforward residential replacements, adding 20 to 40 percent to the total project cost. Labor gets more expensive if:

- Windows are on upper floors (requires scaffolding or ladders)
- Trim work needs to be replaced or repainted around the new windows
- Any structural work is needed around the opening
- The project involves specialty shapes (arched, octagonal, custom)

Most window replacement companies bundle labor into a per-window price. Independent contractors who buy windows wholesale and charge separately for labor can sometimes cost less, but verify their window sourcing and warranty coverage before committing.

### 5. Brand and Quality Tier

Window manufacturers generally fall into three quality tiers:

**Budget/builder grade:** These are the least expensive windows, often sold through big-box stores. Expect to pay **$150 to $400** for the unit. Quality control and warranty coverage are more variable. Fine for investment properties or rental units, but not the first choice for a long-term family home.

**Mid-range:** Brands like Pella, Andersen, Marvin (entry-level lines), JELD-WEN, and Simonton fall here. Units cost **$300 to $800** per window for standard sizes. These windows offer good performance, solid warranties (often 20-plus years on the glass), and are widely available through window dealers and contractors.

**High-end:** Andersen 400 series, Marvin Infinity, Pella Architect Series, and similar. Units run **$600 to $1,500+** per window. These windows use premium materials, offer the most customization, and typically carry longer and more comprehensive warranties. Worth the investment if you are doing a full-home replacement and plan to stay for many years.

## ENERGY STAR Certification and the IRS Tax Credit

This is one of the most valuable financial angles in any window replacement project and one that many homeowners miss.

Under the Inflation Reduction Act, ENERGY STAR certified windows qualify for the **Energy Efficient Home Improvement Credit (Section 25C)**: 30 percent of the cost of qualifying windows, up to **$600 per year**. This credit resets each calendar year. So if you replace windows in two separate tax years, you can claim up to $600 in credits each year.

ENERGY STAR certified windows meet specific performance standards set by the [EPA](https://www.energystar.gov/products/windows_doors_skylights) for your climate zone. The US is divided into four climate zones (northern, north-central, south-central, and southern), and the required performance levels differ by zone. Most major manufacturers clearly label which products are ENERGY STAR certified and for which climate zones.

See [IRS Form 5695](https://www.irs.gov/forms-pubs/about-form-5695) to claim the credit. Save your receipts and any ENERGY STAR product documentation.

The [Department of Energy estimates](https://www.energy.gov/energysaver/windows-doors-and-skylights) that replacing single-pane windows with ENERGY STAR certified double-pane units can reduce heating and cooling costs by **10 to 25 percent** annually in most climates.

## State-by-State Cost Differences

Labor markets are the main regional driver. Material costs are relatively consistent nationally since most major window manufacturers ship across the country.

| State / Region | Cost vs. National Average |
|---|---|
| California, Hawaii | 30 to 50% above average |
| New York, Massachusetts, Connecticut | 25 to 40% above average |
| Washington, Oregon, Colorado | 15 to 25% above average |
| Illinois, Minnesota, Ohio | 5 to 15% above average |
| Texas, Florida, Arizona | Near national average |
| Southeast (Georgia, Tennessee, Carolinas) | 10 to 20% below average |
| Midwest (Indiana, Missouri, Kansas) | 10 to 20% below average |

In concrete terms: replacing 12 windows that costs $12,000 in Atlanta might run $16,000 to $18,000 in Seattle or Boston.

## Signs Your Windows Need Replacing

Repair can sometimes extend the life of a window, but certain conditions point strongly toward replacement:

**Replace, do not repair:**
- Condensation or fog between the panes of a double-pane window (the seal has failed and the insulating gas has escaped; the window can no longer be repaired to perform properly)
- Visible rot or soft spots in wood frames that extend into the wall framing
- Windows that will not stay open, are painted shut, or have failed hardware that cannot be sourced
- Single-pane windows in any room you heat or cool regularly
- Cracks in the glass (replace immediately for safety)

**Repair may work:**
- Broken sash cords on older double-hung windows
- Failed caulking or weatherstripping around an otherwise sound frame
- A cracked pane in a single-pane window where the frame is still solid
- Sticking due to paint buildup (often fixable with planing and sanding)

A window contractor can give you an honest assessment of which situation you are in. If a contractor pushes hard for full replacement on windows that only need weatherstripping, that is worth a second opinion.

## How Many Windows Does a Typical Home Have?

This matters for budgeting a full-home replacement project:

| Home Size | Typical Window Count |
|---|---|
| Under 1,000 sq ft | 8 to 10 windows |
| 1,000 to 1,500 sq ft | 10 to 15 windows |
| 1,500 to 2,500 sq ft | 15 to 20 windows |
| 2,500 to 3,500 sq ft | 20 to 30 windows |

These are rough averages. Older colonial-style homes tend to have more windows than contemporary open-plan designs. Ranch-style homes often have fewer but larger windows.

At an average of $700 per window, a 15-window home runs approximately $10,500 before any tax credits. With the $600 annual IRS credit, you could spread the project over two tax years and claim $1,200 in credits total.

## Getting Fair Quotes

### Get at Least Three Bids

Window installation is a competitive industry and prices vary considerably. For a 10-window project, bids from different companies in the same market often span a $2,000 to $4,000 range. Always get three bids from licensed contractors.

### Beware of High-Pressure Sales Tactics

Window companies are well-known for high-pressure sales presentations. Some will offer a steep discount that expires "today only." This is almost always a sales tactic, not a genuine constraint. A real discount does not disappear overnight. Take your time, get bids in writing, and compare them on an apples-to-apples basis (same glass type, same frame material, same installation method).

### Ask About the Warranty

A quality window installation should come with:
- The manufacturer's warranty on the product (often 20 to lifetime on the frame, 10 to 20 years on the glass seal)
- An installation workmanship warranty from the contractor (at minimum 1 year, ideally 5 years)

A contractor who will not provide a written installation warranty is one to avoid.

### Verify Licensing

Window installers should hold a contractor's license in your state. Many states also require a Home Improvement Contractor registration for residential window work. Check your state's licensing board before hiring anyone. The [National Association of Home Builders](https://www.nahb.org) offers resources for verifying credentials.

## Can You Install Windows Yourself?

Window installation is technically within the ability of a skilled DIYer for straightforward insert replacements in single-story homes. The risks are real, though: an improperly flashed window can allow water intrusion into your wall cavities, leading to rot and mold that costs far more to fix than a professional installation would have. Most manufacturers also require professional installation to maintain the warranty.

If you want to attempt DIY installation, the [Department of Energy provides installation guidance](https://www.energy.gov/energysaver/windows-doors-and-skylights) and your state's building department can tell you whether a permit and inspection are required. In most jurisdictions, window replacement in an existing opening does not require a permit, but adding a new window or enlarging an existing opening does.

## Financing Your Window Replacement

Most window companies offer financing, but the terms and interest rates vary widely. Before accepting a contractor's financing offer:

- Check the annual percentage rate (APR). Promotional zero-interest periods often revert to high rates (20 to 30 percent) if the balance is not paid in full by the deadline.
- Consider using a home equity line of credit (HELOC) or a personal loan for better terms.
- Look into the [HUD Title I Property Improvement Loan program](https://www.hud.gov/program_offices/housing/sfh/title), which offers federally backed home improvement loans with competitive rates.

The Federal Trade Commission's [Consumer Information on Home Improvement Financing](https://consumer.ftc.gov) is a useful guide for understanding your rights and options.
