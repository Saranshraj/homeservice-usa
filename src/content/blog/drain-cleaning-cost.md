Professional drain cleaning costs **$75 to $650** for most homeowners, with the national average landing around **$200 to $250** for a standard sink or tub clog. If your drain needs hydrojetting (high-pressure water blasting), expect to pay **$300 to $600** or more. Knowing what drives these numbers can save you from overpaying or letting a small clog snowball into a $4,000 [sewer line repair](/blog/sewer-line-repair-cost/).

This guide walks you through realistic cost ranges by drain type, the difference between snaking and hydrojetting, and the warning signs that mean you need a plumber right now — and [what that plumber visit is likely to cost](/blog/how-much-does-a-plumber-cost/).

---

## Average Drain Cleaning Costs by Type

Not all clogs are equal. A bathroom sink clog is a completely different job from a main sewer line backup. Here is what you can realistically expect to pay:

| Drain Type | Typical Cost Range |
|---|---|
| Bathroom sink | $75 to $175 |
| Kitchen sink | $100 to $250 |
| Bathtub or shower | $100 to $225 |
| Toilet | $100 to $275 |
| Floor drain (basement) | $100 to $300 |
| Laundry drain | $100 to $225 |
| Main sewer line | $150 to $650 |
| Multiple drains (whole-house) | $400 to $900 |

**Why is the kitchen sink more expensive than the bathroom sink?** Kitchen drain clogs tend to involve grease buildup deep in the pipe, which takes more time and effort to clear than the hair-and-soap-scum clogs common in bathrooms.

## Snaking vs. Hydrojetting: What Is the Difference?

The two main methods plumbers use are drain snaking and hydrojetting. The right choice depends on how bad the clog is and what is causing it.

### Drain Snaking (Augering)

A drain snake is a flexible cable with a corkscrew tip that physically breaks through clogs. It is the most common first step for standard clogs.

- **Cost:** $75 to $250 for most drains
- **Best for:** Hair, soap scum, toilet paper clogs, single-drain blockages
- **What to expect:** Most jobs take 30 to 60 minutes
- **Limitations:** Does not fully clean the pipe walls, so buildup can return faster

### Hydrojetting

Hydrojetting uses a high-pressure water stream (up to 4,000 PSI) to blast away grease, mineral scale, and debris from the inside of pipes.

- **Cost:** $300 to $600 for most jobs; up to $1,000 for major blockages
- **Best for:** Grease buildup, tree root intrusion, recurring clogs, older homes with scale buildup
- **What to expect:** A plumber will run a camera first to confirm the pipe can handle the pressure
- **Limitations:** Not recommended for fragile, older cast iron pipes without camera inspection first

If you have had the same drain cleaned three or more times in a year and it keeps clogging, hydrojetting is often the better long-term investment even though it costs more upfront.

## Cost Factors That Change Your Final Price

Several variables push the price up or down from the averages above.

### 1. Location in the Country

Labor costs vary significantly by state. Plumbers in San Francisco, New York City, and Boston charge $100 to $200 per hour or more. In the rural Midwest or South, the same work often runs $65 to $100 per hour.

| Region | Typical Hourly Rate |
|---|---|
| Northeast (NY, MA, CT) | $100 to $200/hr |
| West Coast (CA, WA, OR) | $95 to $185/hr |
| Midwest (OH, IN, MO) | $65 to $120/hr |
| South (TX, GA, TN) | $65 to $115/hr |
| Southwest (AZ, NV) | $75 to $140/hr |

### 2. Time of Day and Day of Week

Emergency or after-hours service adds a premium. Weekend and holiday calls typically cost 1.5 to 2 times the standard rate. If your drain is slow but not overflowing, scheduling during regular business hours can save you $50 to $150.

### 3. How Accessible the Clog Is

A clog near the drain opening is quick to clear. One that is 20 feet down the pipe, or in a location that requires moving fixtures or accessing a crawl space, takes more time and costs more.

### 4. Camera Inspection

If the plumber is not sure where the clog is or suspects a more serious issue like root intrusion or a collapsed pipe, they will run a camera down the line first. Camera inspections typically add **$100 to $300** to the job. However, they can save you thousands by ruling out a major sewer problem before you commit to expensive repairs.

### 5. Additional Repairs Discovered

Sometimes what looks like a simple clog turns out to be a broken P-trap, a cracked pipe, or root intrusion. These add to the final bill. A P-trap replacement adds roughly $100 to $200. Sewer line repairs can run $1,000 to $5,000 or more depending on the extent and whether excavation is needed.

## When to DIY vs. When to Call a Plumber

Plenty of simple clogs are DIY-friendly. But others are a sign of a bigger problem that a $15 drain snake from the hardware store will not fix.

### Try DIY First When:
- The clog is in a single bathroom sink or shower and is likely hair
- The water drains slowly but has not fully stopped
- You have a basic plunger or hand-crank drain snake available
- The issue started recently and has not been recurring

**DIY options:** A cup plunger (for sinks) or a flange plunger (for toilets), a hand snake, and an enzyme-based drain cleaner like Bio-Clean can handle most simple clogs. Avoid harsh chemical drain cleaners like Drano for anything more than an occasional use. The [Consumer Product Safety Commission (CPSC)](https://www.cpsc.gov) warns that caustic drain cleaners can corrode older metal pipes, damage PVC connections, and cause chemical burns if they splash back during use.

### Call a Plumber When:
- Multiple drains in the house are slow or backing up at the same time (this points to the main sewer line, not individual drains)
- You hear gurgling from the toilet when you run the sink, or vice versa
- Sewage odors are coming from floor drains or cleanouts
- The clog has not cleared after two or three DIY attempts
- You have a slab home and suspect a leak in the slab
- Roots are in the area around your sewer line

Multiple drains backing up simultaneously is the single biggest red flag. It almost always means the main sewer line is blocked or damaged, and that is not something you can solve with a hand snake. The [Environmental Protection Agency (EPA)](https://www.epa.gov) recommends against flushing anything other than human waste and toilet paper, since "flushable" wipes, paper towels, and feminine hygiene products are one of the leading causes of main line backups nationwide.

## Preventing Clogs: What Actually Works

Prevention is cheaper than any plumber visit. Here are the habits that actually make a difference.

**In the kitchen:**
- Pour grease into a container and throw it in the trash. Never pour it down the drain, even with hot water running. Grease cools as it moves down the pipe and sticks to the walls.
- Use a mesh drain strainer to catch food particles.
- Run cold water before and after using the garbage disposal, not hot water. Cold keeps grease solid so the disposal can chop it; hot water melts it and sends it to coat the pipe.

**In the bathroom:**
- Install a $3 to $8 hair catcher in every shower and tub drain. Clean it after every use.
- Do not flush anything other than toilet paper.
- Avoid bar soap if you have a family with multiple people showering daily; bar soap leaves more residue than body wash.

**Enzyme drain maintainers:**
Enzyme-based products like Green Gobbler or Bio-Clean use bacteria to digest organic matter in pipes. Used monthly, they can significantly slow the buildup that causes recurring clogs. They are not as fast as chemical drain cleaners but they are far safer for your pipes and family.

**Annual professional maintenance:**
If you have had clog problems before or live in an older home with cast iron or clay sewer pipes, an annual drain maintenance visit from a plumber runs $75 to $150 and can prevent a $400 emergency call later.

## Drain Cleaning vs. Sewer Line Repair: Knowing the Difference

One of the most important things to understand is where drain cleaning ends and sewer line work begins.

**Drain cleaning** clears blockages inside your home and in the sewer lateral (the pipe running from your house to the city connection). It is a maintenance task.

**Sewer line repair or replacement** is needed when the pipe itself is damaged: cracked, collapsed, corroded, or infiltrated by roots. This is a much larger job.

Signs you may need sewer line work rather than just a cleaning:
- Camera inspection shows cracks, root infiltration, or pipe separation
- Recurring clogs every few months despite professional cleaning
- Sinkholes or wet spots in the yard above the sewer line
- Multiple drain backup events over a couple of years
- An unexplained [drop in water pressure](/blog/low-water-pressure-house/) throughout the house alongside recurring drain issues, which can signal a compromised main line

Sewer line repair can cost **$1,000 to $5,000** for spot repairs, or **$3,000 to $30,000** for full replacement depending on depth, length, material, and whether excavation is required. Trenchless repair methods like pipe lining and pipe bursting fall on the lower end of the replacement cost range and cause much less yard disruption.

## What Plumbers Check for During a Drain Service Call

Understanding what a plumber does during a visit helps you evaluate whether the work was thorough and the price was fair.

A standard drain service call should include:
1. A visual inspection of accessible cleanouts and the drain opening
2. Confirmation of where the clog is (shallow vs. deep)
3. Snaking or hydrojetting to clear the blockage
4. A flush test to confirm the drain is flowing properly
5. A recommendation on whether camera inspection is warranted

If a plumber quotes you a camera inspection on the first visit for a simple bathroom sink clog without any red flags, that may be upselling. Camera work is valuable, but it should be reserved for main line issues, recurring problems, or situations where something looks wrong.

## Finding a Reliable Plumber

Getting a fair price starts with choosing a licensed, reputable plumber — the same fundamentals that apply when [hiring any home service contractor](/blog/how-to-find-good-contractor/). Here is a practical checklist:

- **Verify the license.** Every state has a licensing board. Search your state name plus "plumber license lookup" to verify any contractor before they start work.
- **Get two or three written estimates** for any job over $200. Prices vary widely.
- **Ask about the service call fee.** Most plumbers charge $50 to $100 just to show up. Ask whether that is credited toward the total if you hire them.
- **Check for flat-rate pricing vs. hourly.** Flat-rate is predictable; hourly can add up if the job takes longer than expected.
- **Read reviews on Google, Yelp, and Angi** before calling. Look specifically for mentions of accurate quotes and no surprise charges.
- **Ask if they guarantee their work.** Reputable plumbers offer at least a 30-day guarantee on cleared drains.

The [National Association of Home Builders (NAHB)](https://www.nahb.org) recommends getting at least two estimates for any plumbing job over $500 and confirming the contractor carries both liability insurance and workers compensation.
