Adding space to your home costs **$80 to $200 per square foot** on average in 2026, meaning a 400-square-foot addition runs **$32,000 to $80,000** before finishing touches. A bump-out addition (extending one room a few feet) starts around $5,000 to $15,000, while a full second-story addition can top $300,000. Where you live matters enormously: homeowners in California, New York, and Massachusetts routinely pay 40 to 60 percent more than the national average, while those in the Midwest and South often pay 20 to 30 percent less.

We know sticker shock is real. You pictured a cozy new bedroom or a sunlit family room, and then you got a quote that felt like it was written in a foreign language. This guide breaks down exactly what drives the cost of a home addition, what you can expect to pay for each type, and how to make sure you get a fair deal.

## Types of Home Additions and What They Cost

Not all additions are created equal. The type of space you're adding, and how it connects to your existing house, has an enormous impact on cost.

### Bump-Out Addition: $5,000 to $30,000

A bump-out is a small extension of an existing room, typically 2 to 10 feet out from an exterior wall. It doesn't require a new foundation in most cases, which keeps costs low. Homeowners use them to expand a cramped kitchen, add a breakfast nook, or enlarge a small bathroom.

**Typical bump-out costs by size:**

| Bump-Out Size | Estimated Cost Range |
|---------------|---------------------|
| 2 feet x 10 feet (20 sq ft) | $5,000 to $10,000 |
| 4 feet x 12 feet (48 sq ft) | $12,000 to $20,000 |
| 6 feet x 15 feet (90 sq ft) | $18,000 to $30,000 |

Because bump-outs are small, labor and permits still cost nearly as much as for a larger addition. The cost per square foot is actually higher than a full addition, often $150 to $300 per square foot.

### Room Addition (Single Story): $22,000 to $100,000

A standard single-story room addition, typically 200 to 500 square feet, is the most common type. This includes a new foundation, framing, roofline tie-in, exterior siding, insulation, drywall, and finish work. Costs depend heavily on what kind of room you're adding.

**Average costs by room type:**

| Room Type | Size | Average Cost |
|-----------|------|--------------|
| Bedroom | 200 to 300 sq ft | $22,000 to $65,000 |
| Bathroom | 50 to 100 sq ft | $18,000 to $50,000 |
| Family room or living room | 400 to 600 sq ft | $45,000 to $120,000 |
| Master suite (bedroom plus bath) | 400 to 600 sq ft | $60,000 to $130,000 |
| Garage conversion to living space | 400 to 600 sq ft | $15,000 to $50,000 |

### Sunroom or Four-Season Room: $15,000 to $85,000

Sunrooms and three-season rooms are popular because they feel more affordable than a full addition. A basic prefab three-season room costs $15,000 to $30,000. A fully insulated, climate-controlled four-season room runs $40,000 to $85,000 and qualifies as true living space.

The difference comes down to insulation, HVAC connections, and the quality of the windows and roof system. A three-season room works great in mild climates, but if you're in Minnesota or Michigan, you'll regret skimping on insulation.

### In-Law Suite or Accessory Dwelling Unit: $40,000 to $130,000

An in-law suite or accessory dwelling unit (ADU) is a fully independent living space, usually with a bedroom, bathroom, kitchenette, and its own entrance. Costs vary based on whether it's attached to the existing home or a detached structure.

| ADU Type | Average Cost Range |
|----------|-------------------|
| Attached in-law suite (400 to 600 sq ft) | $40,000 to $90,000 |
| Detached ADU or backyard cottage | $60,000 to $130,000+ |
| Garage conversion to ADU | $30,000 to $75,000 |

Many states now have laws that make it easier to build ADUs. California, Oregon, Washington, and several others have removed local zoning barriers. Check your local municipality or visit [HUD's resource page on ADUs](https://www.hud.gov) for federal guidance.

### Second-Story Addition: $100,000 to $350,000

Adding a full second story is the most expensive type of addition. You're essentially building a new level of your house, which requires reinforcing the existing foundation and structure, replacing or reworking the roof, and tying in all mechanical systems. In high-cost cities like San Francisco, Seattle, or Boston, a full second story can exceed $400,000.

A partial second-story addition, sometimes called adding a dormer or bonus room over an existing garage, typically costs $40,000 to $100,000 and is far less disruptive.

## What Drives the Cost of a Home Addition?

Understanding what goes into the price helps you have smarter conversations with contractors and avoid surprises.

### Foundation Type

One of the biggest variables is whether your addition needs a full foundation or not. Options include:

- **Full concrete foundation:** $10,000 to $25,000. Required for habitable space with a basement.
- **Crawl space foundation:** $7,000 to $15,000. Common in the South and Pacific Northwest.
- **Slab-on-grade:** $5,000 to $12,000. Most economical; works well in warm climates.
- **Helical piers (for bump-outs):** $3,000 to $8,000. Avoids excavation for small additions.

### Where You Live

Labor rates for general contractors and subcontractors vary dramatically by region. Here's a rough comparison of average addition costs per square foot:

| Region | Cost per Square Foot |
|--------|---------------------|
| Northeast (NY, MA, CT) | $150 to $300 |
| West Coast (CA, WA, OR) | $140 to $280 |
| Mid-Atlantic (DC, MD, NJ) | $130 to $250 |
| Midwest (IL, OH, MI) | $90 to $170 |
| South (TX, FL, GA) | $80 to $160 |
| Mountain West (CO, AZ, UT) | $100 to $190 |

These are ballpark figures. A contractor in a rural area will often bid 20 to 30 percent below a contractor in a nearby metro, even within the same state.

### Permits and Design Fees

Permits for a home addition typically cost $1,000 to $3,000, though in some cities they can reach $5,000 or more. Don't skip this step. Unpermitted work can cause serious problems when you sell, and some homeowners insurance policies won't cover unpermitted additions if something goes wrong.

Architectural drawings cost $2,500 to $8,000 for a custom addition design. For simpler additions, some contractors work from standard plans or help you find a draftsperson who charges less than a licensed architect.

### Utilities and Mechanical Work

Extending HVAC, plumbing, and electrical to a new addition adds significant cost:

- **HVAC extension:** $3,000 to $7,000 to extend existing ductwork, or $5,000 to $15,000 if a new system or mini-split is needed
- **Electrical:** $2,000 to $6,000 for wiring and a panel upgrade if necessary
- **Plumbing (for a bathroom or kitchen):** $5,000 to $15,000 depending on distance to existing lines

## Hidden Costs to Plan For

The quote you get from a contractor rarely tells the whole story. Here are costs that often catch homeowners off guard:

**Unexpected structural work:** Older homes sometimes have headers, beams, or load-bearing walls that need to be reinforced or replaced before an addition can be built. Budget a 10 to 15 percent contingency for surprises like this.

**Utility upgrades:** If your electrical panel is near capacity, adding living space may require an upgrade. A 200-amp panel upgrade costs $2,000 to $5,000. Check with your electrician before construction begins.

**Landscaping and grading:** The contractor's crew will disturb your yard. Restoring it, fixing drainage issues, and reseeding or replanting can add $2,000 to $10,000 depending on the scope.

**Temporary housing:** A major addition, especially a second-story addition, can make your home unlivable for weeks or months. Factor in hotel or rental costs if you have young children or pets.

**Matching existing finishes:** Making the new space look like it was always part of the house is harder than it sounds. Matching siding, flooring, trim, and paint can add meaningful cost, especially in older homes.

## Is a Home Addition Worth It?

This is the question only you can answer, but here is how to think about it.

**When an addition makes financial sense:**
- You love your neighborhood and plan to stay 5 to 10 or more years
- Your local real estate market supports higher home values
- The alternative is a costly, stressful move
- You're adding a space with strong demand, like a bedroom or bathroom

**When you might reconsider:**
- You're in a neighborhood where your home is already at the top of its price range (the addition won't add to resale value)
- You're planning to sell within a few years
- The cost of the addition exceeds 10 to 15 percent of your home's current value

According to data from [NAHB (National Association of Home Builders)](https://www.nahb.org), homeowners typically recoup 50 to 75 percent of addition costs in resale value, though this varies widely by market and type of addition.

## How to Finance a Home Addition

Most homeowners use one of three approaches to pay for an addition:

**Home equity loan:** A fixed-rate lump sum loan secured by your home equity. Rates run approximately 6 to 9 percent in 2026 depending on credit and loan-to-value ratio. Good when you know exactly what the project will cost.

**Home equity line of credit (HELOC):** A revolving credit line you draw from as needed. Better for projects where costs unfold over time. Rates are variable, typically tied to the prime rate.

**Cash-out refinance:** You refinance your mortgage for more than you owe and take the difference as cash. This makes sense if current mortgage rates are favorable compared to your existing rate, which may not be the case for many homeowners in 2026.

Some homeowners also look at the [FHA 203(k) Rehabilitation Loan](https://www.hud.gov/program_offices/housing/sfh/203k) for renovation financing, which wraps the purchase or refinance of a home together with renovation costs.

## How to Hire the Right Contractor

A home addition is one of the largest home improvement projects you'll ever undertake. The contractor you choose matters enormously.

**Start with three bids minimum.** Never accept a single quote. Get at least three detailed written bids, and make sure they're bidding on the same scope of work. The lowest bid is not always the best deal.

**Verify licenses and insurance.** Your state's contractor licensing board maintains a searchable database. The [NAHB](https://www.nahb.org) also has a directory of certified professionals. Ask for proof of general liability insurance and workers' compensation coverage before signing anything.

**Check references from recent projects.** Ask specifically for clients who had similar-scale additions completed in the last two years. Call them and ask whether the project came in on time, on budget, and whether they'd hire this contractor again.

**Get a detailed written contract.** The contract should specify the scope of work, materials to be used, payment schedule (never pay more than 10 to 30 percent upfront), timeline, and what happens if there are cost overruns or delays.

**Understand the payment schedule.** Paying a large chunk upfront is a red flag. A typical draw schedule ties payments to completion milestones: foundation complete, framing complete, rough-in complete, drywall complete, final walkthrough.

## DIY: What You Can and Can't Do Yourself

Some parts of a home addition are DIY-friendly. Others absolutely are not.

**Reasonable DIY tasks (with experience):**
- Painting interior walls and trim
- Installing flooring (tile, LVP, hardwood in some cases)
- Landscaping and grading around the addition
- Cabinet assembly and installation (IKEA-style systems)

**Leave these to licensed pros:**
- Foundation work and framing (structural mistakes are catastrophically expensive to fix)
- Electrical wiring (required to be permitted and inspected in all 50 states)
- Plumbing rough-in (improper work leads to leaks, water damage, and mold)
- Roofing tie-in (one of the trickiest parts of any addition)

The [U.S. Consumer Product Safety Commission (CPSC)](https://www.cpsc.gov) reports that DIY electrical and plumbing mistakes are a leading cause of house fires and water damage. The savings rarely justify the risk on structural or mechanical work.
