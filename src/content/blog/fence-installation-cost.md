Fence installation costs **$1,800 to $6,500** for a typical 150-linear-foot backyard fence in 2026, with most homeowners spending around **$3,200** for a standard 6-foot wood privacy fence. Per linear foot, expect to pay **$12 to $45** installed, depending on material, height, and your local labor market. A short decorative picket fence runs as low as **$800 total**, while a large property enclosed in wrought iron can hit **$20,000 or more**. Knowing these numbers before you call a contractor protects you from wildly inflated quotes.

---

## What You Will Actually Pay: Quick Numbers

If you are in a hurry, here is the condensed version:

| Project Type | Typical Total Cost |
|---|---|
| Small decorative picket fence (50 ft) | $800 to $1,800 |
| Standard wood privacy fence (150 ft) | $2,500 to $5,500 |
| Vinyl privacy fence (150 ft) | $3,500 to $7,500 |
| Chain-link fence (150 ft) | $1,200 to $2,800 |
| Aluminum ornamental fence (150 ft) | $3,000 to $7,000 |
| Wrought iron fence (150 ft) | $5,000 to $15,000+ |
| Split rail fence (150 ft) | $1,000 to $2,200 |

These are installed prices, meaning the contractor supplies materials and labor.

## Cost by Fencing Material

Material is the single biggest driver of your fence cost. Here is what each type actually costs per linear foot, installed:

| Material | Per Linear Foot (Installed) | Lifespan | Maintenance |
|---|---|---|---|
| Wood (cedar or pine) | $12 to $28 | 15 to 25 years | Moderate (stain every 2-3 yrs) |
| Vinyl / PVC | $20 to $45 | 30 to 50 years | Very low |
| Aluminum | $18 to $40 | 30 to 50 years | Very low |
| Wrought iron | $30 to $80 | 50+ years | Low (occasional rust touch-up) |
| Chain link | $8 to $18 | 15 to 20 years | Very low |
| Split rail | $6 to $15 | 20 to 30 years | Low |
| Composite | $25 to $50 | 25 to 40 years | Very low |
| Bamboo | $10 to $22 | 15 to 25 years | Low |

### Wood Fences

Wood is the most popular fencing material in the US, and for good reason: it looks warm and natural, takes paint or stain easily, and costs less upfront than vinyl or metal. Cedar and redwood resist rot naturally and are worth the slight premium over pressure-treated pine in wet climates. A 6-foot cedar privacy fence typically runs **$18 to $28 per linear foot** installed. In humid Southern states, ask specifically for rot-resistant species or treated wood rated for ground contact.

### Vinyl Fences

Vinyl (sometimes called PVC fence) costs more upfront than wood, but if you hate maintenance, the math often works out. You never need to paint or stain it, it will not rot, and it looks the same in 20 years as it did the day it was installed. Expect **$20 to $45 per linear foot** installed. In states with extreme heat, like Arizona or Texas, choose a thicker vinyl grade (0.15 inches or more) because thinner panels can warp.

### Chain Link

Chain link is the no-frills option that just works. It is the go-to for enclosing large properties, pet runs, or areas where looks matter less than function. At **$8 to $18 per linear foot** installed, it is the least expensive fencing category by far. You can add privacy slats for about **$1 to $2 per linear foot** extra.

### Aluminum and Wrought Iron

Both give you that classic decorative look without solid privacy. Aluminum is lighter, rust-resistant, and less expensive at **$18 to $40 per linear foot**. Wrought iron is heavier, more ornate, and significantly more expensive at **$30 to $80 per linear foot**, but it can last a lifetime with occasional maintenance. In coastal areas with salt air, aluminum outperforms wrought iron because it will not rust.

## How Fence Height Affects the Price

Height adds material and labor cost directly. Here is a rough multiplier guide starting from a standard 4-foot fence:

| Height | Relative Cost vs. 4-Foot Fence |
|---|---|
| 3 feet | About 20% less |
| 4 feet | Baseline |
| 5 feet | About 15% more |
| 6 feet | About 30 to 40% more |
| 8 feet | About 60 to 80% more |

A 6-foot fence requires longer posts set deeper in the ground, which adds both material and digging labor. In many cities, 6 feet is also the maximum height allowed in a residential backyard without a variance permit, so check local codes before you plan anything taller.

## Labor Costs Broken Down

For most fence projects, labor is 40 to 60 percent of the total bill. Here is what installers actually charge:

- **Standard installation labor:** $5 to $15 per linear foot (on top of materials)
- **Post hole digging (rocky or clay soil):** Add $20 to $50 per post
- **Old fence removal and disposal:** $1 to $5 per linear foot
- **Gate installation:** $100 to $600 per gate, depending on size and hardware
- **Corner posts and end posts:** Add $50 to $150 each for extra-reinforced posts
- **Concrete per post:** $3 to $8 per post (always ask if this is included)

One thing contractors often omit from quotes: demo and haul-away of your old fence. Get a separate line item for that cost before signing anything.

If you are in a high-cost state and sticker-shocked by quotes, know that **material costs are roughly the same everywhere**. The difference is entirely labor. In California and the Northeast, licensed fencing contractors simply charge more per hour.

## Permit Costs and HOA Rules

Here is something many homeowners skip: **fencing typically requires a permit**, and skipping it can cost you later when you sell — and an unpermitted fence may not be covered by your [homeowners insurance](/blog/homeowners-insurance-claims/) after storm damage.

Most cities and counties require a permit for any fence over 3 to 4 feet tall. Permits typically cost **$50 to $200** depending on your municipality. The permitting office will also check that your fence does not encroach on utility easements or your neighbor's property. Always confirm your property lines with a survey (cost: $300 to $700) if there is any doubt.

If you live in a neighborhood with a **Homeowners Association**, you face an additional layer. HOAs commonly restrict:

- Maximum fence height (often 4 to 6 feet)
- Acceptable materials and colors (wood or vinyl in specific shades only)
- Fence style (no chain link in front yards, for example)
- Where a fence can be placed relative to the street

Always get written HOA approval before a contractor even pulls a permit. Ripping out a non-compliant fence is an expensive lesson.

Your [contractor](/blog/how-to-find-good-contractor/) should pull the permit, not just "help you with it." If a contractor suggests skipping the permit process, that is a red flag.

## DIY vs. Hiring a Pro

Should you install your own fence? It depends on the material and your comfort level.

**DIY makes sense if:**
- You are installing split rail or basic chain link (both are beginner-friendly)
- You have a small yard with fewer than 100 linear feet
- You are comfortable renting a post hole digger and spending a weekend outdoors
- You can save 40 to 60 percent on labor, typically **$600 to $3,000+**

**Hire a pro if:**
- You are installing vinyl or composite (mistakes are visible and hard to fix)
- Your yard has slopes, curves, or irregular terrain
- You need to pull permits and coordinate with utilities for dig approvals (required by law via **Call 811** before any digging in the US)
- You want a warranty on workmanship

Even if you DIY, call 811 at least three business days before you dig. Hitting a buried gas line or electrical conduit is both dangerous and expensive. The service is free and required by federal law in all 50 states. More information is available at [call811.com](https://call811.com).

## How to Get the Best Price on Your Fence

A few habits that consistently save homeowners money:

**Get at least three quotes.** Fencing is a competitive market. The spread between the lowest and highest bid for the same project is often 30 to 50 percent. Quotes are free.

**Ask for an itemized bid.** You want to see material costs, labor costs, post concrete, gate hardware, and demo all broken out separately. Lump-sum bids are harder to compare and easier to pad.

**Buy in the fall.** Fencing contractors are busiest from May through August. October through February is the slow season in most parts of the country, and many companies discount their rates by 10 to 20 percent to keep crews busy.

**Supply your own materials.** If you have a local lumber yard or warehouse club, pricing lumber yourself and asking the contractor to install only can save 15 to 25 percent. Not every contractor agrees to this, but it is worth asking.

**Check licensing.** Most states require fencing contractors to be licensed and insured. You can verify a contractor's license through your state contractor licensing board. The [National Association of Home Builders](https://www.nahb.org) keeps a directory of licensed and vetted contractors.

## How Long Will Your Fence Last?

Maintenance and material choice determine longevity more than anything else. Rough lifespans:

- **Cedar or redwood:** 15 to 25 years with regular staining
- **Pressure-treated pine:** 15 to 20 years
- **Vinyl:** 30 to 50 years with almost no maintenance
- **Aluminum:** 30 to 50 years in most climates
- **Wrought iron:** 50+ years with occasional rust treatment
- **Chain link (galvanized):** 15 to 20 years in most climates

The fastest way to kill a wood fence early: let grass and soil accumulate against the base of the boards. Bottom rot from ground contact is the number-one cause of premature wood fence failure — a detail worth knowing for any new homeowner; our [first-year homeowner guide](/blog/first-year-homeowner-guide/) covers this and similar routine upkeep. Keep a 2-inch gap between the fence boards and the soil surface.

## Return on Investment: Does a Fence Add Value?

According to [Remodeling Magazine's Cost vs. Value Report](https://www.remodeling.hw.net/cost-vs-value/), a midrange wood fence project recoups roughly **50 to 65 percent** of its cost at resale. That is not spectacular as a pure investment, but fences improve daily quality of life in ways that go beyond the resale number: privacy, pet safety, noise reduction, and backyard usability that pairs naturally with a [deck or patio](/blog/deck-building-cost/).

In neighborhoods where fences are the norm, not having one can actually hurt your home's appeal to buyers with kids or pets.
