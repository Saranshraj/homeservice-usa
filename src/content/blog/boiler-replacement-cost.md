Replacing a boiler costs **$3,500 to $8,500** for most US homeowners, with the national average sitting around **$5,200** fully installed. If you have a high-efficiency condensing boiler or a large home, the number can climb to $12,000 or more. If you go with a basic electric boiler in a smaller space, you might land closer to $1,800. The wide range exists because boiler type, fuel source, home size, and your local labor market all pull the price in different directions. This guide walks you through every factor so you can budget with confidence and avoid getting overcharged.

---

## Why Your Boiler Needs Replacing (And When to Stop Repairing)

Before we get into costs, it helps to know whether you actually need a full replacement or just a repair. Boilers typically last **15 to 25 years** with proper maintenance, according to the [Department of Energy](https://www.energy.gov/energysaver/heat-and-cool/heat-pump-systems/boilers). If yours is younger than 15 years and a licensed technician has diagnosed a fixable problem like a bad pump or expansion tank, a repair often makes more sense.

Here is when replacement becomes the smarter call:

- The boiler is over 15 to 20 years old and needs an expensive repair
- Your energy bills have climbed significantly without a change in usage
- You are seeing yellow or flickering pilot flames (a safety concern)
- Repairs are happening more than once a year
- The boiler makes loud banging, gurgling, or clunking sounds regularly
- You notice rust, corrosion, or water pooling near the unit

If your boiler is old and showing multiple warning signs, the repair cost can quickly exceed what a new unit would cost you over the next few years in efficiency gains and avoided breakdowns.

## Boiler Replacement Cost by Type

The fuel source and boiler design are the two biggest price drivers. Here is a breakdown of what you can expect to pay for parts plus installation:

| Boiler Type | Unit Cost | Installation | Total Average |
|---|---|---|---|
| Gas (natural gas) | $1,500 to $5,000 | $1,500 to $3,000 | $3,000 to $8,000 |
| Oil boiler | $2,000 to $6,000 | $1,500 to $3,500 | $3,500 to $9,500 |
| Electric boiler | $700 to $2,500 | $800 to $2,000 | $1,500 to $4,500 |
| Propane boiler | $1,800 to $5,000 | $1,500 to $3,000 | $3,300 to $8,000 |
| Combi boiler (heat and hot water) | $2,000 to $6,000 | $1,500 to $3,500 | $3,500 to $9,500 |
| Steam boiler | $3,000 to $7,000 | $2,000 to $4,000 | $5,000 to $11,000 |

**Gas boilers** are by far the most common replacement in the Northeast and Midwest. They offer a good balance of efficiency and running costs where natural gas service is available.

**Oil boilers** cost more upfront and have higher fuel costs, but they are common in rural New England where natural gas lines have not been run. Many homeowners in Massachusetts, Connecticut, and Maine are converting from oil to gas or heat pump systems during a boiler replacement.

**Electric boilers** have the lowest installation cost and no combustion risk, but electricity rates in many states make them expensive to run day-to-day. They make the most sense in states with lower electric rates or when paired with solar panels.

**Combi boilers** combine home heating and domestic hot water in one unit. They are popular in smaller homes and condos because they eliminate the need for a separate water heater. Expect to pay a premium of $500 to $1,500 over a standard boiler.

**Steam boilers** are more expensive and complex than hot water (hydronic) systems. They are common in older homes, especially in New York City and other dense urban areas with older radiator systems. Because steam systems require specialized knowledge, labor costs are higher.

## Boiler Replacement Cost by Efficiency Rating

The AFUE (Annual Fuel Utilization Efficiency) rating tells you what percentage of the fuel a boiler burns actually turns into heat. A boiler with an AFUE of 80% wastes 20 cents of every dollar spent on fuel.

| Efficiency Level | AFUE Rating | Unit Cost | Best For |
|---|---|---|---|
| Standard | 80% to 84% | $1,500 to $4,000 | Mild climates, budget-focused |
| Mid-efficiency | 85% to 89% | $2,000 to $5,000 | Most homes |
| High-efficiency (condensing) | 90% to 98.5% | $3,500 to $8,000 | Cold climates, long-term savings |

High-efficiency condensing boilers cost more upfront, but they can cut your annual heating bill by 15% to 30% compared to an older 70% AFUE unit. In states with harsh winters like Minnesota, Wisconsin, and Michigan, that savings compounds quickly.

They also require a secondary flue pipe to exhaust condensate, which adds installation complexity. If your existing chimney is not compatible, you may need a new liner or PVC exhaust pipe, adding $500 to $1,500 to the project.

## Cost by Home Size

Boilers are sized in BTUs (British Thermal Units). Undersizing your boiler means your home will not heat properly. Oversizing wastes energy and causes "short cycling," which wears out the unit faster. A good HVAC technician performs a "Manual J" load calculation to right-size your new boiler.

| Home Size | Recommended BTU | Average Total Cost |
|---|---|---|
| Under 1,000 sq ft | 50,000 to 75,000 BTU | $2,500 to $5,000 |
| 1,000 to 1,500 sq ft | 75,000 to 100,000 BTU | $3,500 to $6,500 |
| 1,500 to 2,500 sq ft | 100,000 to 150,000 BTU | $4,500 to $8,000 |
| 2,500 to 3,500 sq ft | 150,000 to 200,000 BTU | $6,000 to $10,000 |
| Over 3,500 sq ft | 200,000+ BTU | $7,500 to $14,000+ |

Climate matters too. A 1,500 sq ft home in Vermont needs significantly more heating capacity than the same home in Virginia. Your contractor should account for your local heating degree days when sizing the unit.

## Labor Costs: What You Are Paying the Tech

Labor typically runs **$1,500 to $3,500** for a standard boiler swap, though it can be higher for steam systems or complex retrofits. The job usually takes one to two days for a straightforward replacement.

Factors that push labor costs up:

- **Old boiler disposal:** $150 to $400, sometimes included in the quote
- **New gas line work:** $500 to $1,500 if the line needs to be extended or resized
- **Chimney liner:** $800 to $2,500 if required for the new unit
- **New expansion tank:** $150 to $400
- **Zone valve replacement:** $150 to $350 per zone
- **Permit fees:** $50 to $350 depending on your city or county
- **Emergency or after-hours service:** add 25% to 50% to the base rate

Always ask for a written, itemized quote. Some contractors advertise a low equipment price and make it up on parts and labor markups.

## Federal Tax Credits for High-Efficiency Boilers

This is one area where many homeowners leave money on the table. The Inflation Reduction Act extended and expanded federal tax credits for energy-efficient home improvements through 2032.

If you install an **ENERGY STAR-certified boiler** with an AFUE of 95% or higher, you may qualify for a **30% federal tax credit, up to $600** through the Energy Efficient Home Improvement Credit (IRS Form 5695). This is a direct reduction in your tax bill, not just a deduction.

Visit the [IRS Energy Credits page](https://www.irs.gov/credits-deductions/energy-efficient-home-improvement-credit) for full details and eligibility requirements. Also check [ENERGY STAR's certified boiler list](https://www.energystar.gov/productfinder/product/certified-boilers/) to confirm the unit you are buying qualifies before you purchase.

Many states and utilities also offer rebates on top of the federal credit. The [ENERGY STAR rebate finder](https://www.energystar.gov/rebate-finder) lets you search by zip code to see what is available locally.

## Boiler vs. Furnace: Should You Switch Heating Systems?

When your boiler reaches end of life, some homeowners consider switching to a forced-air furnace or a heat pump system. Here is a quick comparison:

| System | Pros | Cons |
|---|---|---|
| New boiler | Keeps existing radiators, even heat, quiet | No A/C integration, slower response |
| Forced-air furnace | Can run A/C through same ducts, faster heat | Dry air, duct installation if not present |
| Heat pump | Heats and cools, lowest operating cost in moderate climates | Less effective in extreme cold, higher upfront cost |
| Hybrid heat pump | Heat pump efficiency with gas backup for extreme cold | Higher equipment cost |

If your home already has radiators or radiant floor heat, swapping to forced air usually means running new ductwork throughout the house, which adds $5,000 to $15,000 to the project. For most homes with existing hydronic systems, replacing a boiler with a better boiler makes the most financial sense.

In very cold climates (zones 6 and 7, such as northern Minnesota, Wisconsin, and upstate New York), a cold-climate heat pump can work well paired with a gas backup. The [Department of Energy's heating comparison tool](https://www.energy.gov/energysaver/heat-and-cool) can help you model the numbers for your situation.

## How to Get the Best Price on a Boiler Replacement

Here are the moves that actually save money, versus the ones that backfire:

**Do:**
- Get three written quotes from licensed HVAC contractors
- Ask each contractor to specify the exact unit model so you can compare apples to apples
- Ask whether the quoted price includes permits, old boiler removal, and startup
- Check if your utility offers rebates or low-interest financing for efficiency upgrades
- Time the project for spring or summer when HVAC contractors are less busy and may offer better pricing

**Avoid:**
- Hiring a contractor who asks for more than 30% to 50% upfront
- Accepting a quote over the phone without an in-person assessment
- Choosing a unit solely on price without checking AFUE and warranty terms
- Skipping the permit: an unpermitted boiler can void your homeowner's insurance and create problems when you sell

Always verify that your contractor holds a current license in your state. Most state licensing boards have an online lookup. The [National Association of Home Builders (NAHB)](https://www.nahb.org) and the Air Conditioning Contractors of America (ACCA) maintain directories of certified contractors.

## What a Boiler Replacement Project Looks Like, Step by Step

If you have never been through a boiler replacement, here is what to expect so there are no surprises:

1. **Assessment:** The tech inspects your current system, measures your home's heating load, checks the flue and gas line.
2. **Quote and permit:** You receive a written itemized quote. The contractor pulls permits from your local building department.
3. **Delivery and old unit removal:** The new boiler arrives. The old unit is drained, disconnected, and hauled away.
4. **Installation:** The new boiler is connected to existing pipes, gas or oil line, and electrical supply. The flue is inspected and modified if needed.
5. **Startup and testing:** The tech fills and bleeds the system, tests every zone, checks for leaks, and verifies proper pressure and temperature.
6. **Inspection:** A local building inspector may need to sign off on the installation for the permit to close.
7. **Walkthrough:** A good contractor explains how to operate the new system, where the shutoff valves are, and what maintenance is required.

The entire process from delivery to startup typically takes one full day for a straightforward swap. More complex jobs, especially with steam systems or significant pipe work, can take two to three days.

## Boiler Maintenance to Protect Your Investment

A new boiler is a significant investment. Annual professional maintenance, which typically costs **$100 to $250**, extends the life of the unit and keeps it running efficiently. The [ENERGY STAR guidelines](https://www.energystar.gov/sites/default/files/asset/document/ENERGY_STAR_Certified_Boilers.pdf) recommend annual tune-ups.

Basic tasks a homeowner can handle between service visits:

- Check the pressure gauge monthly (most systems run at 12 to 18 psi)
- Bleed radiators annually before heating season to release trapped air
- Keep the area around the boiler clear of storage
- Test the pressure relief valve annually (carefully)
- Listen for unusual sounds and call a tech if you hear knocking or gurgling

Do not ignore warning signs. A carbon monoxide leak from a cracked heat exchanger is a life-safety issue. Install a carbon monoxide detector on every floor of your home. The [Consumer Product Safety Commission (CPSC)](https://www.cpsc.gov/Safety-Education/Safety-Education-Centers/Carbon-Monoxide-Information-Center) recommends CO detectors within 15 feet of every sleeping area.
