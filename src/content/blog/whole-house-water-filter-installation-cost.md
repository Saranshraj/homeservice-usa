A whole house water filtration system costs **$300 to $4,500 installed**, with most homeowners paying between **$600 and $2,000** for a quality point-of-entry filter and professional hookup. The unit itself runs **$150 to $3,000** depending on the type, while plumber labor adds **$150 to $600**. If you have a home on well water and need a multi-stage system to remove iron, bacteria, and sediment, budget **$1,500 to $4,500** for the full solution. City water homes with concerns about chlorine and taste can get a solid single-stage carbon filter system for as little as **$400 to $900** all in.

Water quality issues affect roughly 77 million Americans, according to research from the [Environmental Working Group](https://www.ewg.org), and thousands of municipal water systems still contain detectable levels of lead, chlorine byproducts, and agricultural runoff. If you have been dealing with cloudy water, odd tastes or smells, staining in your fixtures, or you just found out your area has a contamination advisory, this guide will walk you through realistic costs, the right system for your situation, and exactly what to ask a plumber before you sign anything.

## What Makes Whole House Filters Different From Under-Sink Models

A whole house water filter, also called a point-of-entry (POE) system, connects to your home's main water supply line where it enters the house. That means every tap, shower, washing machine, and ice maker receives filtered water. Under-sink and countertop filters, known as point-of-use (POU) systems, only treat water at a single faucet.

For concerns about bathing, laundry, or appliance longevity, a whole house system is the right choice. For concerns limited to drinking water, a [reverse osmosis system](https://www.nsf.org/consumer-resources/articles/reverse-osmosis-systems) under the kitchen sink (typically $200 to $600 installed) is often the more cost-effective path.

## Whole House Water Filter Cost by Type

Different filter technologies address different problems. This matters because buying the wrong filter for your water quality issue is wasted money.

| Filter Type | Unit Cost | Installed Cost | Best For |
|---|---|---|---|
| Sediment filter (single stage) | $50 to $200 | $200 to $500 | Dirt, sand, rust particles |
| Activated carbon filter | $100 to $400 | $300 to $800 | Chlorine, taste, odor, VOCs |
| KDF/carbon combo | $200 to $700 | $500 to $1,200 | Chlorine, heavy metals, bacteria control |
| Multi-stage whole house system | $400 to $1,500 | $800 to $2,500 | Comprehensive city or well water |
| UV purification system | $200 to $800 | $500 to $1,500 | Bacteria, viruses (well water) |
| Iron filter | $500 to $1,500 | $900 to $2,500 | Well water with high iron content |
| Whole house reverse osmosis | $1,000 to $3,500 | $1,500 to $5,000 | Very contaminated or brackish water |

**The most popular choice for city water** is a two or three-stage system combining a sediment pre-filter with an activated carbon block filter. This setup removes chlorine, chloramines, sediment, and common taste issues for most households, and runs **$500 to $1,500 installed**.

**Well water homeowners** almost always need more. A common well water solution includes sediment filtration, iron removal, UV disinfection, and sometimes an acid neutralizer if your well water is corrosive. These multi-component systems typically run **$1,500 to $4,000 installed** when a licensed plumber handles the work.

## What Affects the Total Cost

### Home Size and Water Usage

Flow rate matters a lot with whole house filters. A small two-bedroom home may do fine with a system rated at 10 to 15 gallons per minute (GPM). A four-bedroom home with two bathrooms running simultaneously needs 20 to 30 GPM or more. Higher flow rate units cost more. If you buy an undersized system, you will notice pressure drops when multiple fixtures run at once, which is frustrating and can damage the filter cartridge faster.

### Water Quality and Testing

Before buying any filter, **get your water tested**. Many homeowners skip this step and buy a system that does not actually address their problem. Home test kits from hardware stores run $15 to $50, but a certified lab test covers more contaminants for $100 to $200. The [EPA Safe Drinking Water Hotline](https://www.epa.gov/ground-water-and-drinking-water) at 1-800-426-4791 can connect you with certified labs in your state.

If you are on a municipal supply, your utility is required to publish an annual [Consumer Confidence Report](https://www.epa.gov/ccr) (CCR) showing what is in your water. Find yours at the EPA's website and use those results to pick the right system.

### Labor and Installation Complexity

Plumber rates vary widely by region. In rural areas of the South or Midwest, labor runs **$75 to $125 per hour**. In Boston, San Francisco, Seattle, or New York City, expect **$150 to $250 per hour**. A straightforward main line installation takes two to four hours. If your plumber needs to reroute pipe, install a bypass valve, or add an electrical circuit for a UV system, add another two to three hours to the bill.

Always confirm your plumber will pull a permit if one is required locally. In many jurisdictions, any modification to the main water supply line requires a permit. Unpermitted work can cause problems when you sell the house.

### Filter and Cartridge Replacement Frequency

The upfront installation cost is not the only number that matters. Whole house filter cartridges need regular replacement, and that ongoing cost adds up.

| Filter Stage | Replacement Interval | Replacement Cost |
|---|---|---|
| Sediment pre-filter | Every 3 to 6 months | $10 to $30 |
| Carbon block filter | Every 6 to 12 months | $30 to $80 |
| KDF/carbon media | Every 12 months | $50 to $150 |
| UV lamp | Every 12 months | $50 to $100 |
| Iron filter media | Every 3 to 5 years | $200 to $500 |

Annual maintenance costs for a standard two-stage system run **$80 to $200 per year** when you replace cartridges yourself. If you hire a plumber for every change, multiply that by three or four times.

## Regional Price Differences

Where you live affects both installation cost and what kind of filtration you actually need.

**High-iron well water states:** Michigan, Wisconsin, Minnesota, Pennsylvania, and large parts of the Southeast have significant numbers of homes on private wells with iron contamination. Iron filtration systems cost more and are almost mandatory in these areas.

**Chloramine treatment areas:** Many large cities (Los Angeles, Houston, Denver, Philadelphia) switched from chlorine to chloramines for disinfection. Standard carbon filters do not fully remove chloramines. You need a catalytic carbon or KDF filter, which costs slightly more.

**Hard water regions:** Arizona, Nevada, New Mexico, Texas, and parts of California have exceptionally hard water. While a water softener is usually the first recommendation (see our [water softener installation cost guide](/blog/water-softener-installation-cost/)), a sediment pre-filter ahead of the softener protects the softener resin and extends its life.

**States with known PFAS concerns:** Michigan, New Hampshire, New Jersey, and North Carolina have documented PFAS (forever chemicals) contamination in some water supplies. Reverse osmosis or activated carbon filters certified for PFAS removal are the solution there. The [EPA PFAS resources page](https://www.epa.gov/pfas) has a map of known contaminated sites.

## DIY vs Hiring a Plumber

Installing a whole house filter yourself is feasible for confident DIYers who are comfortable cutting copper or PEX pipe and working with shutoff valves. The process involves:

1. Shutting off the main water supply
2. Cutting into the main line after the meter and before the water heater
3. Installing compression or push-to-connect fittings
4. Mounting the filter housing with a bypass valve
5. Turning water back on and checking for leaks

**DIY saves $150 to $500 in labor**, but mistakes can cause water damage to your home. If you are not confident with plumbing, the savings are not worth it. If your home has older galvanized or lead pipe, do not attempt this yourself. Any disturbance to old plumbing can release sediment or leached materials downstream.

For a UV system that requires an electrical connection, hire a licensed electrician for that portion of the work even if you handle the plumbing yourself.

## Signs You Need a Whole House Filter

You do not need to wait for a contamination notice to benefit from filtration. These are common signals that a whole house system would help your home:

**Water tastes or smells off.** A chlorine or bleach smell from your tap is normal for city water but unpleasant. A rotten egg smell from hot water suggests hydrogen sulfide, common in some groundwater. Carbon filtration addresses both.

**White or rust-colored staining.** White scale buildup on faucets and fixtures usually means calcium and magnesium (hard water). Rust-orange or brown staining usually means iron. Each requires a different filter approach.

**Cloudy water.** Cloudiness right from the tap is usually sediment or air. If it does not clear within 30 seconds, sediment filtration is likely needed.

**Appliances wearing out early.** Water heaters, dishwashers, and washing machines all degrade faster when the water flowing through them carries sediment, minerals, or oxidizing agents. Filtration protects these investments.

**You are pregnant or have young children at home.** The [EPA and American Academy of Pediatrics both recommend](https://www.epa.gov/ground-water-and-drinking-water/basic-information-about-your-drinking-water) extra caution with water quality during pregnancy and early childhood. Lead exposure in particular has no safe threshold for developing children.

## How to Hire the Right Plumber for the Job

Not every plumber has experience with whole house filtration systems. When you are getting quotes, ask these specific questions:

- Do you work with this brand or system type regularly?
- Will you pull a permit if my municipality requires one?
- Does your quote include the bypass valve?
- What is your warranty on the installation labor?
- Can you source my replacement cartridges, or will I need to order online?

Get at least two to three quotes for any installation over $500. The range between low and high bids is often $300 to $600 on the same job, and the cheapest bid is not always the worst quality, it is just worth asking why the price is different.

You can verify plumber licensing through your state's licensing board. The [National Association of Home Builders](https://www.nahb.org) and [Plumbing-Heating-Cooling Contractors Association](https://www.phccweb.org) both have contractor locator tools that can help you find credentialed local pros.

## Is a Whole House Water Filter Worth the Investment?

For most homeowners, yes. Here is why the math works out:

A quality two-stage system installed costs **$600 to $1,200**. At $100 to $200 per year in cartridge costs, you are spending **$80 to $200 annually** to protect your plumbing, appliances, and family's health. That compares favorably to the cost of a [water heater replacement](/blog/water-heater-replacement-cost/) that burned out early from sediment damage ($1,000 to $3,000) or a [sewer line repair](/blog/sewer-line-repair-cost/) caused by scale buildup ($3,000 to $7,500).

Homes with well water especially benefit because there is no municipal utility filtering the water before it reaches you. The CDC estimates approximately 15% of Americans rely on private wells, and those wells are the homeowner's sole responsibility to maintain.

If you are on city water with average quality, an under-sink reverse osmosis filter for drinking water ($200 to $600 installed) combined with a simple sediment or carbon filter at the main line ($300 to $600 installed) is often the most cost-effective approach.
