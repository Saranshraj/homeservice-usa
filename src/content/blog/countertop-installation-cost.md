Countertop installation costs **$1,500 to $5,500** for a typical kitchen with 30 to 40 square feet of counter space, though prices range from $400 for basic laminate to $12,000 or more for premium marble. The national average lands around **$2,800 installed**, including materials and labor. If you are planning a kitchen update, countertops are often the single biggest line item, and choosing the right material for your budget can make or break the entire project budget.

This guide breaks down real costs by material type, explains what drives prices up or down in your state, and helps you avoid the common mistakes that turn a $3,000 project into a $6,000 one.

---

## What Drives Countertop Installation Costs

Before jumping into material prices, it helps to understand the three main cost drivers:

**Material type:** The stone or surface you choose is the biggest variable. Laminate costs five to ten times less than marble. Everything else flows from this choice.

**Square footage:** Most kitchens have 30 to 50 square feet of counter space. An island adds another 15 to 40 square feet. Contractors price per square foot, so accurate measurement matters.

**Complexity:** Edge profiles, cutouts for sinks and cooktops, backsplash integration, and unusual layout angles all add labor time. A simple rectangle with an undermount sink cutout costs far less than an L-shaped layout with a farmhouse sink and decorative ogee edge.

### Laminate: The Budget Champion

Laminate (brands like Formica and Wilsonart) gets a bad reputation, but modern laminate looks remarkably close to stone at a fraction of the cost. It is water-resistant, easy to clean, and available in hundreds of colors and textures. The downside is that it scratches, chips at edges, and cannot withstand heat directly from pots.

If you are flipping a house, renting a home, or simply need a functional kitchen without a big investment, laminate delivers real value. Expect to pay **$15 to $40 per square foot installed**, or roughly $600 to $1,200 for a typical kitchen.

### Quartz: The Most Popular Choice Right Now

Quartz countertops, made from engineered stone (roughly 90% crushed quartz bound with resin), have become the top-selling countertop material in the US for good reason. They are non-porous (so they resist bacteria and stains without sealing), extremely hard, and available in consistent colors that natural stone cannot match.

Installed cost runs **$70 to $200 per square foot**, with most homeowners spending **$3,000 to $5,500** for a full kitchen. Brands like Caesarstone, Cambria, and Silestone sit at the higher end; builder-grade quartz can be found closer to $70 to $90 per square foot.

The one weakness: quartz can discolor from prolonged direct sunlight and can crack under extreme heat. It is not ideal for outdoor kitchens.

### Granite: The Classic That Still Delivers

Natural granite remains one of the best long-term value countertop materials available. Each slab is unique, it handles heat well, and it can last the lifetime of the house if properly maintained. It does require sealing every one to two years to stay stain-resistant.

Budget granite starts around **$40 to $60 per square foot installed**, making it competitive with mid-range quartz. Premium exotic granite slabs (rare colors imported from Brazil or India) can reach $200 per square foot. Most homeowners land between **$2,000 and $4,500** for a typical kitchen.

### Marble: Beautiful but High-Maintenance

If you have seen gorgeous white countertops in kitchen magazine photos, they are probably marble, and they come with a real tradeoff. Marble etches from acids (think: lemon juice, wine, tomato sauce) and stains from oils. Keeping marble looking pristine requires diligence, quick cleanup, and annual sealing.

That said, marble adds undeniable resale value in high-end markets. Installed costs run **$75 to $250 per square foot**, with most projects landing between $3,000 and $8,000.

### Butcher Block: Warmth at a Mid-Range Price

Wood countertops, particularly butcher block, bring warmth and texture that stone cannot match. They are also the friendliest surface for knife work (you can actually cut on them). The downside is moisture sensitivity: standing water can cause warping and mold growth if the wood is not regularly oiled and properly sealed around the sink.

Expect **$40 to $100 per square foot installed**, or about $1,500 to $3,500 for a full kitchen. Many homeowners use butcher block on a kitchen island and stone elsewhere for a cost-effective mixed-material approach.

## What Does Labor Cost?

Labor typically runs **$35 to $85 per hour**, but most countertop contractors price by the square foot rather than hourly. Labor represents roughly **20 to 30% of the total project cost** on a full stone installation.

Factors that increase labor costs:

- **Sink cutouts:** Add $100 to $300 per cutout (undermount sinks cost more than drop-in)
- **Cooktop cutouts:** Add $150 to $400
- **Edge profiles:** Standard eased edge is usually free; ogee, waterfall, or mitered edges add $10 to $30 per linear foot
- **Seams:** Each seam in natural stone adds $75 to $150 in labor
- **Removal of old countertops:** Usually $200 to $500 for a full kitchen

If your new countertop requires plumbing disconnection and reconnection (to remove the old sink), budget another **$150 to $400** for a plumber.

## Cost to Replace vs. Install New Countertops

**New installation (during a remodel):** This is the least expensive scenario because there is no demo work required. Costs match the per-square-foot ranges above.

**Countertop replacement only:** Adding demo costs ($200 to $500), disposal fees ($50 to $150), and potential minor cabinet repairs bumps total project cost up by **$350 to $650** compared to a new installation.

**Adding an island countertop:** An island typically adds 15 to 35 square feet of material. Budget an extra **$1,000 to $5,000** depending on the material and any waterfall edges you choose.

## DIY Countertop Installation: When It Makes Sense

Most natural stone countertops (granite, quartz, marble) require professional installation. Slabs are extremely heavy (granite weighs 18 to 20 pounds per square foot), require precision templating with laser equipment, and demand diamond-blade wet saws for cutting. A DIY mistake on a $1,500 granite slab is a very expensive lesson.

However, DIY is genuinely viable for:

- **Laminate:** Pre-cut laminate countertop sections from Home Depot or Lowe's can be installed with basic woodworking tools. Budget $200 to $500 in materials for a typical kitchen. Skill required: moderate.
- **Tile:** Ceramic and porcelain tile countertops are DIY-friendly for someone comfortable with tile work. Materials run $150 to $600. Skill required: moderate to high.
- **Butcher block:** Relatively lightweight and cutable with standard woodworking tools. Materials cost $300 to $1,200 for a typical kitchen. Skill required: moderate.

Saving on labor for laminate or butcher block can cut your project cost by 40 to 60%. For stone, the risk of a cracked slab eliminates most of the savings.

## How to Save Money on Countertop Installation

**Buy remnant slabs:** Stone fabricators often have leftover pieces from larger jobs. Remnant granite and quartz can sell for 50 to 70% off retail if your kitchen fits within the available piece size. Ask fabricators directly, or check online marketplaces.

**Choose a simpler edge profile:** An eased or beveled edge looks clean and modern. Upgrading to an ogee, bullnose, or waterfall edge adds $10 to $30 per linear foot for no functional benefit.

**Skip the full backsplash:** Many quotes bundle a 4-inch backsplash strip. Skipping it and tiling later (or never) reduces material costs.

**Get three quotes:** Countertop pricing varies wildly between fabricators. Getting three quotes is especially important for stone, where fabrication quality and customer service vary as much as price.

**Avoid trendy materials:** Ultra-exotic stones surge in price when they become fashionable. Staying with widely available materials (standard granite colors, popular quartz lines) typically saves 20 to 40% compared to trending imports.

## When Should You Replace Countertops?

Countertops do not have a fixed expiration date, but these signs suggest it is time:

- **Visible cracks or chips:** Beyond cosmetic issues, cracks in stone can harbor bacteria and worsen over time
- **Deep laminate swelling or delamination:** Once moisture gets under laminate seams, bubbling and warping follow
- **Persistent staining that will not come out:** Common with unsealed marble or porous concrete
- **Major kitchen remodel:** Replacing just the countertops during a remodel costs less than returning later for a separate project
- **Resale preparation:** Kitchen countertop upgrades consistently deliver strong return on investment; according to data from the [National Association of Home Builders (NAHB)](https://www.nahb.org), kitchen updates remain among the top value-adding improvements for resale

## The Bottom Line

For most US homeowners, quartz or granite offer the best combination of durability, beauty, and long-term value at $2,500 to $5,500 for a standard kitchen. Laminate and butcher block are smart choices when budget is the priority. Marble is stunning but unforgiving for busy kitchens.

Whatever material you choose, get at least three quotes from local fabricators, ask about remnant options, and factor in the cost of sink and cooktop cutouts before you commit to a total budget. The difference between a $3,000 job and a $4,500 job often comes down to those add-ons, not the slab itself.
