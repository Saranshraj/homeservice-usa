A standard two-car driveway (about 600 square feet) costs **$1,800 to $6,000 for asphalt**, **$4,200 to $10,800 for concrete**, and **$9,000 to $22,000 for pavers** in 2026. Gravel is the most affordable option at just $600 to $1,800 for the same size. The wide range comes down to your material choice, the condition of your existing driveway, local labor rates, and whether the ground needs significant preparation before the new surface goes in.

If your driveway is cracking, sinking, heaving from tree roots, or just looks embarrassing every time you pull in, you are not alone. This is one of the most common deferred maintenance projects homeowners face, and the good news is that most replacements are straightforward projects with a clear price range. This guide breaks down every major material option, what adds to the cost, and what you can expect to pay in your region.

## Why Driveways Fail (and When Replacement Makes More Sense Than Repair)

Before spending money on a full replacement, it is worth knowing whether patching the existing surface would buy you a few more years.

**Repair usually makes sense when:**
- Cracks are narrow (under 1/4 inch wide) and surface-level
- Less than 25 to 30 percent of the surface is damaged
- The base underneath is still stable (no sinking or soft spots)

**Replacement is the right call when:**
- Cracks are wide, deep, or interconnected ("alligator cracking")
- Sections are sinking or heaving unevenly
- The driveway is over 20 to 25 years old and showing widespread wear
- Water is pooling and not draining properly
- You are seeing multiple problems across the whole surface

A professional will dig down to check the base material. If the gravel base is compromised or the soil beneath has shifted, even a fresh surface will fail within a few years without proper base repair. Significant soil movement under a driveway can sometimes signal the same ground settling that affects a [home's foundation](/blog/foundation-repair-cost/), so it is worth having a professional assess both if you notice uneven sinking. That base prep is included in replacement cost estimates but is an add-on for repair jobs.

## Cost by Driveway Material

### Asphalt: $3 to $10 per Square Foot

Asphalt is the most popular choice for homeowners who want a durable surface without the highest price tag. A standard two-car driveway (600 sq ft) runs **$1,800 to $6,000** installed, with most homeowners paying around $4,000.

**What affects asphalt cost:**
- **Thickness:** Standard residential asphalt is 2 to 3 inches thick. A longer or heavier-use driveway may need 3 to 4 inches, adding cost.
- **Oil prices:** Asphalt is petroleum-based, so your quote can shift with oil market prices. Summer tends to be slightly more expensive.
- **Regional climate:** Freeze-thaw cycles in northern states mean contractors often recommend thicker base layers, which adds $500 to $1,500 to a typical job.

**Pros of asphalt:** Lower upfront cost, easier to repair, softer surface that handles temperature extremes reasonably well, commonly available contractors in most markets.

**Cons of asphalt:** Needs sealing every 3 to 5 years (roughly $150 to $400 per application), can soften and indent slightly in intense summer heat, has a lifespan of 20 to 30 years with maintenance.

### Concrete: $7 to $18 per Square Foot

Concrete driveways are the most popular upgrade choice for homeowners who want longevity and curb appeal. A two-car driveway costs **$4,200 to $10,800** installed, with most projects landing around $6,500 to $8,000.

**What affects concrete cost:**
- **Finish type:** Plain broom finish is cheapest; exposed aggregate, stamped, and colored concrete each add $2 to $8 per square foot.
- **Thickness:** Standard residential concrete is 4 inches thick. Heavier vehicles or areas prone to freeze-thaw damage may call for 5 to 6 inches, which adds 15 to 25 percent to the cost.
- **Reinforcement:** Wire mesh is standard; rebar reinforcement adds $1 to $2 per square foot but significantly extends the life of the driveway.
- **Demolition and haul-away:** Removing old concrete costs $1 to $3 per square foot and can add $600 to $2,000+ to your total.

**Pros of concrete:** 30 to 50 year lifespan, very low maintenance (no regular sealing needed), heat-resistant, higher resale value contribution, lighter color keeps surface cooler in hot climates.

**Cons of concrete:** Higher upfront cost, can crack in severe freeze-thaw conditions without proper joint cutting, stains more visibly than asphalt, repairs are harder to hide.

### Pavers (Brick or Concrete Pavers): $15 to $40 per Square Foot

Paver driveways are the premium option. Installed cost for a two-car driveway runs **$9,000 to $24,000**, sometimes higher for natural stone like travertine or bluestone.

**Types of pavers:**
| Paver Type | Cost per Sq Ft (Installed) | Lifespan |
|---|---|---|
| Concrete pavers | $15 to $25 | 25 to 50 years |
| Brick pavers | $20 to $30 | 25 to 50 years |
| Cobblestone | $25 to $40 | 50 to 75+ years |
| Travertine/natural stone | $30 to $50+ | 30 to 75 years |

**Pros of pavers:** Beautiful curb appeal that can significantly boost home value, individual pavers can be replaced if damaged, permeable options allow stormwater drainage, extremely long-lasting when properly installed.

**Cons of pavers:** Highest upfront cost, sand-set pavers can shift over time in areas with poor drainage or heavy freeze-thaw cycles, weeds can grow in the joints without proper sealing.

### Gravel: $1 to $3 per Square Foot

Gravel is the most budget-friendly option and works well in rural areas or for homeowners who prioritize function over curb appeal. A two-car driveway costs **$600 to $1,800** installed.

**Types of gravel used for driveways:**
- Crushed stone (most durable, stays in place best)
- Pea gravel (softer look but migrates easily)
- Recycled concrete (affordable, eco-friendly)
- Decomposed granite (popular in the Southwest)

Gravel driveways need regrading and replenishment every few years, typically $200 to $600 per service. They are not ideal for steep slopes, and they require edging to keep the material from spreading onto grass or garden beds.

## What Adds to the Cost of Driveway Replacement

### 1. Old Driveway Removal and Demolition

Removing an existing driveway before installing a new one adds $1 to $4 per square foot to your project. For a 600-square-foot driveway:
- Asphalt removal: $600 to $1,500
- Concrete removal: $900 to $2,400 (concrete is heavier and harder to break up)
- Paver removal: $300 to $800 (pavers can sometimes be salvaged and reused)

If you have a concrete driveway being replaced with asphalt, some contractors can lay asphalt directly over the old concrete, avoiding demolition costs entirely. However, this only works if the concrete base is still structurally sound.

### 2. Grading and Base Preparation

The base layer under your driveway is what makes it last. A proper base is 4 to 8 inches of compacted gravel, and preparing it correctly can add $500 to $2,000 to your project, depending on:
- How much grading the yard needs (flat vs. sloped)
- Whether poor drainage issues need correcting
- Soil type (clay-heavy soil drains poorly and often needs a deeper base)

Skipping proper base prep is the number one reason driveways fail prematurely. Saving $500 now and replacing the driveway in 5 years instead of 25 is not a savings.

### 3. Drainage Solutions

If water currently pools in your driveway or runs toward your foundation, the contractor may recommend:
- **Channel drain installation:** $500 to $2,000 depending on length
- **French drain integration:** $1,000 to $3,000
- **Slope correction:** Included in base prep estimates above

In areas prone to flooding or with heavy clay soil, the [Federal Emergency Management Agency (FEMA)](https://www.fema.gov/flood-maps) recommends evaluating how all impervious surfaces (driveways, patios, roofs) contribute to stormwater runoff. Permeable pavers or gravel can help reduce runoff and may even qualify for local stormwater credits in some municipalities.

### 4. Driveway Width and Size

Most cost estimates assume a standard driveway, but your actual dimensions matter significantly:
- Single car (10 ft x 20 ft = 200 sq ft): $600 to $7,200+ depending on material
- Two-car standard (20 ft x 30 ft = 600 sq ft): the benchmark used in this guide
- Three-car or extended (1,000 sq ft+): $3,000 to $40,000+
- Circular or curved driveways: Add 15 to 30 percent for the additional complexity

### 5. Permits

Most municipalities require a permit for driveway replacement, especially if you are changing the drainage pattern or widening the driveway. Permit costs range from $50 to $300 and are typically pulled by the contractor (the fee is often included in the quote, but ask to confirm).

## Will a New Driveway Increase Your Home Value?

Curb appeal matters, and driveways are one of the first things buyers notice. According to the [National Association of Home Builders (NAHB)](https://www.nahb.org), exterior improvements including driveways consistently rank among the projects most valued by buyers.

**What to expect for ROI:**
- Asphalt driveway: typically recoups 50 to 70 percent of cost at resale
- Concrete driveway: typically recoups 60 to 75 percent
- Paver driveway: can recoup 70 to 80 percent in upscale neighborhoods

If your existing driveway is crumbling, cracked, or stained, replacement likely has an outsized impact because a bad driveway actively reduces perceived home value. In that situation, the ROI calculation changes significantly.

## Tax Credits and Local Incentives

The federal government does not currently offer tax credits specifically for driveway replacement. However, there are some situations where you might find savings:

- **Permeable paver installations** may qualify for local stormwater management rebates or credits. Check with your city or county public works department.
- **Energy efficiency improvements** to your property as a whole can sometimes offset costs. The [IRS](https://www.irs.gov) updates energy-related credits annually.
- **Homeowner's insurance:** If your driveway was damaged by a covered event (storm, tree fall, flooding), your [homeowners insurance](/blog/homeowners-insurance-claims/) may cover partial or full replacement. Document the damage and contact your insurer before hiring a contractor.

## Hiring a Driveway Contractor: What to Look For

This is a significant investment, and the contractor you choose matters as much as the material.

**Steps to hire well:**
1. **Get at least three quotes.** Prices vary widely, and low bids sometimes mean skimped base prep.
2. **Ask about base thickness.** Any reputable contractor should spec at least 4 inches of compacted gravel base for asphalt or concrete.
3. **Verify licensing and insurance.** Most states require contractors to be licensed. Check your state contractor licensing board to verify.
4. **Ask to see completed projects.** Look at driveways the contractor installed 5 to 10 years ago, not just recent work.
5. **Understand the warranty.** Most contractors offer a 1 to 5 year warranty on workmanship. Materials often carry separate warranties from manufacturers.
6. **Get the payment terms in writing.** A reputable contractor typically asks for 10 to 30 percent upfront and the balance on completion. Be wary of contractors demanding full payment before work begins.
7. **Confirm who hauls away the debris.** Old concrete and asphalt disposal costs money; make sure it is included in the quote.

For more guidance on vetting contractors and avoiding common scams, see [how to find a good contractor](/blog/how-to-find-good-contractor/).

## DIY Driveway Replacement: Is It Realistic?

Honestly, for most homeowners, full DIY driveway replacement is not practical.

- **Gravel:** This is genuinely DIY-friendly. You can rent a plate compactor, order crushed stone delivery, and do the work yourself for material cost only.
- **Asphalt:** Asphalt requires a hot-mix batch that needs specialized equipment to deliver and compact. Not a realistic DIY project.
- **Concrete:** Technically possible for small areas or experienced DIYers, but a full two-car driveway pour is a large, time-sensitive job. One mistake (wrong water ratio, slow placement, missed joints) can ruin the whole surface. Most homeowners save money by hiring a pro and not wasting materials.
- **Pavers:** Enthusiastic DIYers can lay pavers, but the key is proper base prep. If the base is not level and compacted correctly, pavers will shift and look poor within a few years.

The [Consumer Product Safety Commission (CPSC)](https://www.cpsc.gov) notes that tool and equipment injuries are common in DIY projects involving heavy machinery and concrete work. Renting a concrete saw, plate compactor, or asphalt equipment without proper experience carries real risk.

## How to Get the Best Price

- **Get quotes in late fall or winter** in northern states. Contractors have less work and may offer better pricing. (Asphalt cannot be laid in freezing temperatures, so timing matters.)
- **In southern states, late summer and fall are often slower seasons** for contractors, which can translate to better availability and pricing.
- **Bundle projects.** If you also need your garage floor resurfaced, patio installed, or a French drain put in, combining projects can sometimes reduce mobilization costs.
- **Ask about removing the old driveway yourself.** If you are willing to rent a concrete saw and jackhammer and haul debris to a local disposal site, some contractors will credit you for the demolition savings.
