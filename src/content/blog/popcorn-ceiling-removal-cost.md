Removing popcorn ceilings professionally costs **$1 to $3 per square foot**, which works out to **$400 to $1,200 for a 400-square-foot room**. If asbestos testing comes back positive, that range jumps to **$3 to $7 per square foot**, because licensed abatement contractors must handle the work. If you go the DIY route and your home was built after 1978, you can scrape a standard room for as little as $50 to $150 in materials. Those are the real numbers, and everything below will help you figure out exactly where your project lands.

Popcorn ceilings, also called acoustic or cottage cheese ceilings, were massively popular from the 1950s through the late 1980s. Builders loved them because they hid imperfections, reduced echo, and were fast to apply. Today, most homeowners want them gone. They look dated, they trap dust, they are nearly impossible to repaint cleanly, and in homes built before 1978 they may contain asbestos.

## The Asbestos Question: Test Before You Touch Anything

This is the most important thing to understand before any popcorn ceiling project. The U.S. Consumer Product Safety Commission (CPSC) banned asbestos in textured ceiling products in 1977, but homes built in 1978 and even into the early 1980s may still have asbestos-containing materials because builders worked through existing inventory for years after the ban.

If your home was built before 1985, get it tested. Full stop.

An asbestos test from a certified inspector costs **$25 to $75 per sample**, and most labs charge another $25 to $50 for analysis. You can buy a DIY test kit at a hardware store for around $30 and mail in a small sample yourself. The [EPA recommends](https://www.epa.gov/asbestos/asbestos-and-your-home) hiring a licensed asbestos professional for both testing and any remediation.

If asbestos is present, do not scrape the ceiling yourself. Disturbing asbestos releases microscopic fibers that can cause lung cancer and mesothelioma decades later. You need a licensed abatement contractor, and costs rise accordingly.

## What Contractors Charge and Why Prices Vary

Popcorn ceiling removal is labor-intensive but not highly skilled work. Most of the price variation comes from these factors:

**Room accessibility.** A standard 8-foot ceiling in a bedroom is simple. A 12-foot cathedral ceiling in a great room requires scaffolding, which adds $150 to $400 per room and slows the job considerably.

**Ceiling condition.** Smooth drywall under a popcorn texture is the best case. But many contractors find that the popcorn was applied directly over bare drywall paper, not over primed or finished drywall. In those cases, wet scraping can tear the paper facing off the drywall, and you end up needing a skim coat (an additional $0.75 to $1.50 per square foot) or even full drywall replacement in damaged areas.

**Multiple layers.** Some ceilings have been painted over repeatedly before the popcorn, which makes removal harder and messier.

**Geographic location.** Labor costs vary significantly by state. A contractor in rural Ohio might charge $0.90 per square foot while the same job in San Francisco or New York City runs $2.50 to $4.00 per square foot.

**Existing paint.** If the popcorn texture has been painted over (very common in older homes), wet scraping becomes much harder. Painted popcorn often requires dry scraping or chemical softeners, which takes longer.

## DIY Popcorn Ceiling Removal: Is It Worth It?

For homes built after 1985 with no asbestos concerns, DIY removal is absolutely achievable on a weekend. Expect real savings of $300 to $1,000 on a typical room.

**What you need:**

- Pump garden sprayer or spray bottle: $20 to $40
- Wide drywall scraper (6 to 10 inches): $15 to $30
- Drop cloths and plastic sheeting: $20 to $50
- Safety goggles, N95 respirator mask: $15 to $25
- Primer and ceiling paint for afterward: $40 to $80

**Total DIY materials: $110 to $225**

The process is straightforward. Cover everything in the room with plastic sheeting because the mess is significant. Lightly mist a 3-foot by 3-foot section of ceiling with water and wait about 5 minutes for the texture to soften. Then scrape gently with the wide scraper held at a low angle. Work in small sections and do not over-wet the ceiling, because soaked drywall tears and buckles easily.

The real DIY cost is time: plan on 2 to 4 hours per 100 square feet, plus cleanup.

**DIY risks to know about:**

- Tearing the drywall paper face requires a skim coat to fix, which often costs more than hiring out the removal in the first place.
- If you discover asbestos partway through scraping, you have created a hazardous situation. Stop immediately, leave the room, and call a licensed abatement contractor.
- Mold is sometimes hiding under old textured ceilings. If you see black or green discoloration during scraping, stop and consult a mold remediation professional.

## What Comes After Removal

Scraping off the texture is just the beginning. After removal, your ceiling needs to be finished before it looks good.

**Skim coating** is applying a thin layer of joint compound over the entire ceiling to fill in imperfections and create a smooth surface. This is often necessary and adds $0.75 to $1.50 per square foot to the project. Many homeowners are surprised that a freshly scraped ceiling still looks rough and uneven.

**Priming** is non-negotiable. Raw drywall or skim-coated surfaces absorb paint unevenly without a quality primer. Use a PVA drywall primer for new or repaired drywall. Cost: $30 to $60 per gallon, covering about 350 to 400 square feet.

**Painting** adds $1.00 to $2.50 per square foot if you hire it out, or $0.15 to $0.50 DIY in materials.

**Re-texturing.** Some homeowners prefer a different texture, such as orange peel or knockdown, instead of a smooth finish. These textures are less demanding to apply over imperfect drywall and cost $0.50 to $1.25 per square foot to spray on professionally.

## Finding and Hiring the Right Contractor

Popcorn ceiling removal is often done by painting contractors, drywall contractors, or general handymen. Here is how to vet who you hire:

**Get at least 3 quotes.** This is labor-based work with significant price variation. A spread of $500 between quotes on a 400-square-foot project is not unusual.

**Ask specifically about asbestos protocol.** If a contractor says they will just go ahead and scrape without asking about your home's age or test results, that is a red flag. Any reputable contractor on a pre-1985 home asks about asbestos first.

**Ask what happens if they find damaged drywall.** Will they charge extra? How much? Get this in writing before work begins.

**Check licensing.** Painting and drywall contractors typically need a contractor's license in most states, though requirements vary. Check your state contractor licensing board to verify. The [National Association of Home Builders (NAHB)](https://www.nahb.org/) offers a contractor locator tool.

**Ask about containment and cleanup.** Removing popcorn texture creates an enormous amount of dust and debris. The contractor should plastic-sheet the room, including furniture, floors, and HVAC vents.

**Get it in writing.** A signed contract should specify the scope of work (scraping only vs. scraping plus skim coat and paint), cost per square foot or lump sum, what triggers additional charges, and the cleanup expectation.

## Financing Your Ceiling Project

For larger whole-house projects, the cost can reach $3,000 to $6,000 or more, which is a real budget consideration.

**Home equity line of credit (HELOC).** If you have equity in your home, a HELOC often offers the lowest interest rates for home improvement projects. The [Consumer Financial Protection Bureau (CFPB)](https://www.consumerfinance.gov/) has a guide to understanding HELOCs.

**Personal loan.** For smaller projects, an unsecured personal loan from a bank or credit union is straightforward and does not require using your home as collateral.

**Contractor financing.** Many larger painting and remodeling contractors offer financing through third-party lenders. Read the terms carefully, as interest rates vary widely.

**Credit card.** For amounts under $2,000, a 0% APR promotional credit card can work if you pay it off before the promotional period ends.

Note: Unlike some energy efficiency improvements, popcorn ceiling removal does not qualify for federal tax credits. Check with a tax professional for any state-level deductions that may apply in your area.

## Is It Worth Removing Popcorn Ceilings Before Selling?

Real estate professionals generally say yes, with one caveat: do the math on your specific market first.

In most US housing markets, buyers view popcorn ceilings as a negative. They associate them with dated homes, difficult maintenance, and the possibility of asbestos. Listing agents commonly say that updated ceilings help homes sell faster, though quantifying the exact price premium is difficult.

The calculus shifts in the presence of asbestos. Professional abatement plus skim coat plus paint in a 1,500-square-foot home could run $12,000 to $18,000. In that case, disclosing the presence of asbestos and adjusting the asking price accordingly is often more practical than full remediation before listing. A local real estate attorney can advise on your state's disclosure requirements.

For homes without asbestos, a professional ceiling refresh before listing typically costs $2,000 to $5,000 for a full house and is usually money well spent in competitive markets.

## A Few Things to Watch Out For

**Lead paint.** Homes built before 1978 may also have lead paint on the ceiling. If you are disturbing the surface at all, including for asbestos testing, consider having a lead test done at the same time. The EPA's [Renovation, Repair and Painting (RRP) Rule](https://www.epa.gov/lead/renovation-repair-and-painting-program-information-renovators) governs work in homes with lead paint.

**Mold under the texture.** Water stains on popcorn ceilings often hide more serious mold problems underneath. If you see brownish rings or discoloration, have a mold inspector evaluate the ceiling before you scrape. The [EPA's mold guide](https://www.epa.gov/mold) is a useful reference.

**Second-floor bathroom leaks.** Brown stains below a bathroom are almost always active or past water damage, and the problem needs to be fixed before any ceiling work begins.
