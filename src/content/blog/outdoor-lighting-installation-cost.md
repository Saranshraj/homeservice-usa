Installing outdoor lighting is one of the smartest investments you can make as a homeowner. It adds security, makes your home look stunning after dark, and can even help your property sell faster. But what does it actually cost? **Most homeowners spend between $200 and $6,000 on outdoor lighting installation, with the national average landing around $1,200 to $1,500 for a standard residential project.** Simple single-fixture swaps start as low as $75 to $150, while full landscape lighting systems with pathway lights, uplights, and smart controls can push past $10,000 for larger properties.

If you just want the short answer before deciding to call a pro: budget $800 to $2,000 for a modest front yard refresh with 6 to 10 fixtures, and $2,500 to $5,000 for a full front-and-backyard system with pathway lighting, spotlights, and smart features.

## Why Outdoor Lighting Costs What It Does

Outdoor lighting prices swing widely because "outdoor lighting" covers everything from swapping a single porch light to burying 400 feet of low-voltage wire for 30 landscape fixtures. Three factors drive cost more than anything else:

**1. Fixture type and count.** Low-voltage landscape lights cost $20 to $150 each. Line-voltage security floodlights run $50 to $300 per fixture. Decorative post lanterns go from $100 to $800 or more. Every light you add is another fixture + labor cost.

**2. Line voltage vs. low voltage.** Line-voltage systems (120V) power security lights, hardwired wall sconces, and post lamps. They require a licensed electrician and dedicated circuits, which means more permitting and labor. Low-voltage landscape systems (12V) run off a transformer and are far cheaper to install, but are typically limited to path lights, step lights, and ground-level uplighting.

**3. New wiring vs. existing circuit.** Replacing a fixture on an existing circuit is quick and inexpensive. Running new wire through walls, across a yard, or adding a new circuit breaker adds $200 to $1,500 or more to the job.

## What Makes Your Project Cost More or Less

Understanding these variables helps you have a more informed conversation with your electrician and avoid sticker shock on the quote.

### Trenching and Conduit

If you are burying wire underground, you are paying for trenching. Low-voltage landscape wire can be laid in a shallow, 6-inch trench that a homeowner can dig. But line-voltage wire running outdoors must be buried in rigid conduit at least 6 inches deep (or 12 to 24 inches depending on conduit type and local code). Trench digging runs $5 to $12 per linear foot when done by hand, and $2 to $5 per foot with a machine. A 100-foot run can add $500 to $1,200 in labor alone.

### Permits and Inspections

Most line-voltage exterior electrical work requires a permit. In most US cities, permits cost $50 to $300 for residential electrical work. Some counties waive permits for simple fixture swaps; others require them for anything touching the main panel. Your electrician should pull the permit as part of the job. If they say "we don't need a permit for this," that is a red flag. Always ask upfront.

### Smart Home Integration

Smart outdoor lighting has exploded in the last few years, and for good reason. Systems from brands like Kichler, VOLT, and FX Luminaire can be controlled from your phone, sync with sunset/sunrise, and integrate with Alexa or Google Home. The catch is a higher upfront cost. A smart low-voltage transformer runs $150 to $400 versus $40 to $120 for a basic unit. Smart individual fixtures add $20 to $80 per light compared to standard versions. The total upgrade typically adds $400 to $1,200 to a system but saves money on your electric bill over time through better scheduling.

### Number of Circuits and Panel Capacity

Adding multiple line-voltage fixtures, especially for a large security or decorative installation, may require adding a new circuit to your electrical panel. A new dedicated 20-amp outdoor circuit costs $200 to $600 installed, depending on panel access and wire run length. If your panel is already near capacity, you may need a panel upgrade, which is a separate project costing $1,500 to $4,000.

California, Illinois, and Oregon have strict Title 24/local energy codes that may require LED-only fixtures and specific fixture ratings, which can add $100 to $400 to a project budget.

## Low-Voltage vs. Line-Voltage: Which Should You Choose?

If you are on the fence, here is the simple breakdown. Low-voltage (12V) is the right choice for most landscape lighting: pathway lights, uplights for trees and shrubs, step lights, and garden accents. It is safer, cheaper to install (no permit usually required), and DIY-friendly. A quality 150-watt transformer costs $80 to $200, and you can run multiple light strings off a single unit.

Line-voltage (120V) is necessary for security lights, wall-mounted porch fixtures, post lamps tied to a circuit, and anything requiring significant brightness for a large driveway or parking area. These jobs always require a licensed electrician, and in most states, a homeowner cannot legally do this work themselves unless they live in a state with a homeowner exemption (Ohio, Texas, and several others allow homeowners to pull their own permits for work on their primary residence, with inspection).

When in doubt, consult your local building department or state licensing board before starting any electrical project.

## LED vs. Halogen vs. Incandescent: Operating Costs Matter

The fixture installation cost is just one part of the picture. Your ongoing electricity cost matters too, especially for lights you run several hours per night. According to the U.S. Department of Energy, LED bulbs use at least 75% less energy than incandescent bulbs and last 15 to 25 times longer.

Here is a real-world comparison for a single 60W-equivalent outdoor fixture running 5 hours per night:

- **Incandescent (60W):** Costs about $11 to $13 per year in electricity at average US rates
- **Halogen (43W):** Costs about $8 to $10 per year
- **LED (9W):** Costs about $1.50 to $2 per year

For a system with 20 fixtures, switching from incandescent to LED saves $185 to $225 per year in electricity alone. That often pays for itself within two to three years on a new installation.

## How to Save Money on Outdoor Lighting

You do not have to spend a fortune to get a great result. These strategies genuinely work:

**Start with low-voltage for landscape areas.** Low-voltage path and accent lights are inexpensive, safe enough for a confident DIYer, and look just as good as hardwired systems. Kits from VOLT, Hampton Bay, and Ring start at $80 to $200 for 8 to 10 lights.

**Batch your electrical work.** If you are already having an electrician for another project, like adding an outlet or panel work, ask them to also run your outdoor circuit at the same time. The trip charge and setup are already built in, and you will pay significantly less for the additional run.

**Buy fixtures yourself.** Most electricians mark up fixtures 15 to 40%. Buy fixtures through Amazon, Home Depot, or a lighting specialty store and have your electrician supply only the labor. Make sure to confirm this arrangement upfront since some contractors only warranty the work if they supply materials.

**Choose solar for remote areas.** For pathway lights far from the house, solar-powered units eliminate the wire run entirely. Modern solar path lights from brands like Aootek and Litom cost $3 to $15 per light and work well in most US climates. They are not as reliable in the Pacific Northwest or northern regions in winter, but they are a genuinely smart choice for sunny regions.

**Get permits properly.** This sounds counterintuitive as a cost-saving tip, but unpermitted electrical work can cost you dearly at resale. Buyers' home inspectors flag unpermitted exterior electrical, and you may have to tear out and redo work that would have cost $100 to permit originally.

## DIY vs. Professional Installation

For low-voltage landscape systems, confident DIYers can absolutely install the transformer, run wire, and place fixtures themselves over a weekend. The total time is usually 4 to 8 hours for a 10 to 15 light system. The National Electrical Manufacturers Association (NEMA) notes that low-voltage outdoor systems are among the most DIY-friendly electrical projects homeowners can take on.

For line-voltage work, meaning anything that taps your main electrical system, hiring a licensed electrician is both the legal requirement in most states and the smart safety choice. Outdoor wiring exposed to moisture, temperature swings, and physical damage is no place for amateur work. The CPSC reports that electrical failures are a leading cause of home fires; exterior wiring is particularly vulnerable when improperly installed.

To verify your electrician is properly licensed, check your state's contractor licensing board website. Most states have online lookup tools. In states like California (CSLB), Texas (TDLR), and Florida (DBPR), license verification takes under two minutes and protects you from unlicensed operators.

## Signs Your Outdoor Lighting Needs Attention

If you already have outdoor lighting but it is not working right, these symptoms deserve a call to an electrician:

- Flickering or dimming fixtures despite new bulbs
- GFCI outlet tripping repeatedly near outdoor fixtures
- Corroded or discolored wire connections in the fixture housing
- Lights that work intermittently after rain
- A burning smell near an outdoor fixture or junction box

Any of these could indicate a wiring problem, a compromised weatherproof seal, or a fixture that has reached the end of its life. Do not ignore them, especially moisture-related issues, which can escalate to a short circuit or fire.

## Questions to Ask Before Hiring an Electrician

Before you sign a contract for outdoor lighting work, ask these questions:

1. Are you licensed and insured in this state, and can I see proof?
2. Will you pull the required permits for this job?
3. Is the quote for parts and labor, or labor only?
4. Are the fixtures you are using rated for wet or damp outdoor locations?
5. What is your warranty on both parts and labor?
6. How long will the job take, and will I need my electricity shut off?

A reputable electrician will answer all of these without hesitation. If someone dodges the permit question or cannot provide a license number, move on.

## What Outdoor Lighting Does for Your Home's Value

Real estate professionals consistently rank exterior lighting among the highest-ROI improvements for curb appeal. The National Association of Realtors' remodeling impact reports have found landscape lighting among the top curb appeal projects for recouping costs at sale. Well-lit homes photograph better, show better at evening open houses, and signal to buyers that the exterior has been maintained.

Beyond aesthetics, security lighting is a documented deterrent to property crime. A study cited by the Bureau of Justice Statistics found that improved lighting around a property correlates with reduced break-in rates in residential neighborhoods. This also matters for your homeowners insurance: some insurers offer small discounts for security lighting, so it is worth a call to your agent after installation.
