Replacing knob and tube wiring in your home typically costs **$3,500 to $15,000**, with most homeowners paying around **$7,000 to $9,000** for a 1,500-square-foot house. If your older home still has this outdated system, you are probably facing higher homeowners insurance premiums right now, and many insurers refuse to cover homes with active knob and tube wiring at all. The good news is that the replacement process is well-understood, and licensed electricians do it every day in older neighborhoods across the country.

## What Is Knob and Tube Wiring?

Knob and tube (K&T) wiring was the standard method for wiring American homes from the 1880s through the 1940s. It uses separate hot and neutral wires supported by ceramic knobs (which hold the wire away from wood framing) and porcelain tubes (which guide wire through joists and studs). There is no ground wire, no plastic sheathing, and no way to add a third-prong grounded outlet without additional work.

If your home was built before 1950, there is a good chance it still has at least some K&T wiring, especially in older sections of the house like the basement, attic, or original kitchen circuits.

The core problems with knob and tube wiring today are:

- **No ground wire.** Modern appliances, computers, and power strips rely on a grounded circuit for safety. K&T has none.
- **Cloth insulation degradation.** The original rubber and cloth insulation becomes brittle and crumbles with age, exposing live wires.
- **Incompatibility with insulation.** Building codes in most states prohibit covering K&T wiring with insulation, which makes energy upgrades impossible until the wiring is replaced. The Consumer Product Safety Commission has flagged this as a fire hazard for decades.
- **Low amperage capacity.** Original K&T circuits were designed for 15 amps. Modern kitchens alone may need 50 to 100 amps of dedicated circuits.

## What Affects the Total Cost

### 1. How Much K&T Is Still Active

Some electricians distinguish between "active" K&T (still connected and carrying current) and "deactivated" K&T (cut off and left in walls). Active wiring is the safety concern and the insurance problem. If a previous owner already replaced most of the system but left a few active circuits, your cost can be considerably lower than a full replacement.

An electrician will do a panel inspection and trace which circuits are still fed by old wiring before giving you a quote.

### 2. Accessibility

In homes where the walls and ceilings are plaster (common in pre-1950 construction), running new wires requires either drilling through walls or opening them up. Plaster walls are more expensive to work in and repair than drywall. Expect to pay 15 to 30 percent more if your home has plaster throughout.

Attic and basement access makes a huge difference. If an electrician can run wires through open attic space and down through walls without opening them, the job goes faster and costs less.

### 3. Panel Upgrade

Many homes with K&T wiring also have undersized fuse boxes. A 60-amp fuse panel cannot safely power a modern home; most electricians recommend at least 100 amps for a small home and 200 amps for anything over 1,500 square feet. If you need a panel upgrade at the same time, budget an extra **$1,500 to $4,000**.

The U.S. Department of Energy notes that electrical system upgrades are often necessary when making other energy efficiency improvements, like adding insulation, since you cannot insulate around active K&T wiring legally in most states.

### 4. Local Labor Rates

Electrician labor ranges from **$50 to $120 per hour** depending on where you live. High cost-of-living states like California, Massachusetts, New York, and Hawaii tend to be on the high end. States in the South and Midwest generally run lower.

### 5. Permits and Inspections

Most states require a permit for electrical rewiring work. Permit fees typically run **$100 to $500** depending on your city or county. Your electrician will usually pull the permit on your behalf and include it in the quote, but always confirm this.

Do not skip the permit. An uninspected electrical job can void your homeowners insurance and create serious problems when you sell. The National Fire Protection Association's NFPA 70 (the National Electrical Code) sets the standards your electrician must meet, and an inspection confirms the work passes.

### 6. Additional Repairs

After rewiring, walls and ceilings may need patching. Many electricians do not include drywall repair in their quotes. Budget an additional **$500 to $2,000** for patching and painting if walls need to be opened, depending on extent.

## The Insurance Problem Is Real

Here is something that catches many homeowners off guard: a large number of major insurance carriers will not write a new homeowners policy on a home with active knob and tube wiring. Others will cover it but charge significantly higher premiums or require a home inspection before renewal.

According to the Insurance Information Institute, electrical failures and malfunctions are one of the top causes of house fires in the United States. Insurers view K&T wiring as a material risk factor.

If you are buying a home with K&T wiring, ask your insurance agent directly whether your preferred carrier will cover it and at what cost. Some buyers negotiate a credit from the seller to cover rewiring, since the cost is a known liability.

If you already own a home with K&T, call your insurer and ask whether you are currently covered and whether replacing the wiring would lower your premium. Many homeowners discover they can recover part of the rewiring cost through reduced premiums over five to ten years.

## Can You DIY Knob and Tube Replacement?

Technically, some states allow homeowners to do their own electrical work with a permit and inspection. Practically speaking, this is not a good candidate for DIY.

Knob and tube replacement involves working in tight spaces, tracing old wiring that may not follow any logical pattern, understanding load calculations, and ensuring every connection meets current code. It is also high-stakes work in a fire safety context.

The U.S. Consumer Product Safety Commission strongly recommends hiring licensed electricians for any work involving the replacement of old wiring systems. This is one job where the peace of mind of a professional inspection is worth every dollar.

If you want to cut costs, focus instead on getting three to four quotes from licensed electricians, asking about off-season scheduling, and bundling the work with any panel upgrades you need.

## What the Replacement Process Looks Like

Understanding what will happen helps you prepare your home and your schedule.

**Step 1: Assessment.** A licensed electrician visits to identify all active K&T circuits, assess panel capacity, and check for other issues like aluminum wiring or double-tapped breakers.

**Step 2: Permit pulled.** Your electrician submits permit applications with your local building department.

**Step 3: Rewiring.** The crew runs new Romex (non-metallic sheathed cable with a ground wire) throughout the home, replacing old circuits one at a time. Depending on home size, this takes two to five days.

**Step 4: Panel work.** If upgrading the panel, that is done in conjunction with the rewiring, often requiring a brief utility shutoff coordinated with your electric company.

**Step 5: Inspection.** A local building inspector visits to verify the work meets NEC standards. Your electrician should be present for this.

**Step 6: Patching.** Any walls or ceilings opened during the job are patched. Some electricians include this; most do not. Clarify upfront.

**Step 7: Final documentation.** You receive a certificate of completion or inspection sign-off. Keep this document in a safe place, as you will likely need it when you sell or when updating your insurance.

## Signs You Have Knob and Tube Wiring

If you are not sure whether your home has active K&T wiring, here are the signs to look for:

- Two-prong outlets throughout the house (no ground slot)
- Round fuse box instead of a modern circuit breaker panel
- Cloth-covered wires visible in the attic or basement
- Ceramic knobs or tubes visible attached to joists and studs in unfinished areas
- Wiring that looks like two separate wires running parallel rather than a single sheathed cable

If you see any of these, have a licensed electrician perform an assessment before you add attic insulation, do any renovation work, or list your home for sale.

## Will Homebuyers Find Out?

Yes. Any competent home inspector will note active K&T wiring in an inspection report. It will appear in writing and will be disclosed to the buyer's agent and lender. Some mortgage lenders require K&T wiring to be replaced as a condition of approving the loan, particularly FHA and VA loans, which follow HUD's minimum property standards.

If you are selling a home with K&T, your options are to replace it before listing (and potentially command a higher price), offer a price reduction to reflect the buyer's future cost, or negotiate a credit at closing. In a competitive market, replacing it first puts you in a much stronger position.

## How to Get the Best Quote

A few practical steps will save you money and stress:

1. **Get three to four quotes in writing.** Prices for electrical work vary significantly between contractors. A written quote protects you if the scope changes.
2. **Ask what is included.** Make sure the quote covers permits, all materials (wire, boxes, outlets, switches), and labor. Ask explicitly whether drywall patching is included.
3. **Verify licensing and insurance.** Your electrician must be licensed in your state and carry liability insurance and workers' compensation. You can verify licenses at your state's electrical licensing board website.
4. **Ask about timing.** Rewiring jobs scheduled during slower seasons (late fall and early winter) may come in lower than peak spring and summer rates.
5. **Ask about financing.** Many electrical contractors offer financing plans. You can also check whether your utility company offers low-interest loans for electrical upgrades through energy efficiency programs.

## Tax Credits and Rebates

Replacing old wiring does not directly qualify for federal tax credits under the Inflation Reduction Act. However, if you upgrade your electrical panel to 200 amps in connection with adding an EV charger, heat pump, or other qualifying equipment, you may be eligible for credits on those items. The IRS publishes current guidelines on home energy credits at [irs.gov](https://www.irs.gov/credits-deductions/energy-efficient-home-improvement-credit).

Some state and local utility programs offer rebates for electrical upgrades. Check with your utility company and state energy office for current programs.
