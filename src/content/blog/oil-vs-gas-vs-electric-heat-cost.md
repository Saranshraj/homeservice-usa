If you are trying to figure out which heating fuel will cost you the least this winter, here is the straight answer: natural gas is the cheapest option for most of the country, averaging **$900 to $1,400 per year** for a typical 1,500 to 2,000 square foot home. Oil heat runs significantly higher at **$1,600 to $2,800 per year**, making it the most expensive conventional option. Electric resistance heat falls in between at **$1,200 to $2,000 per year**, but an electric heat pump can actually beat natural gas in mild climates, costing as little as **$600 to $1,100 annually**. Where you live, what equipment you have, and how well your home is insulated all shape the final number.

## Heating Costs at a Glance

Before diving into the details, here is a side-by-side comparison based on average 2026 energy prices from the U.S. Energy Information Administration (EIA):

| Fuel Type | Avg. Annual Cost | Efficiency Range | Best For |
|-----------|-----------------|-----------------|----------|
| Natural gas furnace | $900 - $1,400 | 80% - 98% AFUE | Most of the US |
| Oil furnace/boiler | $1,600 - $2,800 | 80% - 95% AFUE | Northeast, rural areas |
| Electric resistance | $1,200 - $2,000 | 100% | Low-usage homes |
| Electric heat pump | $600 - $1,100 | 200% - 400% effective | South, Pacific Coast |
| Propane | $1,400 - $2,200 | 80% - 98% AFUE | Rural, no gas access |

These figures assume a typical American home of about 1,800 square feet with average insulation. Your actual costs can swing 30 to 40 percent depending on local utility rates, climate zone, and how well your home holds in heat.

## Why Fuel Comparisons Are Tricky

Comparing heating costs across fuel types is not as simple as looking at the price per unit at the pump or on your utility bill. Each fuel has different energy content, and furnaces have different efficiencies. The honest way to compare them is on a cost-per-million-BTU basis, where one million BTUs represents roughly the same amount of heat delivered to your home.

Here are approximate 2026 cost-per-million-BTU figures based on EIA national averages:

| Fuel | Avg. Price | Cost per Million BTU (Delivered) |
|------|-----------|----------------------------------|
| Natural gas | $1.10/therm | $11 - $14 |
| Heating oil | $3.60/gallon | $29 - $37 |
| Propane | $2.75/gallon | $29 - $38 |
| Electricity (resistance) | $0.17/kWh | $50 - $60 |
| Electricity (heat pump) | $0.17/kWh | $13 - $25 |

The reason a heat pump can compete with natural gas, even though electricity costs more per unit of energy, is that it does not generate heat. It moves heat from outside air into your home, delivering 2 to 4 units of heat for every unit of electricity it consumes. That ratio, called the coefficient of performance (COP), flips the math in favor of heat pumps in climates that do not spend weeks below 10 degrees Fahrenheit.

## Natural Gas: The Workhorse for Most Homeowners

Natural gas is the most common heating fuel in the US, used by roughly 50 percent of homes according to the EIA. It burns cleanly, heats up fast, and the infrastructure is already in place for most suburban and urban neighborhoods.

**Where gas shines:** The Midwest, Mid-Atlantic, Mountain West, and most metro areas. If you have a gas line running to your house, upgrading to a high-efficiency furnace (95+ AFUE) can cut your bills by 25 percent compared to an older 80 percent model.

**Annual costs by efficiency:**
- 80% AFUE furnace: $1,100 - $1,400
- 90-95% AFUE furnace: $950 - $1,200
- 97-98% AFUE condensing furnace: $900 - $1,100

**The catch:** Gas prices are volatile. Supply disruptions or cold snaps can cause short-term price spikes of 30 to 60 percent. If your gas utility has time-of-use pricing, heating at night can help. And new homes in some California and New York municipalities now face restrictions on gas appliance installation, so check local regulations before you invest in a new gas system.

## Heating Oil: Expensive but Still Necessary for Millions

About 4.5 million American homes rely on heating oil, the vast majority in New England. If you are in Maine, Vermont, New Hampshire, or Connecticut, oil heat is not unusual. If you are anywhere else in the country, it is rare enough that your neighbors probably cannot give you a referral for a good oil delivery service.

**Why oil is so expensive:** Heating oil is a refined petroleum product that must be trucked to your home and stored in a tank. You are paying for the fuel itself, the storage tank maintenance, and the delivery logistics. Oil prices also track crude oil markets closely, meaning they can jump dramatically when geopolitical events disrupt supply.

**Annual costs in 2026:** Expect $1,600 to $2,800 for a typical New England home, with the Northeast average hovering around $2,100 for a full heating season. Homes with older, less efficient boilers can easily top $3,000 in a cold winter.

**The oil-to-heat-pump switch:** Thousands of New England homeowners are converting from oil to cold-climate heat pumps with help from federal tax credits and state rebate programs. Maine's Efficiency Maine program, for example, offers rebates of $1,500 to $3,000 for qualifying heat pump installations. The Massachusetts Clean Energy Center has similar programs. If you heat with oil, the math on switching is better in 2026 than it has ever been.

## Electric Heat: Two Very Different Technologies

When someone says "electric heat," they might mean two completely different things, and the cost difference between them is enormous.

### Electric Resistance Heat

Electric resistance heat, the kind you get from electric baseboard heaters or a traditional electric furnace, converts electricity directly into heat with 100 percent efficiency. That sounds great until you consider that electricity is 3 to 5 times more expensive per BTU than natural gas. Electric resistance heat is the most expensive way to heat a home in cold climates, full stop.

It makes the most sense for homes that rarely need heat (mild climates or very well-insulated homes) or as supplemental heat in a specific room where extending ductwork would be cost-prohibitive.

### Heat Pumps: The Game-Changer

A heat pump is an electric device that extracts heat from outdoor air (yes, even cold outdoor air down to minus 15 degrees Fahrenheit on modern cold-climate models) and moves it indoors. Because it is moving heat rather than generating it, a heat pump delivers 2 to 4 units of heat per unit of electricity consumed.

**Heat pump annual costs by climate zone:**
- Mild climate (South, Pacific Coast): $600 - $900
- Moderate climate (Mid-Atlantic, Pacific Northwest): $800 - $1,200
- Cold climate (Northeast, Midwest): $1,000 - $1,600

**Types of heat pumps:**
- **Central ducted heat pump:** Replaces a central air handler, uses existing ductwork. Best for whole-home heating.
- **Mini-split heat pump:** Ductless system with indoor units mounted on walls. Great for additions, older homes, or zone heating.
- **Geothermal heat pump:** Uses stable ground temperature for extreme efficiency. Very high upfront cost ($10,000 to $30,000) but lowest operating cost of any system.

## The Hidden Costs: Equipment, Installation, and Maintenance

Annual fuel costs are only part of the picture. You also need to think about:

**Equipment and installation costs:**
- Gas furnace: $2,500 - $6,500 installed
- Oil boiler: $4,000 - $9,000 installed
- Central heat pump: $5,000 - $12,000 installed
- Mini-split (single zone): $1,500 - $4,000 installed
- Electric furnace: $1,500 - $4,000 installed (low upfront, high operating)

**Maintenance costs:**
- Gas furnace: $80 - $150/year for annual tune-up
- Oil system: $150 - $250/year (oil systems need more frequent servicing)
- Heat pump: $100 - $200/year
- Electric resistance: Near zero (no combustion, no annual service needed)

Oil systems typically need a new filter, nozzle, and tune-up every year to stay efficient and safe. Skipping this maintenance can reduce efficiency by 10 to 15 percent and risk carbon monoxide issues.

## When Does It Make Sense to Switch Fuels?

Switching your heating fuel is a major decision. Here are the scenarios where it makes financial sense:

**Switch from oil to heat pump if:**
- You have an oil system that is more than 15 years old and needs replacement
- You live in a region with available utility rebates (New England, Mid-Atlantic)
- Your annual oil bills exceed $2,000

**Switch from electric resistance to heat pump if:**
- Your electric bills are above $150/month in winter
- You live in a mild to moderate climate (heat pumps lose efficiency below 10 degrees)

**Stick with natural gas if:**
- Gas is available in your area and your furnace is less than 10 years old
- You live in a very cold climate (below zero winters regularly)
- You are not planning to stay in the home more than 5 years

## 2026 Federal Tax Credits and State Rebates

The federal Inflation Reduction Act provides significant incentives for switching to more efficient heating:

**Federal tax credits (through 2032):**
- Heat pump installation: 30% credit, up to $2,000 per year
- High-efficiency gas furnace (95+ AFUE): 30% credit, up to $600
- Home energy audit: 30% credit, up to $150

**How to claim:** Use IRS Form 5695 when filing your federal taxes. The IRS has updated guidance at [irs.gov](https://www.irs.gov). Keep all receipts and manufacturer certifications.

**State rebates:** Many states add rebates on top of the federal credit. The Database of State Incentives for Renewables and Efficiency (DSIRE) at dsireusa.org tracks all available programs by zip code. Some utilities also offer their own rebates, so check with your gas or electric company directly.

If you paid for a qualifying heat pump in 2026, you could reduce your net equipment cost by 30 percent plus any state rebates. On a $9,000 heat pump installation, that is $2,700 back from the federal government alone.

## 7 Ways to Cut Heating Bills Regardless of Fuel Type

Switching fuels is a big project, but several smaller steps can shave 10 to 30 percent off your heating bill this winter:

1. **Schedule a furnace or boiler tune-up.** A clean, properly adjusted system uses 10 to 15 percent less fuel.
2. **Replace your air filter.** A clogged filter forces your system to work harder. Change it every 1 to 3 months during heating season.
3. **Seal air leaks.** Gaps around windows, doors, and electrical outlets let warm air escape. The DOE estimates that air sealing saves the average home $166 per year. See guidance at [energy.gov](https://www.energy.gov).
4. **Add a programmable or smart thermostat.** Setting back your thermostat 7 to 10 degrees for 8 hours while you sleep or work cuts heating costs by about 10 percent. Our [smart thermostat installation guide](/blog/smart-thermostat-installation-cost) has details.
5. **Insulate your attic.** Heat rises, and a poorly insulated attic lets it escape. The DOE says attic insulation upgrades save 10 to 50 percent on heating costs depending on your starting point.
6. **Close off unused rooms.** If you have a spare bedroom or formal dining room you rarely use, close the vents and shut the door.
7. **Get a home energy audit.** A professional audit costs $150 to $500 but identifies your biggest efficiency opportunities. Many utilities offer them for free or at reduced cost. Our [home energy audit guide](/blog/home-energy-audit-cost) explains what to expect.

## What This Means for Your Wallet

For most homeowners, the conclusion is straightforward: if you have access to natural gas and your equipment is in good shape, you are already in a reasonable position. If you heat with oil and your system is aging, 2026 is an unusually good time to run the numbers on a heat pump switch, given the federal tax credits and state rebates available. And if you have electric resistance heat in a mild climate, a heat pump is probably the single highest-ROI upgrade you can make to your home.

Do not make this decision based on this year's fuel prices alone. Heating oil and natural gas prices fluctuate year to year. Run the numbers over a 10-year horizon using the DOE's heating fuel comparison tools at [energy.gov](https://www.energy.gov).
