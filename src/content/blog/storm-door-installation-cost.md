Installing a storm door typically costs **$175 to $600 for the door and labor combined**, with most homeowners landing between $275 and $425. The national average sits around $340. If you go the DIY route, you can cut that down to just $100 to $350 for the door itself, since professional installation usually adds $75 to $150 in labor.

If you have been putting off adding a storm door because you are worried about the expense, you are not alone. Many homeowners are surprised to learn that this is one of the more affordable home improvement projects with a real payback in energy savings, improved security, and weather protection.

## Why Storm Doors Are Worth Considering This Winter

Before diving into costs, it helps to understand what you are actually getting. A storm door adds a second layer of protection in front of your main entry door. That means less drafts in January, less rain blowing into your entryway, and an extra barrier against bugs in the summer.

The U.S. Department of Energy notes that storm doors can reduce heat loss through an entryway by 45 percent when combined with an older, poorly insulated primary door. That is real money back in your pocket each month on heating bills.

### Labor Costs Alone

Most contractors charge **$75 to $150 for storm door installation**, assuming your door frame is in good shape. Some charge a flat rate; others bill by the hour at $50 to $90. Expect costs at the higher end if you live in a metro area like New York City, San Francisco, or Seattle. Smaller markets in the Midwest or South tend to run cheaper.

If your door frame needs repair or is out of square, add another $50 to $200 for frame work before the new door can go in.

## What Affects Your Final Cost

Several factors push the price up or down.

### Door Size

Standard residential storm doors fit openings of 32 to 36 inches wide and 80 inches tall. If your opening is non-standard or you have a double door entry, you will need a custom-sized unit that costs significantly more. French storm doors for double entries typically run $500 to $1,200 installed.

### Glass Type and Style

**Full-view glass doors** let in maximum light and have a retractable screen panel. These run $150 to $350 for the door itself. **Half-glass doors** have a solid lower panel (usually aluminum or steel) and glass only in the upper half. They cost slightly less, $100 to $250, and offer a bit more privacy and insulation in the lower section.

**Low-E glass** (low emissivity) has a special coating that reflects heat back inside during winter and blocks solar heat gain in summer. Doors with low-E glass cost $50 to $100 more than standard glass, but the ENERGY STAR program recognizes them as an energy-efficient upgrade. According to the ENERGY STAR program, low-E storm doors can reduce energy costs by up to 10 percent compared to no storm door at all.

### Frame and Construction Material

**Aluminum** is the most common and affordable choice. It is lightweight, rust-resistant, and holds up well in most climates. Most doors under $300 use aluminum frames.

**Steel** offers more security and dent resistance but adds weight and costs $50 to $150 more than comparable aluminum models.

**Wood-core composite** looks more traditional and is a good match for older craftsman-style homes. These run $200 to $400 for the door alone.

### Existing Door Frame Condition

If your door frame is rotted, damaged, or badly out of square, that work has to happen before the new storm door can be installed. Frame repairs typically add $50 to $200 to the job. This is one reason it is worth asking your installer to assess the frame before agreeing to a final price.

## Popular Storm Door Brands and What They Cost

Three brands dominate the market in the US.

**Larson** is the most widely sold storm door brand in the country. You can find their doors at Home Depot and Lowe's. Expect to pay $120 to $450 for a Larson door depending on the style. Their Tradewinds series with retractable screen is popular and runs $200 to $350.

**Andersen** (through their Emco brand) makes higher-end storm doors that often include triple-track glass and screen options. Prices run $175 to $500. Andersen is also the parent company of EMCO, which is sold at Home Depot.

**Pella** offers storm doors in the $250 to $550 range, with solid construction and a range of finishes to match their primary doors. Pella products are sold through Pella showrooms and select home improvement stores.

**Generic or house-brand doors** from big box retailers run $80 to $200 and are a reasonable choice for a low-traffic secondary door. The tradeoff is fewer finish options and sometimes heavier hardware that feels less refined.

## DIY vs. Hiring a Pro

This is genuinely a project many handy homeowners can handle. Storm door installation typically takes two to four hours if you have basic tools and your door frame is in good shape.

**DIY is reasonable if:**
- You are comfortable using a drill and level
- Your door opening is a standard size
- Your existing frame is solid and square
- You have help lifting the door into place

**Hire a pro if:**
- Your opening is non-standard or the frame needs work
- You want the installation warranted
- Your primary door sits at an angle or the threshold is uneven
- This is a main entry door where you want it done right the first time

Hiring a handyman through a service like TaskRabbit or a local handyman service typically costs $75 to $125 for a standard install, while a licensed contractor may charge $100 to $150. For a standard storm door on a typical frame, there is rarely a reason to hire a full-service contractor versus a skilled handyman.

If you want to try DIY, Larson and Andersen both publish detailed installation guides. Most doors come with all the hardware you need, including hinges, closer, and handle set.

## Energy Savings: What to Actually Expect

Storm doors are not going to slash your heating bill in half. But they do provide real, measurable benefit in certain situations.

The biggest savings come from replacing an older, uninsulated primary door. If your front door is leaky or single-pane glass, adding a storm door can reduce air infiltration noticeably.

The Department of Energy recommends storm doors primarily for homes in cold climates that have older, less-energy-efficient entry doors. If you already have a modern, well-insulated primary door, the energy savings from a storm door are smaller but the door still adds weather protection and an extra security barrier.

For homes in hot southern climates, the case for storm doors is a bit different. A full-view glass storm door can actually trap solar heat against your primary door and raise cooling costs slightly. In that scenario, choose a model with a retractable screen so you can get ventilation during mild weather.

## Permits and HOA Considerations

Most storm door installations do not require a building permit. They are considered minor home improvement work in almost all US jurisdictions.

However, if you live in a neighborhood with a homeowners association, check your CC&Rs before purchasing. Some HOAs restrict door colors or styles to maintain a uniform appearance. Getting written approval before you buy saves you from having to return a door that does not meet the guidelines.

## How to Save Money on Storm Door Installation

**Shop seasonal sales.** Storm doors go on sale at the end of summer (August to September) as retailers clear inventory before the colder months. Fall is a great time to buy.

**Buy the door yourself, then hire labor.** Contractors sometimes mark up materials 15 to 30 percent. If you buy the door at Home Depot or Lowe's and just pay for installation labor, you can save $50 to $100.

**Bundle with other door work.** If you are also replacing weatherstripping or threshold seals on your primary door, ask the same handyman to do both jobs. You often get a discount for bundling.

**Check utility company rebates.** Some state utility companies offer rebates for installing ENERGY STAR-certified storm doors. Check the ENERGY STAR rebate finder or your local utility's website to see what is available in your area.

**Use manufacturer rebates.** Larson, Pella, and Andersen periodically offer mail-in rebates of $20 to $75. Check their websites before purchasing.

## Signs You Need a New Storm Door (or Finally Need to Add One)

You might not think about your storm door until something goes wrong. Here are common signs that it is time to act:

Your screen rips every season and cannot be repaired anymore. Storm door screens are replaceable, but if the frame is damaged or the door no longer closes smoothly, it may be more economical to replace the whole unit.

Your storm door rattles in the wind or lets in a cold draft even when closed. The seals have failed, and no amount of weatherstripping adjustment will fully fix it.

You have an exterior door with no storm door protection and you notice drafts, moisture, or bugs getting in around the door frame.

Your current storm door does not lock, or the lock mechanism has failed. Security is a real consideration at a main entry.
