Professional smoke detector installation costs **$50 to $150 per unit**, including labor and the device itself. Carbon monoxide detector installation runs **$60 to $200 per unit**. If you need a whole-home system with interconnected detectors professionally installed, expect to pay **$300 to $800 for a typical 3-bedroom home**. These numbers vary quite a bit based on your home's wiring, the type of detector, and your region, so keep reading for the full breakdown.

If you are moving into a new home or your detectors are more than 10 years old, now is exactly the right time to deal with this. Every fall, as people start running furnaces and closing windows, carbon monoxide incidents climb. You are not being paranoid by taking this seriously.

## Why Detector Costs Vary So Much

Here is the honest truth: most smoke detectors are not expensive devices. A basic battery-powered smoke alarm at a hardware store costs $10 to $30. So why does professional installation cost $50 to $150?

The answer is labor and expertise. A licensed electrician or a certified installer ensures:

- Detectors are placed in the right locations per [NFPA 72 (National Fire Alarm Code)](https://www.nfpa.org/codes-and-standards/nfpa-72-standard-for-the-national-fire-alarm-and-signaling-code)
- Hardwired units are connected to your home's electrical system correctly
- Interconnected systems trigger every alarm at once when one detects smoke
- Your installation meets local building code and passes inspection if required

In many states and cities, if you are selling your home, a licensed inspector will check detector placement and function. Installing them wrong can delay a sale or cause a failed inspection.

## Smoke Detector Installation Cost Breakdown

### Battery-Powered vs. Hardwired

| Type | Device Cost | Installation Labor | Total per Unit |
|---|---|---|---|
| Battery-powered (basic) | $10-$30 | $25-$50 | $35-$80 |
| Battery-powered (smart/WiFi) | $30-$80 | $30-$60 | $60-$140 |
| Hardwired (no backup) | $20-$50 | $50-$100 | $70-$150 |
| Hardwired with battery backup | $25-$60 | $60-$120 | $85-$180 |
| Interconnected hardwired system | $25-$60 each | $80-$150 each | $105-$210 per unit |

For a **3-bedroom home**, the NFPA recommends at minimum:
- One detector on every level
- One outside each sleeping area
- One inside each bedroom

That typically means 6 to 10 detectors total. A professional installation for that full coverage runs **$400 to $1,200** depending on whether you go with battery or hardwired units.

### Smart Smoke Detectors

Smart detectors like the Google Nest Protect ($119 each) or First Alert OneLink ($100 each) connect to your WiFi and send alerts to your phone. Professional installation runs $30 to $60 extra per unit because the installer also helps with app setup and network configuration.

These are genuinely worth considering if you travel frequently or have a vacation home. Getting a phone alert when something triggers is not a luxury feature for everyone.

## Carbon Monoxide Detector Installation Cost Breakdown

CO detectors are often overlooked, and that is dangerous. Carbon monoxide has no smell, no color, and kills quietly. The [Consumer Product Safety Commission (CPSC)](https://www.cpsc.gov/safety-education/safety-guides/home/carbon-monoxide-detectors) estimates CO poisoning from non-fire sources kills around 400 Americans annually and hospitalizes more than 100,000 people.

| Type | Device Cost | Installation Labor | Total per Unit |
|---|---|---|---|
| Plug-in CO detector | $20-$50 | $20-$40 | $40-$90 |
| Battery-powered CO detector | $20-$60 | $25-$45 | $45-$105 |
| Hardwired CO detector | $30-$70 | $60-$120 | $90-$190 |
| Combination smoke/CO detector | $25-$120 | $50-$130 | $75-$250 |

### Combination Units: Usually the Smart Move

Combination smoke and CO detectors are popular because they save money and reduce the total number of devices. A quality combination unit like the Kidde Combination Detector runs $25 to $80, and professional installation of a combination unit costs roughly the same as installing a single-purpose unit. You save real money compared to buying two separate devices per room.

For most homeowners, combination units make the most practical sense. Your electrician can install them in a single visit rather than two separate ones.

## State-Specific Requirements

This matters more than most homeowners realize. Requirements for both smoke and CO detectors vary dramatically by state. Here is a quick overview:

| State | Smoke Detectors Required | CO Detectors Required |
|---|---|---|
| California | Yes, in every bedroom and outside each sleeping area | Yes, in every dwelling unit |
| New York | Yes, on every floor and outside sleeping areas | Yes, in all dwelling units |
| Texas | Yes, in each bedroom, outside each bedroom, on every story | Yes, in one- and two-family dwellings |
| Florida | Yes, on each level and outside sleeping areas | Not statewide (some counties require) |
| Illinois | Yes, in every room | Yes, required statewide |
| Ohio | Yes | Yes |
| Pennsylvania | Yes | Yes, adjacent to sleeping areas |
| Georgia | Yes | Not statewide, but strongly recommended |

**Check your local requirements** through your state's fire marshal office or local building department. Many states now require CO detectors when you have any fuel-burning appliances: gas furnaces, gas water heaters, fireplaces, or attached garages.

When you sell a home, requirements get stricter. California, for example, requires sellers to install both smoke and CO detectors that meet current standards before closing.

## When You Must Hire a Pro

**You should hire a licensed electrician for:**

- Hardwired detector installation in homes where no wiring exists
- Interconnected system installation
- Homes with aluminum wiring (common in homes built in the 1960s and 70s, requires special handling)
- Any new construction or renovation requiring permits and inspection
- Replacing failed hardwired units where the wiring needs to be assessed

The [National Fire Protection Association (NFPA)](https://www.nfpa.org) recommends interconnected systems because they wake everyone in the house when any single detector triggers. In a two-story home, a fire starting in the basement at night might not wake someone upstairs before the smoke alarm on that floor sounds. Interconnected systems eliminate that gap.

**You can safely DIY:**

- Battery-powered detectors in homes already meeting code
- Plug-in CO detectors
- Replacing like-for-like battery units (same brand, same model line)
- Smart detectors with wireless interconnect features

If you go the DIY route, follow placement guidelines from the [NFPA 72 standard](https://www.nfpa.org/codes-and-standards/nfpa-72-standard-for-the-national-fire-alarm-and-signaling-code): smoke detectors should go on the ceiling or high on a wall, at least 4 inches from a corner, and at least 10 feet from cooking appliances to avoid nuisance alarms.

## How Long Do Detectors Last?

This is where a lot of homeowners get caught off guard. Smoke detectors have a **10-year lifespan**, and CO detectors typically last **5 to 7 years**. After that, sensors degrade and devices can fail silently.

Check the manufacture date on the back of your existing detectors. If you cannot find it, or if it is more than 10 years ago, replace everything. A dead battery is not the only way a smoke alarm can fail.

The [NFPA recommends testing detectors monthly](https://www.nfpa.org/educational-resources/home-fire-safety/smoke-alarms) by pressing the test button, and replacing batteries annually or using 10-year sealed-battery models.

## Placement Guide: Where Detectors Must Go

Getting placement right matters as much as buying the right device. Here is what the NFPA and most state fire codes require:

**Smoke detectors:**
- Inside every bedroom
- Outside every sleeping area (hallway near bedrooms)
- On every level of the home, including the basement
- At the top of stairs
- Keep them away from bathrooms, kitchens, and garages where false alarms are common

**Carbon monoxide detectors:**
- On every floor of the home
- Near sleeping areas (you need to hear the alarm at night)
- Near attached garages
- Near any fuel-burning appliance: furnace, boiler, water heater, fireplace

**Where not to put them:**
- In kitchens directly (within 10 feet of cooking appliances for smoke alarms)
- In bathrooms (steam triggers false alarms)
- In extremely cold or hot spaces like unheated attics or garages
- Near windows or HVAC vents (drafts can dilute smoke before it reaches the sensor)

## Is This a Good Time of Year to Install?

October is actually ideal timing. Here is why: you are heading into heating season, which is when most CO incidents happen. Gas furnaces, fireplaces, and wood stoves all burn fuel, and if there is a cracked heat exchanger or blocked flue, CO accumulates. Having working detectors before the first cold snap is not just sensible, it is urgent.

The [NFPA specifically notes](https://www.nfpa.org/educational-resources/home-fire-safety/co-home-fires) that CO incidents peak in December, January, and February because of increased heating appliance use and people keeping windows tightly shut. Starting fall with fresh detectors or a full system check puts you ahead of that risk.

## Tips to Save Money

**Buy the detectors yourself.** Electricians sometimes mark up devices. If you buy your own units at a big-box store or online, you bring your own cost down by $20 to $50 per unit. Just confirm with your electrician what model they prefer to install before purchasing.

**Bundle detector installation with other electrical work.** If you have been putting off adding an outdoor outlet or installing a ceiling fan, doing all of it on one visit saves you a service call fee ($50 to $100).

**Check for utility rebates.** Some state utility programs and local fire departments offer free or subsidized smoke and CO detectors for income-qualifying households. Contact your local fire department to ask. Some programs even include free installation.

**Look into the Weatherization Assistance Program.** The [Department of Energy's Weatherization Assistance Program](https://www.energy.gov/eere/wap/weatherization-assistance-program) sometimes includes safety improvements like smoke detectors in qualifying homes alongside insulation and HVAC upgrades.

## What to Ask When Hiring an Installer

When you call electricians for quotes, ask these specific questions:

- Are you licensed and insured in this state?
- Do you pull permits when required by local code?
- What brands do you recommend and why?
- Will the detectors be interconnected?
- What is your warranty on labor?
- Do the new units meet current NFPA 72 standards?

A good electrician answers all of these without hesitation. Be cautious of anyone who says permits are not necessary for hardwired detector installation in your area without specifically verifying that against your local code.

## Quick Decision Guide

- **Battery detectors already in place, less than 10 years old:** Test monthly, replace batteries, you are fine.
- **Battery detectors more than 10 years old:** Replace them yourself with new combination smoke/CO units. This is a legitimate DIY job.
- **No hardwired system in an older home:** Get quotes from licensed electricians for interconnected hardwired units.
- **Hardwired system stopped working:** Do not ignore it. Call an electrician; wiring issues can be a fire hazard themselves.
- **Selling your home:** Check your state's real estate disclosure requirements for detectors before listing.
