Hardwood floor refinishing costs **$3 to $8 per square foot**, or **$1,200 to $4,800 for a typical 1,500 sq ft home**. Most homeowners pay around $2,500 for professional sanding, staining, and three coats of polyurethane on a standard ranch-style floor plan. If your floors are scratched, dull, or discolored but structurally sound, refinishing is almost always the right call over full replacement, which can run $6 to $22 per square foot installed.

## Is Refinishing Really Worth It?

Here is the honest answer: yes, almost always. A set of hardwood floors can be refinished **four to seven times** over their lifetime before the wood gets too thin. If your floors were installed in the 1970s or earlier and have never been refinished, you likely have at least two or three refinishes left in them. Refinishing costs roughly 30 to 50 percent of what new hardwood flooring and installation would run.

The catch is that not every floor qualifies. Engineered hardwood floors typically have a 1/16 to 1/8 inch veneer, which means they can only be lightly screened one to two times before the veneer wears through. Solid hardwood, which is 3/4 inch thick, can take far more rounds of sanding.

## What You Are Paying For: The Refinishing Process

Professional floor refinishing is not a one-afternoon job. Understanding what goes into it helps you evaluate quotes accurately and avoid being overcharged.

**Step 1: Floor prep and furniture removal.** Most contractors will move furniture for an added fee ($50 to $150), or you can do it yourself to save money.

**Step 2: Drum sanding.** The contractor uses a large drum sander to remove the old finish and a thin layer of wood. This is where the skill matters most: an inexperienced operator can sand through the wood or leave swirl marks that show under the final finish. Edge sanding with a smaller machine follows for corners and tight spots.

**Step 3: Staining (optional).** If you want to change the color of your floors, stain is applied after sanding. This adds $1 to $3 per square foot to the cost. Stain adds a day to the timeline because it needs to dry completely before finishing coats go on.

**Step 4: Finishing coats.** A quality job uses three coats of polyurethane (either oil-based or water-based), with light sanding between coats. This is what protects the wood and gives it the look you want.

**Step 5: Dry time.** You will be out of the room for at least 24 hours after the last coat. Full cure time is five to seven days, meaning you should avoid placing area rugs and dragging furniture for about a week.

### Oil-Based vs. Water-Based Polyurethane

This choice affects both cost and appearance, and contractors have strong opinions on both sides.

**Oil-based polyurethane** costs $30 to $50 per gallon and gives floors a warm amber tone. It is more durable and scratch-resistant but takes 24 hours between coats (versus 3 to 4 hours for water-based) and has strong fumes that require the house to be ventilated for 2 to 3 days. Total dry time before moving back in: 48 to 72 hours.

**Water-based polyurethane** costs $40 to $70 per gallon and dries clear, preserving the natural wood color. It dries faster and has lower VOC emissions, which matters if you have children, pets, or anyone with respiratory issues at home. It is slightly less durable than oil-based in heavy-traffic areas but performs well for most households.

If you have pets or young children and want to minimize exposure to fumes, water-based is the better choice. If you love the warm, rich look of traditional hardwood and can vacate for a few days, oil-based is hard to beat for durability.

## What Makes Refinishing Cost More

Several factors push the price above the typical range, and knowing them in advance keeps you from being surprised mid-project.

**Floor condition.** Deeply gouged, cracked, or buckled boards need repairs before sanding. Contractors typically charge $50 to $150 per hour for repairs, and a floor with significant damage can add $200 to $800 to your total.

**Stair refinishing.** Each stair tread and riser is priced separately because it is tedious hand work. Expect to pay $15 to $30 per tread step, plus $10 to $20 per riser. A 14-step staircase can add $350 to $700 to the project.

**Multiple levels or room configurations.** Open floor plans and layouts with many separate rooms increase edge sanding time. Stairs, landings, and closets all add complexity.

**Removing existing finish type.** If your floors have an aluminum oxide finish (common on pre-finished engineered hardwood), it is exceptionally hard to sand and requires more labor time and different equipment. Some contractors charge a 20 to 30 percent premium for these floors.

**Floor layout.** A diagonal or herringbone pattern takes more time and expertise to sand without damaging the wood grain.

## Signs Your Floors Need Refinishing (Not Replacement)

You do not always need a full replacement when your floors look tired. Refinishing is the right call when:

- The finish looks dull, worn through in high-traffic areas, or scratched but the wood itself is intact
- You see minor surface staining (pet accidents, water rings) but no warping or raised grain
- Color has faded unevenly from sun exposure
- You want to change the color or sheen level of your floors

Replacement makes more sense when:

- Boards are cracked, warped, cupped, or buckled
- Water damage has caused structural issues in the subfloor
- The wood has been sanded down to less than 1/4 inch thick (you can check this at a floor vent or HVAC register)
- You have engineered hardwood with a very thin veneer that has already been screened once or twice

If you are not sure which situation you are in, a reputable flooring contractor will assess the floor thickness with a depth gauge and give you an honest answer.

## DIY vs. Hiring a Pro: What You Need to Know

Drum sander rental runs $50 to $75 per day. Add $20 to $30 for an edge sander, and another $20 to $30 for finishing supplies. Materials for a 1,000 sq ft job (polyurethane, sandpaper, stain) typically run $200 to $400. Total DIY cost: $400 to $700 for materials and rentals on a medium-sized home.

The savings are real. So is the risk.

Drum sanders are powerful machines that can gouge through flooring in seconds if tilted or stopped while running. The most common DIY mistake is a visible wave pattern left by uneven passes with the drum sander. These show clearly under finish, especially in raking light from windows. Fixing them means renting the sander again and starting over.

For most homeowners, the honest recommendation is: hire a pro for the sanding and staining, and apply the final coat of finish yourself if you want to save something. Some contractors will do a "screen and recoat" where they lightly scuff the existing finish and apply a fresh top coat without sanding to bare wood. This runs $0.75 to $1.50 per square foot and is a good middle-ground option if your floors have light wear but do not need full refinishing.

## How to Hire a Floor Refinishing Contractor

This part matters more than most homeowners realize. Floor refinishing is a skill that separates average results from floors that look brand new, and the difference is often not visible until after the contractor has left.

**Get at least three quotes.** Prices can vary by 30 to 40 percent between contractors in the same city for the same job.

**Ask specifically about dustless sanding.** Modern drum sanders connect to containment systems that capture 99 percent of dust. If a contractor does not mention this, your home will be covered in fine wood dust for days. Dustless systems are standard with reputable contractors.

**Ask to see past work.** Any experienced floor refinisher should be able to show photos or walk you to completed jobs.

**Verify licensing and insurance.** Most states require contractors who do home improvement work to be licensed. The National Wood Flooring Association (NWFA) also offers certification for flooring professionals. Check your state contractor licensing board for verification. In states like California, Florida, and Texas, licensing is required for this type of work.

**Get the finish brand in writing.** Ask which brand and product of polyurethane they use. Reputable contractors use Bona, Loba, or Minwax professional-grade products, not big-box store consumer finishes.

**Beware of unusually low bids.** A quote that is 40 percent lower than others often means fewer coats of finish, cheaper materials, or shortcuts on edge sanding. Three coats of finish is standard. Two coats is not enough for durability.

## How Long Does Refinished Hardwood Last?

With normal household traffic, a professionally refinished hardwood floor with three coats of polyurethane should look good for **8 to 12 years** before needing attention. A few practices extend this significantly:

- Place felt pads under all furniture legs
- Use area rugs in high-traffic zones (entryways, hallways, in front of the kitchen sink)
- Keep pet nails trimmed, since nails scratch polyurethane faster than almost anything else
- Sweep or vacuum weekly to remove grit that acts like sandpaper underfoot
- Clean with a pH-neutral hardwood floor cleaner (Bona is widely recommended by contractors)

Avoid steam mops on hardwood floors entirely. They force moisture into the wood and can cause warping, swelling, and finish adhesion failure. The same goes for wet mopping.

## Can You Partially Refinish Just One Room?

Technically, yes. Practically, it rarely looks great. Because wood changes color slightly when the finish is stripped and restained, matching a newly refinished room to an adjacent room with original or older finish is very difficult. Most contractors will tell you that refinishing one room and leaving the adjacent hallway or living room with the old finish will result in a visible color mismatch.

The exception is if the floors are separated by a threshold or doorway and not visually connected. If your kitchen opens to your living room with no dividing line, refinish both at the same time.

## What to Expect on Project Day

On the day of work, plan to be out of the house for most of the day. Even with dustless sanding, the noise from the drum sander is significant, and fumes from oil-based polyurethane require ventilation. Here is a realistic timeline:

- **Day 1:** Furniture moved, sanding begins (4 to 6 hours for a 1,000 to 1,500 sq ft area), first coat of polyurethane applied by end of day
- **Day 2:** Second coat applied (after overnight drying for oil-based, or 4 to 6 hours for water-based)
- **Day 3:** Final coat applied; you can walk on floors in socks after 24 hours
- **Days 4 to 7:** Avoid area rugs, furniture replacement, and dragging heavy items until full cure

Most contractors can complete a standard home in two to three days. Larger homes or homes requiring significant repairs take longer.
