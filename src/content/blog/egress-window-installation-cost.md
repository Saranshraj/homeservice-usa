Egress window installation costs **$2,500 to $5,500 for most US homeowners**, with the national average landing around $3,500. That price covers the window unit itself, excavation of a window well, cutting through the foundation wall, and all finish work. Projects at the high end, say $6,000 to $8,000+, usually involve deep or oversized wells, tricky soil conditions, waterproofing upgrades, or premium window brands. If you are finishing a basement and need a legally required escape route, or converting a basement room into a bedroom, understanding exactly where your money goes will help you avoid surprises.

---

## Why Egress Windows Matter (and Why They Are Required)

An egress window is simply a window large enough for a person to escape through in a fire and for a firefighter to enter through. The International Residential Code (IRC), adopted by most states, requires at least one egress window in every basement sleeping room.

The IRC minimum specs are:
- **Minimum opening width:** 20 inches
- **Minimum opening height:** 24 inches
- **Minimum net clear opening:** 5.7 square feet (5.0 sq ft at ground level)
- **Maximum sill height from floor:** 44 inches

Skipping this requirement is not just a safety risk; it means the room cannot legally be listed as a bedroom on an appraisal, which affects your home's value. Local building departments almost always require a permit for egress window work. Your contractor should pull that permit for you.

## Factors That Move the Price Up or Down

### 1. Foundation Type

**Poured concrete** foundations cost more to cut than **block** foundations. A concrete saw cuts through poured walls, which takes more time and specialized equipment, adding $200 to $500 compared to a block wall project.

**Brick** foundations are the most labor-intensive and can add $500 to $1,000 to the base cost.

### 2. Window Well Depth and Size

If your basement floor sits more than 4 feet below grade, you need a deep well. Deep wells require more excavation, more gravel for drainage, and sometimes a taller well cover. Expect to add $400 to $900 for every extra foot of depth beyond the standard 36-inch well.

### 3. Soil Conditions

Rocky soil or expansive clay (common in Texas, Colorado, and parts of the Midwest) slows excavation and may require specialized equipment. This can push labor costs up by $300 to $700.

### 4. Window Style and Brand

**Casement windows** are the most common choice for egress because they open fully with a single crank. **Single-hung sliders** are cheaper but require a larger rough opening to meet the minimum clear-opening requirement.

Budget brands: $300 to $500
Mid-range (Andersen, Pella, Marvin): $500 to $1,200
Premium fiberglass (Integrity, Alside): $1,000 to $2,000+

### 5. Number of Windows

Installing two windows at once is cheaper per unit than two separate projects. Most contractors offer a 10 to 20 percent discount on the second window when it is done in the same mobilization.

### 6. Waterproofing Upgrades

Standard installs include a gravel-lined well with a drain that ties into your existing drainage system. If you want a window well cover (highly recommended to keep out water, leaves, and animals) add $50 to $200 per well. If you want a full drain-tile connection to a sump system, add $500 to $1,500.

## DIY vs. Hiring a Pro

This is one project where hiring a licensed contractor is almost always the right call. Here is why.

**Cutting a foundation wall is structural work.** A mistake creates water intrusion, cracks, or even structural compromise. Many homeowners who attempt this as a DIY project end up paying more to fix errors than a professional would have charged upfront.

**Permits and inspections are non-optional.** A building inspector will check the opening size, the lintel, the waterproofing, and the well drain. If the work is done by an unlicensed person and fails inspection, you may be required to remove it and start over.

**DIY cost savings, in reality:** You might save $800 to $1,500 on labor if you handle only the finish work (drywall patching, painting around the window, installing the well cover). The core structural and waterproofing work should be left to a licensed contractor.

If you want to handle part of the project yourself, talk to your contractor about which portions you can take on. Many are open to splitting the work, especially on the finish end.

## Finding and Vetting a Contractor

Not every general contractor handles foundation work. For this project, look for one of these:

- **Foundation specialists** (usually advertised as basement waterproofing or foundation repair companies)
- **Licensed general contractors** with documented experience cutting foundation walls
- **Window companies** that offer full egress installation, not just the window unit

Questions to ask before hiring:
1. Do you pull the building permit, or do I?
2. How do you waterproof the perimeter of the new opening?
3. What lintel do you use, and how do you determine the size?
4. Do you install a window well drain, and where does it drain to?
5. Can I see photos of three recent egress installs you've completed?

**Red flags:** Any contractor who suggests skipping the permit, quotes a price without seeing the site, or cannot explain what a lintel does. Permits protect you: they create a record that the work was inspected and approved.

You can check contractor license status at your state's licensing board website. The National Association of Home Builders (NAHB) also maintains a directory at [nahb.org](https://www.nahb.org).

## The Permit Process: What to Expect

Here is the typical sequence for a permitted egress window project:

1. **Contractor submits permit application** with drawings showing the window location, dimensions, and well depth.
2. **Building department reviews** (typically 3 to 10 business days in most jurisdictions; faster in rural areas, slower in cities).
3. **Work begins** after permit is approved. Most jobs take 1 to 2 days of on-site work.
4. **Rough inspection** is called when the opening is framed but before drywall is closed. Inspector checks the lintel, opening dimensions, and well drain.
5. **Final inspection** after all finish work is complete. Inspector checks the window operation, the well cover, and any interior finish.

Keep your permit documentation. When you sell the home, buyers and appraisers will ask for it. A basement bedroom without a permit record raises questions that can delay or sink a sale.

## Does an Egress Window Increase Home Value?

Yes, in most markets, adding an egress window to convert an unfinished or non-conforming basement space into a legal bedroom adds measurable value. A finished basement adds an average of 70 cents on the dollar in resale value according to data compiled by the National Association of Realtors. But a basement bedroom is worth significantly more than a finished rec room on an appraisal because it adds to the official bedroom count.

In markets where $3,500 buys you a legal third or fourth bedroom, the return on that investment is typically very strong.

## Maintenance and Upkeep

Egress windows require modest but important ongoing maintenance:

- **Clear the window well** of leaves and debris at least twice a year. Clogged drains lead to pooling water, which presses against your foundation.
- **Inspect the well cover** (if installed) annually for cracks or warping. Replace a damaged cover promptly.
- **Check the window seal** every 3 to 5 years. Casement window cranks can wear out; a window that does not open fully is a code violation and a safety hazard.
- **Test the crank mechanism** every spring. It should open smoothly to the full open position.
- **Inspect the perimeter caulk** around the frame each fall before winter. Reapply exterior-grade silicone caulk if you see gaps.

A well-installed egress window, properly maintained, lasts 20 to 30 years before it needs replacement.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Can I add an egress window to a room that is not a bedroom?**
Yes. While egress windows are required in basement sleeping rooms, you can add one to any basement space for safety, light, or ventilation. There is no code restriction against more egress than required.

**Does my homeowners insurance cover egress window installation?**
Generally no. Standard homeowners insurance covers sudden damage (a storm breaks a window), not planned improvements. However, adding an egress window that creates a legal bedroom may change your policy's replacement cost calculation. Contact your insurer after the work is done.

**Can I install an egress window in a slab foundation?**
Most slab-on-grade homes do not have basements where egress windows are needed. If you have a split-level home with a below-grade room on a slab, the situation is unusual and you would need a structural engineer to assess feasibility.

**What if my HOA restricts exterior modifications?**
Review your HOA's CC&Rs before starting. Most HOAs have an exception process for safety-required modifications like egress windows. Submit the request in writing with your permit documentation.

**How long does installation take?**
Most egress window installations take 1 to 2 days of on-site work for a single window. Waiting for permits can add 1 to 2 weeks to the total timeline.
