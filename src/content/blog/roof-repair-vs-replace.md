**The short answer:** Repair your roof if it is under 15 years old, the damage is limited to a small area (a few missing shingles, one leak, isolated flashing failure), and the repair cost is under 30 percent of a full replacement. Replace it if the roof is 20 or more years old, damage is spread across multiple sections, you have had two or more leaks in the past two years, or a contractor's repair quote is already climbing above $2,000 to $3,000. When age and damage extent both point the same direction, the decision is easy. The hard cases sit in the middle, and that is what this guide is for.

This is one of the most stressful calls a homeowner has to make, because the two options are so far apart in cost. A repair might run $300 to $1,500. A full replacement runs $8,500 to $24,500 for most US homes, according to our [roof replacement cost guide](/blog/roof-replacement-cost/). Getting this decision wrong in either direction costs you real money, either by replacing a roof that had years of life left, or by pouring repair money into a roof that was going to fail anyway.

## The Core Decision Framework

Roofing professionals generally use three factors together, not any single one alone, to make this call.

### 1. Age Relative to Expected Lifespan

| Roofing Material | Expected Lifespan | Repair Makes Sense Until | Replace Territory |
|---|---|---|---|
| 3-tab asphalt shingles | 15-20 years | Year 12-14 | Year 15+ |
| Architectural asphalt shingles | 25-30 years | Year 18-20 | Year 22+ |
| Wood shakes | 25-30 years | Year 18-20 | Year 22+ |
| Metal roofing | 40-70 years | Year 30-35 | Year 40+ |
| Clay or concrete tile | 50+ years | Year 35-40 | Year 45+ |
| Slate | 75-100+ years | Year 60+ | Year 80+ |

If you do not know your roof's exact age, check your home inspection report from when you bought the house, ask the previous owner, or look for a permit record with your local building department. Many counties post permit history online.

### 2. Extent and Location of Damage

**Repair-friendly damage:**
- A handful of missing or cracked shingles in one area
- A single active leak with a clearly identifiable source
- Flashing failure around one chimney, vent, or skylight
- Minor granule loss on a small section
- Storm damage limited to one slope of the roof

**Replace-signaling damage:**
- Missing shingles or granule loss spread across multiple sections
- Multiple active leaks in different rooms
- Visible sagging anywhere on the roof deck (this can indicate structural rot)
- Widespread curling, cupping, or cracking across the whole roof surface
- Daylight visible through the roof boards from the attic
- Mold or rot on the roof deck itself, not just the shingles

### 3. The 30 Percent Rule

If a single repair quote comes in above 30 percent of what a full replacement would cost, replacement usually wins on value. A $3,500 repair on a roof that would cost $12,000 to replace is right at that threshold. Beyond it, you are spending serious money on a roof that will likely need full replacement within a few years anyway, meaning you pay twice.

## Signs You Need a Full Replacement, Even If It Feels Premature

Some situations override the age-and-damage math because they signal the roof's underlying structure is compromised, not just its surface:

**Sagging roof deck.** This usually means water has been getting into the wood sheathing for a long time. Once the deck itself is soft or sagging, patching shingles will not fix the underlying problem. This needs a structural assessment and likely a full tear-off.

**Multiple leaks in different areas.** One leak might be a flashing issue. Three or four leaks in different rooms usually means the underlayment has failed broadly, and the shingles above it are no longer doing their job even where they look intact.

**You keep calling the roofer back.** If you have paid for two or three repairs in the past 18 months and new problems keep appearing, the roof is failing faster than repairs can keep up. This is the clearest signal to stop repairing and start budgeting for replacement.

**Granule loss is heavy in your gutters.** Asphalt shingles shed some granules as they age, but if your gutters are filling with what looks like coarse sand after every storm, the shingles are losing their protective coating faster than normal.

**Energy bills have crept up.** An aging, failing roof loses efficiency. If your attic insulation is fine but your heating and cooling costs have climbed over the past two to three years, a compromised roof could be part of the reason. ## Signs a Repair Is Genuinely Enough

**The roof is under 10 years old.** Even significant-looking storm damage on a newer roof is almost always a repair situation, not a replacement one. The materials and underlayment underneath are still in good condition.

**Damage is confined to one slope or section.** A tree branch that damaged one corner, or hail that hit only the south-facing slope, can usually be repaired by matching shingles to that section without touching the rest of the roof.

**It is your first leak.** A single, first-time leak with an identifiable cause (a popped nail, a cracked pipe boot, a small flashing gap) is a textbook repair. Do not let anyone talk you into full replacement over one contained leak.

**You are planning to sell within a year or two.** If the roof still has functional life left and you are not staying long-term, a targeted repair plus a roof certification (many roofers offer a 1 to 2 year warranty certification for around $150 to $300) can satisfy buyers and lenders without the full replacement cost.

## What a Repair Actually Costs

| Repair Type | Typical Cost |
|---|---|
| Replacing a few shingles | $150-$400 |
| Flashing repair (chimney, vent, skylight) | $300-$700 |
| Fixing a single active leak | $300-$1,200 |
| Storm damage patch (one section) | $500-$1,500 |
| Sealing and re-caulking | $150-$350 |
| Ridge cap replacement | $300-$600 |

Most straightforward repairs fall between $300 and $1,500. If your quote is significantly above this range for what sounds like a contained problem, get a second opinion before you commit.

## Getting an Honest Assessment

The tricky part of this decision is that the person telling you whether to repair or replace is often the same person who profits more from replacement. Here is how to protect yourself:

**Get two to three independent inspections.** Not quotes, inspections. Ask each roofer to explain their reasoning for repair versus replace, not just give you a number.

**Ask to see the damage yourself.** A trustworthy roofer will walk you through what they found, ideally with photos from the roof or attic, and explain why it does or does not warrant full replacement.

**Be skeptical of scare tactics.** If someone tells you the whole roof needs replacement based on damage you can see is limited to one small area, ask direct questions. "Why can't this be repaired instead?" A professional with a legitimate answer will not get defensive.

**Check if insurance is involved.** If you are filing a homeowners insurance claim for storm or hail damage, the insurance adjuster's assessment matters as much as the contractor's. Our [homeowners insurance claims guide](/blog/homeowners-insurance-claims/) walks through how that process works and how to avoid a lowball settlement.

## A Simple Decision Checklist

Answer these questions honestly:

1. Is the roof under 15 years old (asphalt) or under 30 years old (metal/tile)? → Lean repair
2. Is the damage limited to one area or slope? → Lean repair
3. Is this your first leak or repair call in the past 2 years? → Lean repair
4. Is there any visible sagging in the roof deck? → Lean replace
5. Have you had 2+ leaks or repairs in the past 18 months? → Lean replace
6. Is the repair quote above 30 percent of a full replacement cost? → Lean replace

If you answered "lean repair" to most of these, a targeted repair is likely the right call. If "lean replace" dominates, start getting replacement quotes now rather than continuing to patch a roof that is telling you it is done.
