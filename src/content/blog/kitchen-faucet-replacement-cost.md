If your kitchen faucet is dripping, leaking at the base, or just plain worn out, you are probably wondering what the repair bill will look like. Here is the direct answer: **kitchen faucet replacement costs between $200 and $700 for most homeowners**, with the average landing around $380 when you hire a licensed plumber and use a mid-range faucet. Luxury faucets with smart features, or situations requiring extra pipe work, can push the total past $1,500. If you are comfortable with basic plumbing, a DIY replacement cuts costs to just the faucet price: $50 to $500 for most models.

This guide breaks down every cost factor, gives you real numbers to budget with, and walks you through what to expect before you call a plumber.

## What You Are Actually Paying For

When you hire a plumber to replace a kitchen faucet, the total bill has two parts: **the faucet itself** and **the labor**. These can vary dramatically, so it helps to understand them separately.

### Faucet Cost by Type

| Faucet Type | Price Range | Best For |
|---|---|---|
| Basic single-handle | $50 to $150 | Budget kitchens, rentals |
| Two-handle kitchen faucet | $80 to $250 | Traditional style, separate hot/cold |
| Pull-down sprayer | $120 to $400 | Most popular choice today |
| Pull-out sprayer | $100 to $350 | Smaller sinks, limited overhead space |
| Commercial-style (pot filler) | $200 to $800 | Open kitchens, heavy cooking households |
| Touchless or smart faucet | $150 to $700 | Convenience, accessibility, modern homes |
| Bridge faucet (farmhouse style) | $150 to $600 | Farmhouse sinks, period-correct kitchens |

Popular brands like Moen, Delta, and Kohler dominate the $120 to $350 sweet spot for pull-down faucets. At this price range, you get solid brass construction, ceramic disc valves (which rarely drip), and lifetime warranties. Brands like Pfister and American Standard offer comparable quality at slightly lower prices.

At the high end, brands like Brizo, Grohe, and Rohl start around $400 and can exceed $1,500, but these are largely aesthetic choices, not durability improvements.

### Labor Cost

Plumbers charge **$45 to $150 per hour** depending on your region. A standard kitchen faucet replacement takes 1 to 2 hours, making the typical labor bill **$90 to $300**. Most plumbers also charge a service or trip fee of $50 to $100 on top of the hourly rate.

Here is a realistic labor breakdown:

- **Remove old faucet:** 20 to 40 minutes (longer for corroded supply lines)
- **Install new faucet:** 30 to 60 minutes
- **Connect supply lines and test for leaks:** 15 to 30 minutes
- **Clean up:** 10 to 15 minutes

If your existing supply lines are corroded or the shutoff valves under the sink do not work properly, the plumber may need to replace those as well. New braided stainless supply lines add $10 to $30 in parts. Replacing a failing shutoff valve adds another $50 to $150.

## Signs Your Kitchen Faucet Needs Replacing

Sometimes a repair is all you need, not a full replacement. But there are clear signs it is time to swap the whole unit.

**Replace the faucet when you notice:**

- **Persistent dripping even after replacing washers or cartridges.** If you have fixed the same leak twice already, the valve body is worn out.
- **Low water pressure from the faucet alone.** A clogged aerator is an easy DIY fix. But if cleaning the aerator does not help, mineral buildup inside the faucet body may have narrowed the internal passages beyond recovery.
- **Visible corrosion at the base or around the handles.** Rust and green mineral deposits are not just ugly. They indicate the faucet is deteriorating from the inside.
- **Wobbling or loose handles.** When handles rock or spin freely, the internal cartridge is failing.
- **Water collecting under the sink without an obvious source.** A leak at the faucet base usually means a worn O-ring. If replacing the O-ring does not solve it, replacement is more cost-effective.
- **Squealing or grinding when you turn the handle.** This means the ceramic disc or rubber seat is worn.
- **The faucet is more than 15 to 20 years old.** Most quality faucets last this long with basic maintenance, but older models may use parts that are no longer available.

A plumber can diagnose whether a repair makes more sense than replacement. As a general rule, if a repair costs more than half the price of a new faucet, replace it.

## DIY vs. Hiring a Plumber

Kitchen faucet replacement is one of the more beginner-friendly plumbing jobs. You do not need to solder pipes or understand complex systems. The core skills are: shutting off the water supply, disconnecting old connections, and tightening new ones without overtightening.

### When DIY Makes Sense

- You are comfortable working under the sink
- Your shutoff valves work correctly
- The old faucet is not corroded in place
- You have a standard single or three-hole mounting situation
- You have basic tools: adjustable wrench, basin wrench, plumber's tape

A basin wrench is the one specialty tool you truly need for this job. It costs $15 to $30 at any hardware store and is designed specifically to reach the mounting nuts that attach the faucet from below. Without it, the job is nearly impossible in tight spaces.

Most major faucet brands publish detailed installation videos on YouTube. Delta, Moen, and Kohler walkthroughs are especially clear and assume no prior plumbing knowledge.

### When to Hire a Plumber

- You see corrosion on supply line connections
- The shutoff valve under the sink is stuck or dripping
- You have a non-standard mounting situation (unusual hole configuration, apron-front sink)
- Your home has old galvanized pipes instead of copper or PEX
- You are replacing a standard faucet with a commercial-style model that requires different connections
- You simply prefer the peace of mind of a professional guarantee

If your shutoff valve fails when you try to turn it off, that becomes an emergency. A plumber can address this safely. Attempting to force a stuck shutoff valve can result in a flooded kitchen, which creates a much larger and more expensive problem.

## What to Expect During a Professional Installation

Knowing what the plumber will do helps you prepare and avoid surprises on the invoice.

1. **Shutoff and drain:** The plumber turns off the hot and cold supply valves under the sink and opens the faucet to drain residual water.
2. **Disconnect supply lines:** The braided lines connecting the valves to the faucet are removed. These are often replaced with new lines as a standard practice.
3. **Remove the mounting hardware:** A basin wrench loosens the mounting nuts from below. The old faucet lifts out.
4. **Clean the sink surface:** Mineral deposits and old plumber's putty are cleaned off the mounting holes.
5. **Install the new faucet:** The new unit is positioned, mounting hardware is tightened, and supply lines are connected.
6. **Test for leaks:** The plumber restores water pressure slowly and checks every connection point while the faucet is running.
7. **Check drain function:** A responsible plumber also checks the P-trap and drain while they are under the sink.

A good plumber will show you the finished installation and point out anything else they noticed under the sink, such as signs of past leaks or soft cabinet flooring that might indicate water damage.

## How to Save Money Without Cutting Corners

**Buy the faucet yourself before scheduling the plumber.** Most plumbers add a markup of 20 to 50 percent to parts they supply. If you purchase the faucet directly from a hardware store or online retailer and confirm it is compatible with your sink's mounting holes, you can save $50 to $150 on the faucet cost alone. Just make sure you buy a complete kit that includes all mounting hardware and supply lines.

**Check for manufacturer rebates.** Kohler and Delta periodically offer rebates on WaterSense-certified faucets. The EPA's WaterSense program labels faucets that use at least 30 percent less water than the federal standard of 2.2 gallons per minute. These faucets are eligible for rebates in some states and utility districts.

**Schedule during off-peak times.** Emergency weekend plumbing calls can carry a 50 percent premium. Scheduling a routine replacement for a Tuesday morning versus a Saturday afternoon can save you $50 to $100.

**Bundle with other small plumbing tasks.** If you also need a bathroom faucet replaced or a running toilet fixed, getting those done in the same visit avoids repeat service fees and trip charges.

## How to Choose a Qualified Plumber

Plumbing is licensed at the state level. Requirements vary, but most states require plumbers to complete a multi-year apprenticeship and pass an exam. You can verify a plumber's license through your state's licensing board.

Key states and their licensing lookup sites:

- **California:** California Contractors State License Board (cslb.ca.gov)
- **Texas:** Texas State Board of Plumbing Examiners (tsbpe.texas.gov)
- **Florida:** Florida Department of Business and Professional Regulation (myfloridalicense.com)
- **New York:** New York Department of State (dos.ny.gov)
- **Illinois:** Illinois Department of Financial and Professional Regulation (idfpr.illinois.gov)

Beyond licensing, look for:

- **Proof of insurance.** General liability and workers' compensation protect you if something goes wrong.
- **Written estimate before work begins.** A legitimate plumber will provide a written quote, not just a verbal estimate.
- **Warranty on labor.** Most reputable plumbers guarantee their labor for 90 days to one year. The faucet manufacturer's warranty covers parts separately.
- **Positive reviews on Google or Yelp with responses to negative reviews.** How a business handles complaints tells you a lot.

Avoid anyone who insists on cash only, refuses to provide a license number, or quotes an unusually low price and then adds fees after the job starts. These are common contractor red flags.

## WaterSense and Water Savings

If you are replacing an older faucet, upgrading to a WaterSense-certified model is worth the modest premium. The EPA estimates that WaterSense faucets and aerators save the average household about 700 gallons of water per year. Over the life of the faucet, that adds up to real savings on your water bill, especially in drought-prone states like California, Arizona, and Texas where water rates continue to rise.

The WaterSense label means the faucet has been independently certified to use no more than 1.5 gallons per minute while meeting strict performance standards. You should not notice any difference in function, and many homeowners report their WaterSense faucets actually feel better than the old high-flow models.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Can I replace just the cartridge instead of the whole faucet?**
Often yes. A worn cartridge is a very common cause of dripping and usually costs $15 to $40 in parts. If the faucet is otherwise in good shape, a cartridge replacement makes sense. However, if the faucet is old and the body shows corrosion, full replacement is smarter.

**How long does a kitchen faucet last?**
A quality kitchen faucet with ceramic disc valves lasts 15 to 20 years with minimal maintenance. Cheaper faucets with rubber washers may need cartridge replacements every 5 to 7 years.

**Do I need a permit to replace a kitchen faucet?**
No. A straight faucet swap is a minor repair and does not require a permit in any US state. Permits are generally only required when you are moving pipes or adding new plumbing runs.

**What faucet finish is most durable?**
Brushed nickel and oil-rubbed bronze hide fingerprints and water spots better than chrome and are generally more durable. Matte black finishes have become popular but can show wear over time in high-use kitchens.

**Can a dripping faucet increase my water bill significantly?**
Yes. A faucet that drips once per second wastes about 3,000 gallons per year, according to the EPA. At average US water rates, that costs an extra $20 to $35 per year, though it varies by region.
