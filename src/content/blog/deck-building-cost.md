Building a deck costs between **$4,000 and $22,000** for most US homeowners in 2026, with the national average landing around **$9,000 to $12,000** for a 300 to 400 square foot deck using pressure-treated wood. A small ground-level deck can come in under $3,000 if you do the framing yourself. A large composite deck with built-in seating, stairs, and lighting can easily top $30,000. The material you choose is the single biggest cost lever: pressure-treated lumber is the cheapest option at **$15 to $25 per square foot installed**, while composite decking runs **$35 to $65 per square foot** and hardwood like Ipe can exceed **$80 per square foot**. If you are trying to budget for this project before calling a single contractor, you are in the right place. Before you hire anyone, read our guide on [how to find a good contractor](/blog/how-to-find-good-contractor/) since deck-building scams and unpermitted work are common complaints among homeowners. And once your deck is built, do not skip seasonal upkeep, our [winterizing guide](/blog/winterize-outdoor-faucets/) covers other outdoor prep tasks worth doing at the same time.

## Why Deck Costs Vary So Much

A "deck" can mean a 150-square-foot ground-level platform or a 600-square-foot multi-level structure with a pergola, built-in grill station, and steps wrapping around two sides. That range explains why deck quotes can shock you. The five biggest cost drivers are: size, material choice, height and complexity (ground-level decks are much cheaper than elevated decks requiring significant framing), local permitting fees, and whether you need your contractor to haul away an old structure first.

When you get quotes, the number per square foot is useful for comparing contractors, but the per-square-foot rate almost always drops as deck size increases. A contractor quoting $45 per square foot for a 200 sq ft deck will often quote $35 per square foot for a 400 sq ft deck because setup costs are spread over more square footage.

## Deck Cost by Material

This is the most important decision you will make, and it affects both upfront cost and long-term maintenance.

### Pressure-Treated Lumber: $15 to $25 per sq ft installed

Pressure-treated pine is the classic American deck material and still the most common choice. It is durable, widely available, and easy for contractors to work with. The wood is treated with preservatives to resist rot and insects, which is why it can last 15 to 25 years with proper maintenance.

The catch: pressure-treated decks need to be stained or sealed every one to three years to stay looking good and to extend their life. If you skip maintenance, the wood grays, checks (develops surface cracks), and splinters. Factor in roughly **$200 to $500 every few years** for sealing and staining — our [first-year homeowner guide](/blog/first-year-homeowner-guide/) includes a seasonal maintenance checklist that covers deck upkeep alongside other essential tasks.

Total cost for a 300 sq ft deck: **$4,500 to $7,500**

### Composite Decking: $35 to $65 per sq ft installed

Composite decking is made from a blend of wood fiber and recycled plastic. It does not splinter, does not need annual staining, and resists insects and rot. The best composite brands (Trex, TimberTech, Azek) carry warranties of 25 to 30 years, and the color stays consistent far longer than stained wood.

The tradeoff is a higher upfront cost and the feel, which some homeowners find less natural than real wood. Composite also gets hotter than wood in direct sun, which matters if you live in Texas, Arizona, or other hot-climate states. Some premium composite products now have capped polymer cores that stay cooler and resist fading better.

Total cost for a 300 sq ft deck: **$10,500 to $19,500**

### PVC Decking: $40 to $70 per sq ft installed

PVC decking is all plastic, no wood fiber. It is the most moisture-resistant option and does not support mold or mildew growth, making it popular in humid climates like Florida, the Carolinas, and the Gulf Coast. It is also the most expensive standard decking material.

Total cost for a 300 sq ft deck: **$12,000 to $21,000**

### Cedar or Redwood: $25 to $40 per sq ft installed

Cedar and redwood are naturally rot and insect resistant, so they need less chemical treatment. They look beautiful, have a natural warmth that composite cannot fully replicate, and take stain exceptionally well. The downside is cost (higher than pressure-treated) and regional availability. Redwood is common in California but expensive elsewhere. Cedar is more widely available across the US.

Total cost for a 300 sq ft deck: **$7,500 to $12,000**

### Tropical Hardwood (Ipe, Cumaru): $60 to $90 per sq ft installed

Ipe (pronounced ee-pay) and similar tropical hardwoods are incredibly dense, hard, and durable. A well-maintained Ipe deck can last 40 to 75 years. They are used on commercial boardwalks for a reason. But they are expensive, require specialized fasteners, and need annual oiling to prevent surface cracking. They are also controversial from a sustainability standpoint, though FSC-certified options exist.

Total cost for a 300 sq ft deck: **$18,000 to $27,000**

These ranges assume a simple single-level rectangular deck. Stairs, curves, multiple levels, built-in seating, railings, and pergolas all add cost.

## What Adds the Most to a Deck Quote

### Stairs: $1,000 to $3,000 per staircase

Stairs are more labor-intensive than straight decking and require careful structural work to meet building codes. A single straight staircase runs $1,000 to $1,500. An L-shaped or wide designer staircase can run $2,500 to $3,000 or more.

### Railings: $150 to $400 per linear foot

Your local building code will require railings if your deck is more than 30 inches above grade (the [International Residential Code](https://www.iccsafe.org) is the baseline, but most states and counties adopt it). Pressure-treated wood railings are cheapest. Cable railings (very popular now for the open, modern look) run $200 to $350 per linear foot installed. Aluminum balusters split the difference at $150 to $250 per linear foot.

### Pergola or Shade Structure: $3,000 to $12,000

A freestanding pergola attached to the deck adds significant cost but dramatically increases usability in summer. Pressure-treated wood pergolas start around $3,000. Cedar or vinyl pergolas run $5,000 to $8,000. A fully covered structure with a metal roof can reach $10,000 to $15,000.

### Built-in Seating and Planters: $500 to $2,500

Built-in benches and planters are a popular upgrade that looks cleaner than patio furniture and is often cheaper than buying quality outdoor furniture separately.

### Lighting: $500 to $2,500

Deck lighting is both functional and beautiful. Post cap lights, riser lights on stairs, and under-rail lighting are common options. Most deck contractors can rough in wiring; you may need a licensed electrician to make the final connection, which the [National Electrical Code (via NFPA)](https://www.nfpa.org/codes-and-standards/nfpa-70-standard-for-electrical-installations/about-the-code) requires for permanent outdoor electrical work.

### Removing an Old Deck: $500 to $2,500

If you are replacing an existing deck, disposal adds cost. A small old deck costs $500 to $1,000 to demo and haul away. A large old deck, especially one with a lot of concrete footings, can run $1,500 to $2,500 extra.

## Deck Permits and Inspection Costs: $150 to $1,000

Almost every US municipality requires a building permit for a new deck. The permit ensures the framing is structurally sound, the footings are deep enough for local frost depths, and the railings meet code height requirements (typically 36 to 42 inches, depending on deck height). Permit fees vary widely: a rural county might charge $100 to $150, while a major city can charge $500 to $1,000.

A contractor who suggests skipping the permit is a contractor you should not hire. An unpermitted deck creates problems when you sell: it must be disclosed, buyers can require it to be torn down, and [homeowners insurance](/blog/homeowners-insurance-claims/) may deny claims related to an unpermitted structure. The [CPSC](https://www.cpsc.gov) has noted that deck collapses cause hundreds of injuries annually, most involving older uncertified structures.

## DIY vs. Hiring a Contractor

A simple ground-level deck is one of the more approachable DIY projects for someone with basic carpentry skills and a free weekend or two. Hiring out the labor on a 300 sq ft pressure-treated deck saves roughly **$2,000 to $5,000** if you do it yourself.

**When DIY makes sense:**
- Ground-level deck (less than 30 inches high, may not require a permit in some areas)
- Simple rectangular design with no stairs
- You are comfortable with a circular saw, drill, and post-hole digger
- You have a helper for setting posts and hanging beams

**When to hire a pro:**
- Elevated deck (structural failure risk is real)
- Deck attached to the house (ledger board attachment is the most failure-prone part of any deck)
- Complex design with stairs, multiple levels, or curves
- You want a warranty and someone to pull the permit
- Your soil has drainage or slope issues that require engineered footings

If you go the DIY route, the [American Wood Protection Association (AWPA)](https://www.awpa.com) publishes standards for treated wood that help you choose the right lumber grade for ground contact versus above-ground use. Using the wrong grade is a common DIY mistake that shortens deck life dramatically.

## How Long Does a Deck Last?

| Material | Expected Lifespan | Maintenance Required |
|---|---|---|
| Pressure-treated pine | 15 to 25 years | Seal or stain every 1 to 3 years |
| Cedar / Redwood | 20 to 30 years | Seal every 2 to 3 years |
| Composite (capped) | 25 to 30 years | Clean annually, no staining |
| PVC | 30+ years | Clean annually |
| Tropical hardwood (Ipe) | 40 to 75 years | Oil annually |

The lifespan numbers above assume the deck is properly built with the right fasteners and adequate drainage underneath. A pressure-treated deck with standing water under it will not last 20 years. Proper site preparation, including gravel or ground cloth under the deck to prevent moisture buildup, extends life significantly.

## What Deck Building Does to Your Home's Value

Decks are one of the better return-on-investment home improvements. According to [Remodeling Magazine's Cost vs. Value Report](https://www.remodelingmagazine.com), a wood deck addition nationally returns about **65 to 75 percent** of its cost at resale, while composite decks return slightly less at **60 to 65 percent**. In markets where outdoor living is a high priority (Pacific Northwest, Southeast, mid-Atlantic), returns are typically higher.

More practically: a deck expands your usable living space, which matters to buyers even if the formal appraisal value does not fully capture it. Homes with well-maintained decks sell faster in most markets, especially when paired with complementary outdoor improvements like [privacy fencing](/blog/fence-installation-cost/).

## How to Get the Best Price Without Getting Burned

**Get [three quotes minimum](/blog/how-to-find-good-contractor/).** Deck contractor pricing varies by 30 to 50 percent for the same project. Always compare quotes with the same square footage, material spec, and features so you are comparing apples to apples.

**Ask to see completed projects.** Deck framing quality varies enormously. Ask to visit or see photos of a similar deck the contractor built in the last 12 months, not just the finished surface but the framing and post-connection details.

**Check licensing and insurance.** Most states require a contractor's license for structural work. Verify through your state licensing board. Require a certificate of general liability insurance and workers' compensation before work starts.

**Verify they pull the permit.** The permit should be in the contractor's name or your name, not skipped. If the contractor asks you to pull the permit yourself "to save money," that is often a red flag that they are not licensed or want to avoid inspection.

**Do not pay more than 30 percent upfront.** A standard payment schedule is one-third at signing, one-third at material delivery, one-third at completion. Contractors who demand 50 percent or more upfront before any work has started are a higher-risk hire.

**Get everything in writing.** The contract should specify materials by brand and grade, square footage, completion timeline, and what happens if materials are delayed. Handshake deals on a $10,000 project invite disputes. The [National Association of Home Builders](https://www.nahb.org) has guidance on what a proper contractor agreement should include.

## Official Resources

These authoritative sources can help you plan, permit, and build a safer deck.

- **[CPSC - Deck Safety](https://www.cpsc.gov/Safety-Education/Safety-Guides/Sports-Fitness-and-Recreation/Outdoor-Recreation/Decks):** The Consumer Product Safety Commission's guide on deck safety, including common structural failure points and what to inspect on existing decks.
- **[ICC - International Residential Code](https://www.iccsafe.org):** The model building code that most US municipalities adopt. Your local code may differ, but IRC is the baseline for deck height, railing requirements, and footing depth.
- **[NFPA 70 - National Electrical Code](https://www.nfpa.org/codes-and-standards/nfpa-70-standard-for-electrical-installations/about-the-code):** Governs outdoor electrical work for deck lighting and outlets.
- **[NAHB - Find a Builder](https://www.nahb.org/find-members):** Directory of National Association of Home Builders members, including deck contractors.
- **[AWPA - Wood Treatment Standards](https://www.awpa.com):** American Wood Protection Association standards for treated lumber grades, essential if you are doing DIY or want to verify contractor materials.
- **[EPA - Wood Preservatives](https://www.epa.gov/ingredients-used-pesticide-products/treated-wood):** EPA guidance on pressure-treated wood safety, including which preservatives are approved for residential use and how to handle treated lumber safely.
- **[HUD - Home Improvement Financing](https://www.hud.gov/program_offices/housing/sfh/title):** FHA Title I loans can be used for deck additions as permanent home improvements. Up to $25,000 available without tapping home equity.
