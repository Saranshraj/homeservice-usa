A professional HVAC tune-up costs **$80 to $150 per unit** for a one-time visit, or **$150 to $500 per year** if you sign up for a maintenance contract covering both your furnace and air conditioner. Most homeowners spend around $200 to $250 annually for a two-system preventive maintenance plan. Costs vary by region, system age, and what the technician actually does during the visit.

If your utility bills have been creeping up, your system is making odd noises, or you simply cannot remember the last time a technician looked at it, this guide is for you. We will walk you through exactly what you are paying for, what fair pricing looks like in your area, and whether a maintenance contract is actually worth the money.

## Why HVAC Tune-Up Costs Vary So Much

The gap between an $80 and a $150 tune-up is not random. Several factors push the price up or down.

**System type.** Standard split systems (separate furnace and AC) are usually the cheapest to service individually. Heat pumps do double duty as both heater and air conditioner, so a full heat pump tune-up touches more components and often costs slightly more. Mini-split systems with multiple indoor heads cost more to inspect per visit because the technician has to check each air handler.

**Your location.** Labor rates in San Francisco or New York City run 30 to 50 percent higher than in rural Tennessee or Kansas. States with extreme climates, such as Arizona or Minnesota, have more HVAC contractors competing for business during shoulder seasons, which can actually push prices down if you book at the right time.

**Age of your system.** Older systems, especially anything over 15 years old, may require additional labor to clean corroded components or diagnose issues that a newer system would not have. A technician may quote you a tune-up and then add time for cleaning a badly fouled evaporator coil or lubricating dry motor bearings.

**What is actually included.** Some contractors offer a "basic tune-up" for $79 that mostly involves checking refrigerant pressure and replacing the filter. A true preventive maintenance visit should cover 20 to 30 individual inspection points. Ask for a checklist before you book.

## What a Real HVAC Tune-Up Should Include

The [U.S. Department of Energy](https://www.energy.gov/energysaver/maintaining-your-air-conditioner) and ENERGY STAR recommend annual professional maintenance for both heating and cooling systems. Here is what a thorough tune-up covers for each system type.

### Air Conditioner Tune-Up Checklist (Spring)

- Clean or replace the air filter
- Inspect and clean the evaporator coil (indoor unit)
- Inspect and clean the condenser coil (outdoor unit)
- Check refrigerant level; note whether a recharge is needed (refrigerant itself is extra)
- Inspect refrigerant lines for leaks or insulation damage
- Test capacitors and contactors
- Lubricate fan motor bearings
- Clear the condensate drain line
- Inspect and tighten all electrical connections
- Test thermostat calibration
- Measure airflow and static pressure
- Check ductwork connections at the air handler

### Furnace Tune-Up Checklist (Fall)

- Inspect and clean the heat exchanger (critical for carbon monoxide safety)
- Clean or replace the air filter
- Test the igniter and flame sensor
- Inspect the burners and adjust as needed
- Check the flue pipe and venting for blockages or leaks
- Test the limit switch and safety controls
- Inspect the blower motor and belt (if belt-driven)
- Lubricate moving parts
- Test the thermostat and verify accurate temperature readings
- Measure temperature rise across the heat exchanger
- Inspect and tighten electrical connections

A cracked heat exchanger can allow carbon monoxide to enter your living space. This is why the [Consumer Product Safety Commission (CPSC)](https://www.cpsc.gov/Safety-Education/Safety-Guides/Home/Carbon-Monoxide-Information-Center) recommends annual furnace inspections, not just filter changes. Catching a crack early is far less expensive than dealing with a CO emergency or a full heat exchanger replacement.

## Annual Maintenance Contracts: Are They Worth It?

Many HVAC companies offer service contracts, sometimes called "comfort plans" or "preventive maintenance agreements." Here is how the math typically works.

A two-system contract might cost $200 to $300 per year and include:
- One AC tune-up (spring)
- One furnace tune-up (fall)
- Priority scheduling for service calls
- A discount (10 to 20 percent) on parts and labor for repairs
- No overtime charges for after-hours emergency calls

If you paid for those two tune-ups individually, you would spend $160 to $300 out of pocket anyway. That makes many contracts a wash or a slight savings on the tune-ups alone. The real value is in the priority scheduling and repair discounts if something breaks.

**When a contract makes sense:**
- Your system is 8 to 15 years old and starting to need occasional repairs
- You own a larger home with multiple systems
- You have a heat pump (it needs tune-ups twice a year because it runs year-round)
- You have trouble remembering annual maintenance and want automatic scheduling

**When a contract may not be worth it:**
- Your system is brand new (still under manufacturer warranty, maintenance may be covered)
- You are in a rental or planning to sell within a year
- You have a simple, well-maintained system and a trusted technician you can call directly

## What You Should NOT Be Charged For in a Tune-Up

This is important. Some HVAC companies use low-priced tune-up offers to get inside your home and then upsell you on services you may not need. Know the difference between legitimate recommendations and questionable add-ons.

**Legitimate upcharges:**
- Refrigerant recharge: If your AC is genuinely low on refrigerant (checked with a gauge), a recharge is real and necessary. Expect $150 to $300+ depending on refrigerant type. R-22 (older systems) is very expensive due to the EPA phaseout; see [EPA refrigerant management rules](https://www.epa.gov/section608).
- New air filter: Fine if you forgot to buy one, but buy your own ahead of time and save $10 to $30.
- UV air purifier or air quality add-ons: These can be legitimate, but do not let a technician pressure you during a tune-up visit. Research separately.

**Red flags:**
- A technician who finds a major problem (cracked heat exchanger, bad capacitor) on every single visit
- Pressure to replace the entire system during a tune-up
- "Special pricing" that expires the moment the technician leaves your home
- Refusing to show you the actual refrigerant pressure readings

Always ask to see the gauge readings and get any recommended repair in writing before authorizing work beyond the tune-up itself.

## DIY HVAC Maintenance: What You Can Do Yourself

You can handle several maintenance tasks on your own between professional visits. This does not replace a professional tune-up, but it extends the life of your equipment and keeps it running efficiently.

**Monthly:**
- Check and replace the air filter (every 1 to 3 months depending on filter type and household factors like pets)

**Spring (before AC season):**
- Clear debris (leaves, grass clippings) from around the outdoor condenser unit; keep 2 feet of clearance on all sides
- Straighten bent condenser fins with a fin comb (under $10 at hardware stores)
- Check that the condensate drain line drains freely; flush with diluted bleach if needed

**Fall (before heating season):**
- Test your thermostat by switching to heat and checking that the furnace ignites
- Check the furnace filter
- Inspect the flue pipe for obvious damage or blockages
- Test your carbon monoxide detectors; replace batteries if needed

The [ENERGY STAR program](https://www.energystar.gov/products/heating_cooling) notes that a well-maintained HVAC system uses 15 to 20 percent less energy than a neglected one, which translates to real savings on your utility bills.

## How HVAC Maintenance Affects Your Utility Bills

The Department of Energy estimates that heating and cooling account for nearly **half of the average US home's energy use**. A dirty or poorly maintained system has to work harder to reach your target temperature, and that extra work shows up on your electricity or gas bill.

A clogged air filter alone can reduce system airflow by 15 to 25 percent, forcing the blower motor to work harder and pushing up energy consumption. A corroded or dirty evaporator coil reduces the system's ability to transfer heat, requiring longer run times to cool your home. These are not abstract concerns; they are the kinds of things a $100 tune-up catches and fixes.

Over a typical cooling season, a well-maintained central air conditioner versus a neglected one can represent $50 to $150 in energy savings. Multiply that over 10 to 15 years, and the math for regular maintenance is clear.

## When to Skip the Tune-Up and Just Replace the System

There is a point where continuing to maintain an old system no longer makes financial sense. The general rule of thumb is the **5,000 rule**: multiply the age of the system (in years) by the repair cost estimate. If that number exceeds $5,000, replacement is usually the better investment.

Other signs it is time to replace rather than tune up:
- The system is 15 to 20 years old (average lifespan per [DOE estimates](https://www.energy.gov/energysaver/heat-pump-systems))
- Repair estimates exceed 50 percent of the cost of a new system
- You are replacing major components (compressor, heat exchanger) repeatedly
- The system uses R-22 refrigerant, which is no longer manufactured

A new high-efficiency system may qualify for federal tax credits under the Inflation Reduction Act. See [IRS Form 5695](https://www.irs.gov/forms-pubs/about-form-5695) for residential energy credit details; as of 2026, credits of up to 30 percent (capped at $600 for central AC and $600 for furnaces meeting efficiency thresholds) are available for qualifying equipment.

## How to Find a Trustworthy HVAC Contractor

Before you hand over your home to any technician, do a few quick checks:

1. **Verify HVAC contractor licensing.** Most states require HVAC technicians to be licensed. Check your state licensing board's website. For example, in Texas, check the [Texas Department of Licensing and Regulation](https://www.tdlr.texas.gov/); in California, check the [Contractors State License Board](https://www.cslb.ca.gov/).

2. **Ask about EPA 608 certification.** Any technician who handles refrigerant is federally required to be certified under [EPA Section 608](https://www.epa.gov/section608/section-608-technician-certification). A reputable company will have certified technicians on staff.

3. **Get at least two quotes.** For a tune-up, call two or three local companies and compare what is included in the service, not just the price.

4. **Check reviews, but critically.** Look for patterns: companies with dozens of reviews mentioning high-pressure upselling are a red flag. The [Better Business Bureau](https://www.bbb.org/) is also worth a quick check.

5. **Ask about the technician's experience.** A technician with NATE (North American Technician Excellence) certification has passed third-party competency exams. The [NATE website](https://natex.org/) has a contractor search tool.
