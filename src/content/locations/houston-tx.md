Houston homeowners deal with a specific combination of home-service pressures that most of the country never has to think about: brutal summer heat and humidity that runs air conditioners nearly year-round, heavy seasonal rainfall and flood risk, and, since the February 2021 freeze, a real possibility of hard winter freezes that Gulf Coast homes were never built to handle. This guide connects our national cost data to what actually matters if you own a home in the Houston metro.

## Why Houston Is Different

Houston averages more than 100 days a year above 90°F, and summer humidity routinely pushes the heat index well past 100. That means air conditioning is not a comfort upgrade here, it is core infrastructure, and HVAC systems in Houston run far more hours per year than the national average, which shortens their working lifespan and increases repair frequency. See our [Houston HVAC guide](/locations/houston-tx/hvac/) for what this means for cost and timing.

Houston also sits in one of the highest flood-risk metro areas in the country. Hurricane Harvey in 2017 flooded more than 150,000 homes across the region, and heavy rain events remain a regular seasonal risk from June through November. This affects plumbing systems (sewer backup during flooding is common), foundation stability in clay-soil neighborhoods, and insurance considerations. See our [Houston plumbing guide](/locations/houston-tx/plumbing/).

The February 2021 winter storm, which left millions of Texans without power and caused widespread pipe bursts, was a wake-up call for a region that historically saw freezing temperatures only a few days a year. Homes built for heat, not cold, often lack adequate pipe insulation. Our national guide on [winterizing outdoor faucets](/blog/winterize-outdoor-faucets/) is especially relevant for Houston homeowners who may not think of winterizing as a priority.

Houston's housing stock skews newer than most major US metros due to decades of sprawling suburban growth, with a large share of homes built from the 1970s through the 2010s. That means less legacy wiring and plumbing than older Northeast or Midwest cities, but also means many homes are now entering the 15 to 25 year window where major systems (roof, HVAC, water heater) start needing replacement.

## Texas Contractor Licensing

Texas does not have a statewide general contractor license, but specific trades do require licensing at the state level. HVAC contractors must be licensed through the [Texas Department of Licensing and Regulation (TDLR)](https://www.tdlr.texas.gov/ac/ac.htm). Electricians are licensed through TDLR as well. Plumbers are licensed through the [Texas State Board of Plumbing Examiners](https://www.tsbpe.texas.gov). Roofing contractors in Texas are not required to hold a state license, which makes verifying insurance and reputation especially important, our [contractor vetting guide](/blog/how-to-find-good-contractor/) covers exactly what to check.
